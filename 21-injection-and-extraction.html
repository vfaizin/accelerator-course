In one page

The wall. A synchrotron works over a limited range. Its iron magnets lose field quality at low field, where remanence (the field the iron keeps when the current is off) and hysteresis (the iron’s memory of how it was magnetised before, F4, section 15) are a large fraction of the field. Its beam is most fragile at low energy, where the beam’s own charge spreads the tunes (the numbers of betatron oscillations per turn) of its particles. Its RF system can follow only a limited swing in revolution frequency. So high energy is reached by a chain: each ring takes the beam over a factor of about 5 to 20 in momentum and passes it on. Every transfer needs a quick, clean and safe way in and out.

The idea. A thin septum separates a field region for the incoming or outgoing beam from a field-free region for the circulating one. A kicker, a magnet that switches on and off in nanoseconds to microseconds, puts the beam on or off the closed orbit within one turn. Placing kicker and septum a quarter of a betatron oscillation apart makes the kick smallest. To stack injections in the same phase space, Liouville’s theorem must be got round: stripping H⁻ ions in a foil creates new protons where old ones already are. To extract slowly, over seconds, the beam is pushed onto a third-integer resonance and peeled off across a septum of wires about 0.1 mm thick.

The equations to remember.

  • Rigidity: \(B\rho = p/q\); \(B\rho\,[\mathrm{T\,m}] = 3.3356\,p\,[\mathrm{GeV}/c]\) for a singly charged particle (\(10^9/c = 3.3356\), worked in section 1).
  • Kick to put a beam that leaves the septum parallel to the orbit at offset \(x_s\) (m) onto the orbit, with phase advance \(\pi/2\): \(\theta_k = x_s/\sqrt{\beta_{x,s}\beta_{x,k}}\) (rad), with \(\beta_{x,s}\) and \(\beta_{x,k}\) (m) the betatron functions at the septum and the kicker.
  • Kicker field and inductance, for a current \(I\) (A) round a gap of height \(g\), width \(w\) and length \(l\) (m): \(B = \mu_0 I/g\), \(L = \mu_0 w l/g\); a travelling-wave kicker of \(n\) cells fills in \(n\sqrt{L_c C_c}\) with impedance \(\sqrt{L_c/C_c}\).
  • Emittance after an injection error of \(a\) beam sizes, once filamented: \(\varepsilon_f = \varepsilon\,(1 + a^2/2)\); after a mismatch, \(\varepsilon_f = \varepsilon H\) with \(H = \tfrac12(\beta_{x,1}\gamma_{x,2} - 2\alpha_{x,1}\alpha_{x,2} + \gamma_{x,1}\beta_{x,2})\).
  • Third-integer extraction: the stable triangle has area \(3\sqrt3\,h^2\) with \(h = 4\pi\,\Delta Q/S\) (in this chapter \(h\) is the triangle’s inscribed radius, not the harmonic number); the spill is chopped by ripple when \(\omega\,\delta Q_{\mathrm{ripple}} > \dot Q\).

Numbers worth knowing. CERN today: Linac4 (H⁻, 160 MeV), PS Booster (2 GeV), PS (26 GeV/c), SPS (450 GeV/c), LHC (6.8 TeV). The LHC dipoles sit at 0.535 T at injection and 8.33 T at 7 TeV. The LHC’s injection kickers rise in 0.9 µs; its dump kickers in 3 µs, which is the length of the particle-free abort gap in an 88.9 µs turn. At design, each LHC beam stores 362 MJ. A 200 µg/cm² carbon foil strips 160 MeV H⁻ with about 99.8 per cent efficiency. The SPS slow-extraction septum uses wires 60 to 100 µm thick and loses a few per cent of the beam on them.

Pre-requisite (continued): Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics, the charged-particle Hamiltonian

Pre-requisite. This section gives you the Poisson bracket, a shorthand for the rate at which any quantity changes under a Hamiltonian, with the two sign conventions found in the books, and a way to read a Hamiltonian of one degree of freedom as a contour map: its level curves are the trajectories, its flat points are the fixed points, and the level curve through a saddle is the separatrix. Section 7 writes the continuity equation of a stripped beam with the bracket; section 11 reads the level curves of the Kobayashi Hamiltonian to find the stable triangle of slow extraction. Taught from Tong, Classical Mechanics, sections 10.1 (Hamilton’s equations as a flow in phase space) and 10.3 (Poisson brackets); Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §42 (Poisson brackets); S. Y. Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, section VII.1 C (the third-order resonance as an autonomous Hamiltonian whose contours are the trajectories); and the Handbook of Accelerator Physics and Engineering, section 4.10 (third-order resonant extraction).

Reminder: chapter 1 built the Hamiltonian \(H(x, p)\) and Hamilton’s equations \(\dot x = \partial H/\partial p\), \(\dot p = -\partial H/\partial x\), and chapter 7 read them as a flow through the phase plane. Chapter 18 turned a turn-by-turn map into a Hamiltonian with the turn number as the clock and classified its fixed points by the shape of \(H\), and its section 7 found Kobayashi’s triangle in action and angle. This section adds a notation, the Poisson bracket, and a way of seeing, the contour map, and with them shows that the triangle’s sides are exactly straight.

The rate of change of any quantity. Let \(f(x, p, t)\) be anything you can work out from a particle’s state: its action, its distance from a septum, or the density of the beam around it. As the particle moves, \(x\), \(p\) and \(t\) all change at once, and the chain rule for a function of several variables (F1, section 9) adds the three contributions:

\[ \frac{df}{dt} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\dot x + \frac{\partial f}{\partial p}\dot p = \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial p}\frac{\partial H}{\partial x} . \]

The last two terms appear so often that they get a name. For any two functions \(f\) and \(g\) on phase space, the Poisson bracket is

\[ \lbrace f, g\rbrace = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial p}\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}, \]

summed over the pairs \((x, p_x)\), \((y, p_y)\), \((z, p_z)\) when there are several. With it,

\[ \frac{df}{dt} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \lbrace f, H\rbrace . \]

Units: with \(H\) in J, \(x\) in m and \(p\) in kg m/s, the bracket \(\lbrace f, H\rbrace\) has the units of \(f\) per second, as a rate should.

Four facts follow at once from the definition.

  • The bracket is antisymmetric, \(\lbrace f, g\rbrace = -\lbrace g, f\rbrace\), so \(\lbrace H, H\rbrace = 0\). A Hamiltonian with no explicit time dependence is conserved.
  • \(\lbrace x, p\rbrace = 1\), and \(\lbrace x, H\rbrace = \partial H/\partial p\), \(\lbrace p, H\rbrace = -\partial H/\partial x\): Hamilton’s equations are \(\dot x = \lbrace x, H\rbrace\) and \(\dot p = \lbrace p, H\rbrace\).
  • A quantity with no explicit time dependence is a constant of the motion exactly when its bracket with \(H\) vanishes.
  • If \(f\) depends on the state only through \(H\), \(f = F(H)\), then \(\lbrace F(H), H\rbrace = F'(H)\lbrace H, H\rbrace = 0\). Any function of the Hamiltonian is constant along the motion.

Two sign conventions. Tong defines the bracket as above, with the derivative of the first function by position first. Landau and Lifshitz (§42) write the rate of change as \(\partial f/\partial t + [H, f]\), with \([H, f] = \partial H/\partial p\,\partial f/\partial x - \partial H/\partial x\,\partial f/\partial p\). Term by term this is Tong’s \(\lbrace f, H\rbrace\): the same number, written with the functions in the other order. So Landau’s bracket of \((f, g)\) is minus Tong’s. The physics never changes; only the order of the two functions does. Whenever a book writes a bracket, check which derivative comes first. This chapter follows Tong, so the rate is always \(\partial f/\partial t + \lbrace f, H\rbrace\), which is how section 7 writes it (there the Hamiltonian is called \(\mathcal{H}\)).

Other clocks, other pairs. Nothing above needs the clock to be the time \(t\) or the pair to be \((x, p)\). Chapter 1 used the path length \(s\) as the clock; a ring uses the turn number. Section 11 uses the count of three-turn steps, \(n_3\), as the clock and the normalised coordinates \((X, P)\) of section 3 as the pair, so there \(\lbrace f, g\rbrace = \partial f/\partial X\,\partial g/\partial P - \partial f/\partial P\,\partial g/\partial X\) and \(dX/dn_3 = \partial\mathcal{H}_K/\partial P\), \(dP/dn_3 = -\partial\mathcal{H}_K/\partial X\).

Level curves are trajectories. Take one degree of freedom and a Hamiltonian with no explicit time dependence. Then \(H\) is constant along the motion, so each trajectory lies on one curve \(H(X, P) = \text{constant}\). The velocity through the phase plane, \((\partial H/\partial P, -\partial H/\partial X)\), has zero dot product (F2, section 2) with the gradient \((\partial H/\partial X, \partial H/\partial P)\), which points straight uphill, across the level curves (F2, section 6): so the velocity points along the level curve, and its size equals the size of the gradient. So a contour map of \(H\) shows the whole motion without solving anything.

  • Where both derivatives vanish the particle does not move: a fixed point. At a minimum or maximum of \(H\) the nearby level curves are closed loops, and the point is stable. At a saddle, level curves cross, and the point is unstable.
  • The speed along a curve is \(|\nabla H|\). It falls to zero at a saddle, so a particle on a curve through a saddle takes for ever to reach it: the pendulum’s separatrix is the example.
  • The level curve through a saddle is a separatrix. Since \(H\) cannot change, no particle crosses it. Inside, the curves close and the motion is bounded; outside, they may run off to large amplitude.
  • The direction is fixed by the signs. For \(H = \tfrac12(X^2 + P^2)\), at \((1, 0)\) the velocity is \((0, -1)\): the motion is clockwise in a plane with \(X\) to the right and \(P\) up, as the rotation matrix of section 3 says.

At work: the Kobayashi Hamiltonian. Section 11 quotes

\[ \mathcal{H}_K = \frac{\kappa}{2}\left(X^2 + P^2\right) + \frac{S}{4}\left(3XP^2 - X^3\right), \]

with \(\kappa = 6\pi\,\Delta Q\) (dimensionless, the extra phase advance per three turns away from the resonance) and \(S\) (m\(^{-1/2}\)) the normalised sextupole strength; \(X\) and \(P\) are in m\(^{1/2}\), and so \(\mathcal{H}_K\) is in m. In polar form, \(X = r\cos\phi\), \(P = r\sin\phi\), the cubic is \(-r^3\cos3\phi\), so

\[ \mathcal{H}_K = \frac{\kappa}{2}r^2 - \frac{S}{4}r^3\cos3\phi : \]

a slow rotation plus a term that repeats three times round the circle.

Hamilton’s equations. Differentiating,

\[ \frac{dX}{dn_3} = \frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_K}{\partial P} = \kappa P + \tfrac32 SXP, \qquad \frac{dP}{dn_3} = -\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_K}{\partial X} = -\kappa X + \tfrac34 S\left(X^2 - P^2\right), \]

which are the changes per three turns that section 11 takes from Bryant.

Fixed points. Both derivatives vanish where \(P(\kappa + \tfrac32 SX) = 0\) and \(\kappa X - \tfrac34 S(X^2 - P^2) = 0\). On the line \(P = 0\) the second gives \(X = 0\), the centre, or \(X = 4\kappa/(3S) = 2h\) with

\[ h = \frac{2\kappa}{3S} . \]

The threefold symmetry puts two more at the same distance, \(120°\) either side. At the centre \(\mathcal{H}_K \approx \kappa r^2/2\), a minimum for \(\kappa > 0\): stable. At \((2h, 0)\) the second derivatives are \(\partial^2\mathcal{H}_K/\partial X^2 = \kappa - \tfrac32 SX = -\kappa\) and \(\partial^2\mathcal{H}_K/\partial P^2 = \kappa + \tfrac32 SX = 3\kappa\), with the mixed one zero: one curvature down, one up, a saddle.

The separatrix. The value at the saddles is \(\mathcal{H}_K(2h, 0) = 2\kappa h^2 - 2Sh^3 = Sh^3\), using \(\kappa = \tfrac32 Sh\). Now evaluate \(\mathcal{H}_K\) along the vertical line \(X = -h\):

\[ \mathcal{H}_K(-h, P) = \frac{\kappa}{2}h^2 + \frac{S}{4}h^3 + P^2\left(\frac{\kappa}{2} - \frac{3Sh}{4}\right) = \frac{3Sh^3}{4} + \frac{Sh^3}{4} + 0 = Sh^3 . \]

The coefficient of \(P^2\) vanishes, so the whole line lies on the level \(Sh^3 = (2\kappa/3)^3/S^2\): it is one side of the separatrix, at distance \(h\) from the centre. By the threefold symmetry the other two sides are the same line turned by \(\pm120°\). Three lines at distance \(h\) from a centre, \(120°\) apart, make an equilateral triangle with inscribed radius \(h\) and corners at \(2h\), the saddles found above. Its side is \(2\sqrt3\,h\) and its area \(3\sqrt3\,h^2\), section 11’s acceptance. Inside, the level curves are closed rounded triangles; outside, they are open and run out along the extensions of the sides, the outgoing arms.

The action is not conserved, the Hamiltonian is. With \(J = \tfrac12(X^2 + P^2)\),

\[ \frac{dJ}{dn_3} = \lbrace J, \mathcal{H}_K\rbrace = X\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_K}{\partial P} - P\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_K}{\partial X} = \tfrac34 SP\left(3X^2 - P^2\right) = \tfrac34 Sr^3\sin3\phi . \]

The sextupole pumps the action up and down three times round each orbit. Only when \(S = 0\) is \(J\) itself conserved; with the sextupole, \(\mathcal{H}_K\) takes its place.

Numbers. Take a medical ring with \(\beta_x = 8\) m at a sextupole of \(k_2\ell = 5\) m\(^{-2}\), so \(S = \tfrac12\times8^{3/2}\times5 = 56.6\) m\(^{-1/2}\), and a tune \(\Delta Q = 0.005\) from the third integer. Then \(h = 4\pi\times0.005/56.6 = 1.11\times10^{-3}\) m\(^{1/2}\). At the sextupole, \(x = \sqrt{\beta_x}X\): the inscribed circle has a radius of 3.14 mm in \(x\) and the corners lie at 6.28 mm. The triangle’s area is \(3\sqrt3h^2 = 6.41\times10^{-6}\) m (the area in \((X, P)\) equals the area in \((x, x')\), since the change of variables has determinant 1). When the tune moves slowly, a particle keeps the area its orbit encloses, \(2\pi J\) (the adiabatic invariant), and becomes unstable when the triangle’s area falls to it. A particle with \(J = 1\) µm is lost from the triangle at \(\Delta Q = 0.0050\); one with \(J = 4.5\) µm, at \(\Delta Q = 0.0105\).

Exercise PB1. Compute \(\lbrace x, p\rbrace\) in Tong’s convention and in Landau’s. Write chapter 7’s Liouville equation, \(\partial f/\partial t + \lbrace f, H\rbrace = 0\), in Landau’s notation.

Answer

Tong: \(\lbrace x, p\rbrace = (\partial x/\partial x)(\partial p/\partial p) - (\partial x/\partial p)(\partial p/\partial x) = 1\). Landau’s bracket of \((x, p)\) is \((\partial x/\partial p)(\partial p/\partial x) - (\partial x/\partial x)(\partial p/\partial p) = -1\). Landau writes the rate of change of \(f\) as \(\partial f/\partial t + [H, f]\), so the Liouville equation reads \(\partial f/\partial t + [H, f] = 0\). The two equations are the same equation.

Exercise PB2. For the pure rotation \(\mathcal{H} = \kappa J\), with \(J = \tfrac12(X^2 + P^2)\), find \(dJ/dn_3\) and \(dX/dn_3\). What is the motion?

Answer

\(\lbrace J, \kappa J\rbrace = 0\): the action is constant. \(dX/dn_3 = \partial\mathcal{H}/\partial P = \kappa P\) and \(dP/dn_3 = -\kappa X\): a clockwise rotation at \(\kappa\) radians per three-turn step, on a circle of radius \(\sqrt{2J}\). This is section 3’s rotation, seen three turns at a time, after the whole revolutions (the \(2\pi p\) of section 11, where \(p\) is an integer, not a momentum) have been dropped.

Exercise PB3. In the medical ring above, at what \(\Delta Q\) does a particle with \(J = 1\) µm become unstable? What are then the inscribed radius and corner distance in \(x\) at the sextupole?

Answer

Instability when \(3\sqrt3h^2 = 2\pi J\), so \(h = \sqrt{2\pi\times10^{-6}/5.196} = 1.10\times10^{-3}\) m\(^{1/2}\) and \(\Delta Q = hS/(4\pi) = 1.10\times10^{-3}\times56.6/12.57 = 0.0050\). In \(x\), with \(\sqrt{\beta_x} = 2.83\) m\(^{1/2}\): inscribed radius 3.11 mm, corners at 6.22 mm.

Exercise PB4. Show that the centre of the Kobayashi Hamiltonian becomes a maximum, not a minimum, when \(\Delta Q < 0\). Is the motion near the centre still stable?

Answer

Near the centre \(\mathcal{H}_K \approx \kappa r^2/2\), which for \(\kappa < 0\) is a maximum. The level curves near a maximum are still closed loops, so the motion is still stable; only its sense reverses (anticlockwise), because \(dX/dn_3 \approx \kappa P\) has changed sign. The triangle has \(h = 2\kappa/(3S) < 0\): it is the same triangle turned through \(180°\), which is why the sign of the sextupole or of \(\Delta Q\) only decides which way the triangle points.

Pre-requisite (continued): Phase space and Liouville’s theorem

Pre-requisite. Chapter 7 showed that a Hamiltonian flow keeps phase-space area and density. This section turns that into two working rules for filling a ring. First, no handling of a beam by fields can raise its peak phase-space density, so every stacking scheme that uses fields alone needs fresh phase space for each new beam. Second, keeping account of area predicts what happens when an empty RF bucket is pushed through a coasting beam: the whole beam is moved the other way, by the bucket’s area divided by \(2\pi\) (phase displacement). Sections 6 and 8 use both. How charge exchange escapes the first rule is the subject of the continuity equation with a source, before section 7. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §46 (Liouville’s theorem); Tong, Classical Mechanics, section 10.2; and S. Y. Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 3, section V.5 (beam stacking and phase-displacement acceleration).

Density can be moved, not raised. Liouville’s theorem says that the density \(f\) of particles in phase space is constant along each particle’s path. A bump, a kicker, a septum field and an RF system all act through a Hamiltonian. Each only carries the values of \(f\) from one place to another, like a stirred liquid that cannot be compressed. So the largest value of \(f\) anywhere after any such operation is the largest value before it. Two consequences matter here.

  • Combining beams. Bring a new beam of density \(f_2\) next to a circulating one of density \(f_1\). Where they do not overlap, each keeps its density. They cannot be made to overlap, because the flow carries both with the same velocity field and never brings two phase points together. So the stack’s area is at least the sum of the areas, and its peak density at most the larger of \(f_1\) and \(f_2\).
  • Gaps. Every real join wastes area: the septum’s thickness, the turns where an injected beamlet comes back to the septum, the mismatch of shapes. Filamentation (section 5: particles with slightly different tunes drift apart round their circles in phase space, until the beam’s phases are spread evenly) then smooths the empty spaces into the beam, and the density averaged over small regions falls. This is why section 6 quotes \(\varepsilon_r \gtrsim 1.5N_t\varepsilon_i\) and not \(N_t\varepsilon_i\): ten turns of 1 µm each make a beam of about 15 µm, never less than 10 µm and never one of 1 µm.

Momentum stacking. The same rule holds in the longitudinal plane \((\phi, \delta)\), with \(\phi\) the RF phase (rad) and \(\delta = \Delta p/p\). Each pulse must be placed at a momentum not yet occupied, so a stack of \(N\) pulses of momentum width \(\Delta\delta_1\) spans at least \(N\Delta\delta_1\). A ring with a wide momentum aperture, like the ISR with its \(\pm2\) per cent, can hold many.

Phase displacement. Now the less obvious rule. Let a coasting beam fill all phases, \(0\) to \(2\pi\), in a band of momenta. With no RF, each particle keeps its \(\delta\) and slides along in phase: its trajectory in the \((\phi, \delta)\) plane is a horizontal line. Choose a reference level \(\delta_{\mathrm{ref}}\) far below the beam, where nothing happens, and measure the area under a particle’s line down to it, over one full \(2\pi\) of phase:

\[ A_{\mathrm{below}} = 2\pi\left(\delta - \delta_{\mathrm{ref}}\right). \]

Now move an empty bucket, of area \(A_b\) in the same plane, slowly down from above the beam, through it, and on to below it. Follow the set of phase points that lay under the particle’s line before the bucket arrived. By Liouville’s theorem this set keeps its area \(A_{\mathrm{below}}\) as it is carried along. The bucket’s interior is a different set of phase points: they start above the particle, travel inside the separatrix with the bucket, and no particle can cross the separatrix into it while the motion is slow. After the bucket has passed, the region under the particle’s line contains both the old set and the bucket. So

\[ 2\pi\left(\delta' - \delta_{\mathrm{ref}}\right) = 2\pi\left(\delta - \delta_{\mathrm{ref}}\right) + A_b, \qquad \Delta\delta = \delta' - \delta = \frac{A_b}{2\pi} . \]

Three features stand out. The shift is the same for every particle whatever its momentum, so the whole stack moves up together, keeping its shape. No particle was ever inside a bucket: the beam was accelerated without being captured. And the direction is the opposite of the bucket’s: a bucket moved down pushes the stack up, one moved up pushes it down. If the ring holds several buckets round its circumference, each sweeps its own stretch of \(2\pi\) in RF phase, and the same argument per bucket gives the same \(A_b/2\pi\). Lee writes the result in energy units as \(\Delta E = \omega_0 A/2\pi\), with the area in \((\phi, \Delta E/\omega_0)\).

Numbers. A stationary bucket’s boundary is the pendulum’s separatrix, \(\delta = \delta_{\max}\cos(\Delta\phi/2)\) with \(\Delta\phi\) measured from its centre (chapter 12). Its area is twice the area under the upper half (the factor 2), and the integral of a cosine is a sine (F1, section 7):

\[ A_b = 2\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\delta_{\max}\cos\frac{\Delta\phi}{2}\,d\Delta\phi = 2\delta_{\max}\left[2\sin\frac{\Delta\phi}{2}\right]_{-\pi}^{\pi} = 2\delta_{\max}\,(2 + 2) = 8\,\delta_{\max} , \]

so one passage moves a stack by \(\Delta\delta = 8\delta_{\max}/2\pi = 1.27\,\delta_{\max}\). With \(\delta_{\max} = 10^{-3}\), one passage gives \(1.27\times10^{-3}\). Raising a stack from 26.6 to 31.4 GeV/\(c\), as the ISR did, is \(\Delta\delta = 0.180\), about 140 passages of such a bucket. (These bucket numbers are only for illustration; a moving bucket is smaller than a stationary one by chapter 12’s area factor.)

Exercise PD1. A septum injects five beams of 2 µm each into a ring. What is the smallest emittance the stack can have? What does Tecker’s rule of thumb predict?

Answer

At least \(5\times2 = 10\) µm, since each beam needs its own area and none can overlap another. Tecker’s rule, \(\varepsilon_r \gtrsim 1.5N_t\varepsilon_i\), gives about 15 µm once the gaps and filamentation are counted.

Exercise PD2. A stationary bucket of half-height \(\delta_{\max} = 2\times10^{-3}\) is moved down through a coasting stack. How far does the stack move, and how many passages raise it by 1 per cent in momentum?

Answer

\(\Delta\delta = 8\times2\times10^{-3}/2\pi = 2.55\times10^{-3}\) per passage. For \(\Delta\delta = 0.01\): \(0.01/2.55\times10^{-3} = 3.9\), so four passages.

Exercise PD3. Instead, an empty bucket is moved up, from below a stack to above it. What happens, and why might one want this?

Answer

Now the bucket starts below the particle’s line and ends above it, so the region under the line loses the bucket’s area: \(\Delta\delta = -A_b/2\pi\). The stack moves down in momentum. Either direction is useful: Lee describes phase displacement moving newly injected particles towards a cooling stack, in antiproton accumulators and polarised-proton cooler rings, and the bucket is moved one way or the other according to where the stack lies.

The wall

The first synchrotrons kept their beams inside. Experimenters put targets into the vacuum chamber and used whatever came out. The Brookhaven Cosmotron had been running for about a year when plans for an external beam began. Oreste Piccioni’s scheme was first tested in 1955. The beam struck a thin “jump target”, lost enough energy that its closed orbit moved inward, and half a betatron wavelength later passed a magnet that bent it out of the machine. Ejection efficiencies of 40 per cent were reached at the Cosmotron (Livingston and Blewett give as much as 50 per cent), and variants at the Bevatron, the ZGS, Saturne and Nimrod reached up to 65 per cent. The method scattered the beam and spread its energy, and it suited a strong-focusing machine badly, because its small momentum compaction needs a large energy loss to move the orbit (M. Q. Barton’s 1967 review).

The strong-focusing machines of the early 1960s needed a cleaner way out. At the AGS, a “fast kicker” was switched on in the 200 ns interval between bunches and deflected them behind a thin septum three quarters of a wavelength downstream. At CERN, B. Kuiper and G. Plass built a fast ejection system for the PS, which fed the neutrino experiments of the 1960s; they reported its operation at the 1965 Frascati conference. CERN’s septum magnets with coils only 3 mm thick let the septum and the ejection magnet be one device.

A fast ejection empties the ring in microseconds. Counting experiments, whose electronics could handle only so many particles a second, needed the beam spread over a much longer time. The answer came from cyclotrons. In 1951 James Tuck and Lee Teng proposed a resonant scheme to increase the radial step of the last turn; Kenneth Le Couteur analysed the regenerative deflector for synchrocyclotrons in 1951 (Proceedings of the Physical Society B 64, 1073), and by Bryant’s account published a fuller quantitative analysis in 1953; it worked at Liverpool in 1954. In 1961 Hugh Hereward proposed resonant extraction for the CERN PS, and C. L. Hammer and L. J. Laslett published a similar idea. Slow extraction over many thousands of turns became standard (P. J. Bryant’s 2017 history).

Getting the beam in was harder still, because one wanted more beam than one injection gives. A septum must sit outside the circulating beam, and anything that moves new particles onto the orbit moves the old ones as well. Phase-space density can be rearranged but not increased while particles move in fields described by a Hamiltonian. Multi-turn injection therefore fills the acceptance from the inside out, each turn in a fresh region, and the result is never denser than the injected beam. Luis Alvarez noted in 1951 that injecting negative ions and stripping them would avoid this. In 1960, when the record H⁻ current was only 70 µA, Gersh Budker at Novosibirsk started a programme to make charge-exchange injection work, with G. I. Dimov; they reported at the 1963 Dubna conference and accumulated protons in a test ring in 1964 (V. Dudnikov’s history).

Behind these was a quieter wall. No ring can take a proton from a few hundred keV to hundreds of GeV. Each machine is good over a limited range, and the highest energies need a chain of them, each handing on to the next. The rest of this chapter is about the joins.

The physics

1 Why a chain

Magnets. A ring of fixed radius needs \(B \propto p\). An iron-dominated magnet keeps a remanent field after it has been cycled, and its field shape at low excitation depends on the history of the iron. The remanent field is the field the yoke keeps when the current is switched off; the coercivity is the opposing field needed to wipe the iron’s magnetisation back to zero, a measure of how hard the steel holds on to it (F4, section 15 has the domains behind both). Both are fixed by the steel, not by the current, so they matter most when the current is small. In a 0.5 m prototype dipole for the FCC-ee booster, cycled between 6.5 and 58.9 mT, the remanent magnetisation of the yoke contributed about 60 µT, 1 per cent of the injection field, and the coercivity of the steel had to match between magnets to 10 per cent to keep dipole-to-dipole errors below \(10^{-3}\). For comparison, LEP injected at 22 mT and the SPS, as LEP’s injector, at 15.7 mT (FCC Feasibility Study Report, volume 2). Superconducting magnets have a different low-field problem: persistent currents in the filaments add sextupole errors that decay at injection and snap back when the ramp starts (chapter 17). The ratio of top field to injection field, the dynamic range, is therefore kept modest: in CERN’s chain each ring spans a factor of 5 to 17 in momentum.

Worked number: the LHC. First the factor in the rigidity (chapter 1, section 4.1). A momentum of 1 GeV/\(c\) is \(10^9\,e\,\mathrm{V}/c\) (F7, section 3), so for a particle of charge \(e\)

\[ B\rho = \frac{p}{e} = \frac{10^9\ \mathrm{V}}{2.9979\times10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}} = 3.3356\ \mathrm{T\,m} \]

for each GeV/\(c\) (a tesla is a V s/m\(^2\), so V s/m is T m). From chapter 1, the LHC’s dipoles bend with \(\rho = 2803.95\) m. At 450 GeV/\(c\), \(B\rho = 3.3356 \times 450 = 1501\) T m, so

\[ B_{\mathrm{inj}} = \frac{1501\ \mathrm{T\,m}}{2803.95\ \mathrm{m}} = 0.535\ \mathrm{T} \]

and at 7 TeV the same sum gives 8.33 T. The dynamic range is \(7000/450 = 15.6\).

Space charge. The beam’s own electric field defocuses it, and the magnetic field of the moving charge cancels most of that force at high energy. It lowers the tune of every particle a little, by an amount that depends on where the particle sits in the beam; this shift of each particle’s own tune is the incoherent tune shift (incoherent because it belongs to each particle separately, not to the motion of the beam’s centre). If it pushes some particles onto a resonance (chapter 18) they are lost, so it must stay below a few tenths. For a given ring and bunch length in metres, it scales as

\[ \Delta Q \propto -\frac{N}{\varepsilon_n\,\beta\gamma^2} \]

with \(N\) the particles per bunch and \(\varepsilon_n\) the normalised emittance; the derivation is in chapter 22. At fixed tune shift, the brightness \(N/\varepsilon_n\) a ring can hold grows as \(\beta\gamma^2\) of its injection energy. For a proton of kinetic energy \(T\), \(\gamma = 1 + T/m_pc^2\) and \(\beta = \sqrt{1 - 1/\gamma^2}\) (chapter 1, section 2); at 50 MeV, \(\gamma = 1.0533\) and \(\beta = 0.3140\). Raising the PS Booster’s injection from Linac2’s 50 MeV to Linac4’s 160 MeV raises \(\beta\gamma^2\) from 0.348 to 0.712, a factor of 2.04. Raising PS injection from 1.4 to 2 GeV raises it by a factor of 1.63. These two factors are the reasons for both parts of CERN’s LHC Injectors Upgrade.

RF frequency. The revolution frequency is \(f_0 = \beta c/C\). Where \(\beta\) changes, the RF must follow. In the PS Booster (\(C = 157.08\) m) a proton at 160 MeV has \(\beta = 0.520\) and \(f_0 = 0.992\) MHz; at 2 GeV, \(\beta = 0.948\) and \(f_0 = 1.809\) MHz. The cavities must sweep by a factor of 1.82 during the acceleration. Above a few GeV, \(\beta\) hardly changes and the frequency swing is small: the PS goes from 452 kHz at 2 GeV to 477 kHz at 26 GeV/\(c\), 5 per cent. Cavities that tune widely are the subject of chapter 13.

2 The chain today

CERN. Linac4 is 86 m long and accelerates H⁻ ions to 160 MeV in pulses of 400 µs. It produced its first beams in 2013 and became the source of protons for the LHC in 2020, replacing Linac2. The ions are stripped at injection into the four superposed rings of the PS Booster, which accelerate them to 2 GeV. The PS takes them to 26 GeV/\(c\), the SPS to 450 GeV/\(c\), and the LHC to 6.8 TeV per beam (7 TeV by design). CERN quotes the energies of its circular machines as momenta in GeV/\(c\), and above a few GeV the difference from the total energy is small.

Fermilab. The linac was commissioned in 1970 and raised to 400 MeV in 1993; it accelerates H⁻. The Booster, a rapid-cycling synchrotron at 15 Hz, takes the stripped protons to 8 GeV; it reached its design in 1971. Pre-requisite (continued): Linear systems and transfer matrices

Pre-requisite. Chapter 8 carried one particle’s \((x, x')\) through drifts and lenses by multiplying \(2\times2\) matrices, and chapter 16 wrote the beam’s ellipse with the Twiss functions and carried it by \(B_2 = MB_1M^T\). This section adds two working forms. First, the transfer matrix between any two points of a lattice written with the Twiss functions at the two ends and the phase advance between them; section 3 reads the placing of a kicker against a septum straight off it, including the case where the beam at the septum is not upright. Second, the same rule written as a \(3\times3\) matrix acting on \((\beta_x, \alpha_x, \gamma_x)\), which section 12 uses for a transfer line. Taught from Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, sections II.2 and II.3 (the Courant-Snyder parametrisation and the mapping matrix between two points), with the normalised coordinates of chapter 16.

The matrix between two points. In the normalised coordinates of section 3, \(X = x/\sqrt{\beta_x}\) and \(P = (\alpha_xx + \beta_xx')/\sqrt{\beta_x}\), every stretch of a linear lattice is a rotation by its phase advance \(\psi\) (rad). Write the change into normalised coordinates at a point as a matrix,

\[ \begin{pmatrix} X \\ P \end{pmatrix} = N\begin{pmatrix} x \\ x' \end{pmatrix}, \qquad N = \begin{pmatrix} 1/\sqrt{\beta_x} & 0 \\ \alpha_x/\sqrt{\beta_x} & \sqrt{\beta_x} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad N^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{\beta_x} & 0 \\ -\alpha_x/\sqrt{\beta_x} & 1/\sqrt{\beta_x} \end{pmatrix} . \]

Going from point 1 to point 2 is then three steps: into normalised coordinates with the Twiss functions at 1, rotate by \(\psi\), and back out with those at 2: \(M_{21} = N_2^{-1}R(\psi)N_1\), with \(R(\psi)\) the rotation of section 3. Multiplying out the three matrices, element by element,

\[ M_{21} = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{\dfrac{\beta_{x,2}}{\beta_{x,1}}}\left(\cos\psi + \alpha_{x,1}\sin\psi\right) & \sqrt{\beta_{x,1}\beta_{x,2}}\,\sin\psi \\ -\dfrac{(1 + \alpha_{x,1}\alpha_{x,2})\sin\psi + (\alpha_{x,2} - \alpha_{x,1})\cos\psi}{\sqrt{\beta_{x,1}\beta_{x,2}}} & \sqrt{\dfrac{\beta_{x,1}}{\beta_{x,2}}}\left(\cos\psi - \alpha_{x,2}\sin\psi\right) \end{pmatrix} . \]

The units come out right: the top right element is in metres (it turns an angle into a displacement), the bottom left in m\(^{-1}\), the diagonal ones are pure numbers. Its determinant is 1, as for any product of matrices of determinant 1. A numerical product of \(N_2^{-1}RN_1\) with \(\beta_{x,1} = 12\) m, \(\alpha_{x,1} = -1.3\), \(\beta_{x,2} = 30\) m, \(\alpha_{x,2} = 0.7\) and \(\psi = 1.1\) rad agrees with the formula element by element.

When the two points are the same point one turn apart, \(\beta_{x,2} = \beta_{x,1}\), \(\alpha_{x,2} = \alpha_{x,1}\) and \(\psi = 2\pi Q_x\), and the formula becomes chapter 16’s one-turn matrix. The matrix needs no knowledge of the magnets in between: the Twiss functions at the ends and the phase advance carry all of it.

Two elements read off. The top right element answers “an angle given here, what displacement there?”:

\[ x_2 = \sqrt{\beta_{x,1}\beta_{x,2}}\,\sin\psi\;\theta_1 . \]

It is largest at \(\psi = \pi/2\), and it is the extraction rule of section 3: a kick \(\theta_k\) appears a quarter of an oscillation later as a displacement \(\sqrt{\beta_{x,k}\beta_{x,s}}\,\theta_k\) at the septum. It is also why a single corrector moves the orbit most where the betatron function is large. The top left element answers “a displacement here with no angle, what displacement there?”. It vanishes when \(\cos\psi + \alpha_{x,1}\sin\psi = 0\), which for an upright beam (\(\alpha_{x,1} = 0\)) is \(\psi = \pi/2\), but otherwise is not.

At work: a septum where the beam is not upright. A beam leaves a septum parallel to the orbit, \(x' = 0\), at \(x_s = 10\) mm, where \(\beta_{x,s} = 60\) m and \(\alpha_{x,s} = 0.5\). For it to cross the orbit at the kicker, \(\tan\psi = -1/\alpha_{x,s}\), so \(\psi = 116.6°\), not \(90°\). With \(\sin\psi = 1/\sqrt{1 + \alpha_{x,s}^2}\) and \(\cos\psi = -\alpha_{x,s}/\sqrt{1 + \alpha_{x,s}^2}\), the bottom left element simplifies (the \(\alpha_{x,k}\) terms cancel) and the angle there is \(-x_s\sqrt{1 + \alpha_{x,s}^2}/\sqrt{\beta_{x,s}\beta_{x,k}}\). The kicker must cancel it:

\[ \theta_k = x_s\,\frac{\sqrt{1 + \alpha_{x,s}^2}}{\sqrt{\beta_{x,s}\beta_{x,k}}} = x_s\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{x,s}}{\beta_{x,k}}} . \]

With \(\beta_{x,k} = 100\) m this is 0.144 mrad, against 0.129 mrad from section 3’s formula, which is the case \(\alpha_{x,s} = 0\). The Twiss functions at the kicker other than \(\beta_{x,k}\) do not enter.

Carrying the Twiss functions. Chapter 16 showed that the matrix \(B = \begin{pmatrix}\beta_x & -\alpha_x \\ -\alpha_x & \gamma_x\end{pmatrix}\) is carried as \(B_2 = MB_1M^T\). Name the elements of \(M\) as in section 12, \(M = \begin{pmatrix} C & S \\ C' & S'\end{pmatrix}\): \(C\) and \(S\) are the displacements at the end of the line of the two particular particles that start with \((x, x') = (1, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\), the cosine-like and sine-like solutions, and \(C'\), \(S'\) their slopes. Multiply out the top left element of \(MB_1M^T\):

\[ \beta_{x,2} = C^2\beta_{x,1} - 2CS\,\alpha_{x,1} + S^2\gamma_{x,1} . \]

The off-diagonal element, with its sign, gives \(\alpha_{x,2} = -CC'\beta_{x,1} + (CS' + SC')\alpha_{x,1} - SS'\gamma_{x,1}\), and the bottom right gives \(\gamma_{x,2} = C'^2\beta_{x,1} - 2C'S'\alpha_{x,1} + S'^2\gamma_{x,1}\). These three are section 12’s \(3\times3\) matrix. It is linear in \((\beta_x, \alpha_x, \gamma_x)\) because \(B\) appears once in \(MB_1M^T\), though quadratic in the elements of \(M\). Its determinant is \((CS' - SC')^3 = 1\), and it keeps \(\beta_x\gamma_x - \alpha_x^2 = 1\): the ellipse changes shape but keeps its area. With the matrix of the septum-to-kicker stretch above, it carries \((60, 0.5, 0.0208)\) to \((100, -0.8, 0.0164)\), the Twiss functions assumed at the kicker, with \(\beta_x\gamma_x - \alpha_x^2 = 1.000\).

At work: a drift from a waist. A drift of length \(L\) has \(C = 1\), \(S = L\), \(C' = 0\), \(S' = 1\). From a waist, \(\alpha_{x,1} = 0\) and \(\gamma_{x,1} = 1/\beta^*\), with \(\beta^*\) the betatron function at the waist:

\[ \beta_x(L) = \beta^* + \frac{L^2}{\beta^*}, \qquad \alpha_x(L) = -\frac{L}{\beta^*}, \qquad \gamma_x(L) = \frac{1}{\beta^*} . \]

For \(\beta^* = 0.5\) m, 5 m of drift gives \(\beta_x = 50.5\) m: the smaller the waist, the faster the beam grows away from it, the trade-off at every collision point and at every stripping foil, where section 7 wants \(\beta_x\) small. \(\gamma_x\) stays fixed because a drift does not change angles.

Exercise TM1. A kicker sits \(60°\) downstream of a septum, with \(\alpha_x = 0\) at both and \(\beta_x = 100\) m at both. A beam leaves the septum parallel to the orbit at \(x_s = 10\) mm. Where is it at the kicker, and can one kick put it on the orbit?

Answer

From the top row, \(x_k = \cos60°\,x_s = 5\) mm, and from the bottom row \(x_k' = -\sin60°\,x_s/100\ \mathrm{m} = -0.087\) mrad. A kicker changes only the angle, so it cannot remove the 5 mm. The beam would circulate with a residual oscillation of normalised amplitude \(\cos60° = 0.5\) of the original offset, an injection error of the kind section 5 prices. This is why the phase advance from septum to kicker is held at the value that zeroes the top left element.

Exercise TM2. Show that the \(3\times3\) matrix of a thin lens of focal length \(f\) leaves \(\beta_x\) unchanged, and find what it does to \(\alpha_x\) for \(\beta_x = 10\) m and \(f = 5\) m at a waist.

Answer

A thin lens has \(C = 1\), \(S = 0\), \(C' = -1/f\), \(S' = 1\). The first row of the matrix is \((1, 0, 0)\), so \(\beta_{x,2} = \beta_{x,1}\): the lens is too short to change the beam’s size. The second row is \((1/f, 1, 0)\), so \(\alpha_{x,2} = \alpha_{x,1} + \beta_{x,1}/f = 0 + 10/5 = 2\): the beam now converges. The third row gives \(\gamma_{x,2} = \beta_{x,1}/f^2 + 2\alpha_{x,1}/f + \gamma_{x,1} = 0.4 + 0 + 0.1 = 0.5\) m\(^{-1}\), and indeed \((1 + 2^2)/10 = 0.5\).

Exercise TM3. The beam leaving a waist of \(\beta^* = 1\) m enters a line. After 2 m of drift what are \(\beta_x\) and \(\alpha_x\), and how far from the waist is \(\beta_x\) five times its waist value?

Answer

\(\beta_x = 1 + 4/1 = 5\) m and \(\alpha_x = -2\). Five times the waist value is \(\beta^* + L^2/\beta^* = 5\beta^*\), so \(L = 2\beta^* = 2\) m: the same point.

3 Phase space at the injection point

Use the normalised coordinates of chapter 16:

\[ X = \frac{x}{\sqrt{\beta_x}}, \qquad P = \frac{\alpha_x x + \beta_x x'}{\sqrt{\beta_x}} \]

with \(x\) (m) the displacement from the closed orbit, \(x' = dx/ds\) (rad), and \(\beta_x\) (m), \(\alpha_x\) the Twiss functions at that point. \(X\) and \(P\) are in m\(^{1/2}\). In these coordinates a particle moves on a circle of radius \(\sqrt{2J}\), where \(J = (X^2 + P^2)/2\) is its action (m rad), and from one point to another the circle rotates by the phase advance \(\psi\) (rad), the betatron phase gained between the two points, \(\psi = \int ds/\beta_x\) (chapter 16, section 7). This is simple harmonic motion drawn as a turning arrow (F3, section 9), with the betatron phase as the angle:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} X \\ P \end{pmatrix}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\psi & \sin\psi \\ -\sin\psi & \cos\psi \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} X \\ P \end{pmatrix}_1 \]

The rms emittance is \(\varepsilon = \langle J \rangle\) for a beam whose phases are spread evenly (action-angle variables, chapter 16): the average over all particles of their actions.

A septum delivers the new beam parallel to the closed orbit but displaced by \(x_s\). Take \(\alpha_x = 0\) at the septum for simplicity; then \((X, P)_s = (x_s/\sqrt{\beta_{x,s}}, 0)\). After a phase advance \(\psi\) the beam is at \(X_k = X_s\cos\psi\), \(P_k = -X_s\sin\psi\). A kicker is short, so the particle’s position does not change while it crosses; only its angle does, and with \(x\) fixed a change of angle changes only \(P\). To land on the orbit, \(X_k\) must be zero, so \(\psi = \pi/2\), and the kicker must cancel \(P_k = -X_s\). At fixed \(x\) the definition of \(P\) gives \(\Delta P = \beta_{x,k}\Delta x'/\sqrt{\beta_{x,k}} = \sqrt{\beta_{x,k}}\,\Delta x'\), so a change \(\Delta P\) is an angle \(\Delta x' = \Delta P/\sqrt{\beta_{x,k}}\), and with \(\Delta P = X_s = x_s/\sqrt{\beta_{x,s}}\)

\[ \theta_k = \frac{x_s}{\sqrt{\beta_{x,s}\,\beta_{x,k}}} \]

The kick is smallest when both betatron functions are large. In practice septum and kicker are placed on either side of a quadrupole that defocuses in the plane of the kick, which lowers the kicker strength needed (Tecker); the formula above then applies with the betatron functions and phase advance of that layout. A closed-orbit bump, three or four small dipoles whose kicks displace the closed orbit over a short stretch of the ring and cancel everywhere else (chapter 18, section 2), moves the circulating beam towards the septum; that reduces \(x_s\) and so the kick. With \(x_s = 10\) mm and \(\beta_{x,s} = \beta_{x,k} = 100\) m, \(\theta_k = 0.1\) mrad.

Extraction is the same picture run backwards: the kicker gives the circulating beam an angle, and a quarter of an oscillation later it is displaced by \(\sqrt{\beta_{x,k}\beta_{x,s}}\,\theta_k\) and enters the septum.

4 Kickers and septa

Kickers. A kicker is a dipole whose field must rise in a time shorter than a gap between bunches. Its coil is usually a single turn, so its inductance is small, and its yoke is ferrite (a typical nickel-zinc kicker ferrite has an initial relative permeability of 2100) because laminated steel cannot follow nanosecond changes. Take Ampère’s law (F4, section 12) round a loop that runs through the ferrite and once across the gap \(g\) (m) of a C-shaped core (Figure 21.4). It encloses the current \(I\) (A) of the single-turn conductor once, so \(\oint \mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{l}/\mu = I\), with \(\mu = \mu_0\) in the gap and \(\mu = \mu_r\mu_0\) in the ferrite. The same flux crosses both, so the ferrite’s path of length \(l_{Fe}\) adds \(Bl_{Fe}/(\mu_r\mu_0)\) against \(Bg/\mu_0\) for the gap: with \(\mu_r = 2100\) the ferrite’s share is \(l_{Fe}/(2100\,g)\), a per cent or less, and is neglected (its reluctance, chapter 8). Then \(Bg/\mu_0 = I\):

\[ B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{g} \]

The inductance is the flux linked per unit current, \(L = \Phi/I\) (F4, section 15). The flux through the gap, of width \(w\) and length \(l\) along the beam, is \(\Phi = Bwl = \mu_0 Iwl/g\), so

\[ L = \frac{\mu_0 w l}{g} \]

For \(w = 0.1\) m, \(l = 1\) m and \(g = 0.05\) m, \(L = 2.51\) µH. A current of 3 kA gives 75 mT. To raise it in 1 µs, the voltage across the magnet is \(L\,dI/dt = 7.5\) kV, which is why kicker systems run at tens of kilovolts (V. Kain’s CAS lecture quotes 3 kA in 1 µs as typical).

Figure 21.4. A C-core kicker in cross-section, the beam coming out of the page. The single-turn conductor carries \(I\) into the page at the back of the gap and returns outside the core. The dashed loop of Ampère’s law runs through the ferrite and crosses the gap once, so it encloses \(I\) once: \(B = \mu_0 I/g\). Round a current into the page the field circulates clockwise, so in the gap it points down, and the force \(q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\) (F2, section 3) on a positive particle coming out of the page points to the right, towards the open side. Proportions not to scale.

The pulse comes from a pulse-forming network or line (PFN, PFL): a chain of capacitors and inductors, or a long coaxial cable, charged slowly to a voltage \(V_p\) and discharged through a fast switch: a thyratron, a valve filled with hydrogen at low pressure that stays open until a trigger pulse makes the gas conduct and then carries kiloamperes within tens of nanoseconds, or a stack of thyristors, its semiconductor equivalent, turned on by a pulse at a gate (chapter 15). A line of impedance \(Z\) discharged into a matched load, a resistor equal to \(Z\), gives a flat pulse of height \(V_p/2\) and length equal to twice the line’s one-way delay \(\tau\). Why (chapter 8; F5, section 11): seen from its end, a charged line behaves like a source \(V_p\) behind its own impedance \(Z\), so the load and the line share \(V_p\) equally, as two equal resistors in series do. Closing the switch launches a wave that halves the line’s voltage as it travels to the far, open end; it reflects there and comes back, emptying the line behind it, and the current stops when it returns to the load, after \(2\tau\). Matching the load to the line is what keeps the top flat: a mismatched load would reflect part of each wave and leave a staircase of steps. If the magnet is a lumped inductor fed from impedance \(Z\), the circuit is the LR circuit of F4, section 15: \(ZI + L\,dI/dt = V\), whose solution from \(I = 0\) is \(I = (V/Z)(1 - e^{-tZ/L})\). The current rises as \(1 - e^{-tZ/L}\), with time constant \(L/Z\): 0.50 µs for the magnet above and \(Z = 5\ \Omega\). To go faster, the magnet is cut into \(n\) cells, each a slice of inductance \(L_c\) with capacitors \(C_c\) to ground, so that the magnet itself is a transmission line of impedance \(\sqrt{L_c/C_c}\). The field then rises in each cell as fast as the switch allows, and the whole magnet is full after the wave has crossed it, in \(n\sqrt{L_cC_c}\). This is a travelling-wave kicker.

Worked number: the LHC injection kicker, MKI (L. Ducimetière and others, PAC 2003). Each of the four magnets per ring is 2.7 m long, made of 33 cells with \(L_c = 101\) nH and \(C_c = 4.04\) nF:

\[ Z = \sqrt{\frac{101\ \mathrm{nH}}{4.04\ \mathrm{nF}}} = 5.0\ \Omega, \qquad n\sqrt{L_cC_c} = 33 \times 20.2\ \mathrm{ns} = 667\ \mathrm{ns} \]

The PFN is charged to 54 kV, so the magnet sees 27 kV and carries \(27\ \mathrm{kV}/5\ \Omega = 5.4\) kA. Each magnet gives 0.3 T m, so four give 1.2 T m, and at 450 GeV/\(c\) the kick is \(1.2/1501 = 0.80\) mrad. The specified rise time (0.5 to 99.5 per cent) is 0.9 µs, the flat top up to 7.86 µs with ripple below ±0.5 per cent, and the fall 3.0 µs. The fill time of 667 ns is most of the rise.

Septa. A septum magnet has two apertures: one with field for the transferred beam, one without for the circulating beam, separated by a thin wall that carries the current. Magnetic septa have walls of 2 to 20 mm and, with single-turn coils, currents of 5 to 25 kA. An electrostatic septum separates the regions with a plane of thin wires or foil, typically 100 µm or less, held at ground, with a high-voltage electrode one or two centimetres away. A particle of charge \(q\), momentum \(p\) and speed \(v\) crossing a transverse electric field \(\mathcal{E}\) (V/m) over a length \(L_E\) (m) spends a time \(L_E/v\) in it and so gains a transverse momentum \(q\mathcal{E} L_E/v\), an impulse (F3, section 3). For a small angle the deflection is the transverse momentum over the momentum:

\[ \theta = \frac{q \mathcal{E} L_E}{p v} \]

For the five electrostatic septa of the SPS, each about 3.1 m long (15.5 m of anode in all), a field of 10 MV/m deflects a 400 GeV/\(c\) proton by \(10^7 \times 15.5/(4\times10^{11}) = 0.39\) mrad (with \(q = e\) the numerator is \(1.55\times10^8\) eV, and for a relativistic proton \(pv \approx pc = 4\times10^{11}\) eV). The plane of wires, at ground, is called the anode, and the high-voltage electrode, at a negative voltage, the cathode. Small, but enough to clear the thick magnetic septa downstream after a quarter of a betatron oscillation. For comparison, the LHC’s injection septa give 12 mrad, \(0.012 \times 1501 = 18\) T m, in five magnets, and its kickers only 0.8 mrad (0.85 mrad in the High-Luminosity LHC design report). Septa give large angles slowly; kickers give small angles quickly.

5 Injection errors and mismatch

Suppose the injected beam arrives with its centre offset in normalised phase space by \((\Delta X, \Delta P)\). Each particle starts at \((X_0 + \Delta X, P_0 + \Delta P)\), where \((X_0, P_0)\) is its position relative to the beam’s centre. The centre oscillates about the orbit and a beam position monitor sees an injection oscillation. Every real ring has some amplitude-dependent tune (from sextupoles, octupoles, chromaticity with momentum spread, space charge), so particles with different amplitudes drift apart in phase. After some hundreds or thousands of turns the phases are spread evenly: the beam has filamented. Each particle’s action is unchanged by the linear motion, so the final rms emittance is the average action:

\[ \varepsilon_f = \left\langle \tfrac12\left[(X_0 + \Delta X)^2 + (P_0 + \Delta P)^2\right]\right\rangle = \varepsilon + \tfrac12\left(\Delta X^2 + \Delta P^2\right) \]

Step by step: \((X_0 + \Delta X)^2 = X_0^2 + 2X_0\Delta X + \Delta X^2\), and the same for \(P\). Average over the particles (F6, section 3): \(\tfrac12\langle X_0^2 + P_0^2\rangle\) is the beam’s own mean action, \(\varepsilon\); the cross terms \(\langle X_0\rangle\Delta X\) and \(\langle P_0\rangle\Delta P\) vanish because \(\langle X_0\rangle = \langle P_0 \rangle = 0\), the offsets being measured from the beam’s centre; and \(\Delta X\), \(\Delta P\) are the same for every particle. Writing the offset as \(a\) rms beam sizes, \(a^2 = (\Delta X^2 + \Delta P^2)/\varepsilon\):

\[ \varepsilon_f = \varepsilon\left(1 + \frac{a^2}{2}\right) \]

A tracking check with \(2\times10^5\) particles and an amplitude-dependent tune gives 1.494 for \(a = 1\) against 1.5 from the formula.

Figure 21.5. Filamentation after an injection error of \(a = 2\) beam sizes, with 400 particles drawn at random. (a) As injected: a round Gaussian beam of rms emittance \(\varepsilon\) whose centre sits at \(\Delta X = 2\sqrt\varepsilon\); the centre then circles the origin on the dashed path, which a beam position monitor sees as an oscillation. (b) Later: every particle has kept its distance from the origin, \(\sqrt{2J}\), but slightly different tunes have spread their phases evenly round. The beam is now centred, and larger. Grey circle: radius \(\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\), the rms radius of the beam as injected; blue: \(\sqrt{2\varepsilon_f}\) with \(\varepsilon_f = \varepsilon(1 + a^2/2) = 3\varepsilon\). The mean action of these 400 points is \(3.01\varepsilon\). An offset of one beam size costs 50 per cent of emittance; to stay within 5 per cent, \(a < 0.32\). At LHC injection, with \(\varepsilon_n = 3.5\) µm, the geometric emittance is \(\varepsilon = \varepsilon_n/\beta\gamma\) (chapter 16, section 9), with \(\beta\gamma = p/m_pc = 450/0.93827 = 479.6\): \(3.5\ \mu\mathrm{m}/479.6 = 7.30\) nm, so at an injection point with \(\beta_x = 100\) m (an example value) the beam size is 0.85 mm and the injection must be good to 0.27 mm. The beam size is \(\sigma_x = \sqrt{\beta_x\varepsilon}\), and \(a < 0.32\) means an offset below \(0.32\sigma_x\). Hadron rings therefore have transverse dampers, feedback systems that measure the oscillation turn by turn and kick it out before filamentation sets in.

If the beam’s ellipse has the wrong shape, Twiss parameters \((\beta_{x,1}, \alpha_{x,1}, \gamma_{x,1})\) instead of the ring’s \((\beta_{x,2}, \alpha_{x,2}, \gamma_{x,2})\), the second moments of the beam are \(\langle x^2\rangle = \varepsilon\beta_{x,1}\), \(\langle xx'\rangle = -\varepsilon\alpha_{x,1}\), \(\langle x'^2\rangle = \varepsilon\gamma_{x,1}\). The ring’s action is \(J = \tfrac12(\gamma_{x,2}x^2 + 2\alpha_{x,2}xx' + \beta_{x,2}x'^2)\) (chapter 16’s ellipse, written with the ring’s Twiss functions). Averaging it over the beam replaces \(x^2\), \(xx'\) and \(x'^2\) by their averages: \(\langle J\rangle = \tfrac12(\gamma_{x,2}\,\varepsilon\beta_{x,1} - 2\alpha_{x,2}\,\varepsilon\alpha_{x,1} + \beta_{x,2}\,\varepsilon\gamma_{x,1})\). Each action is kept as the beam filaments, so after filamentation

\[ \varepsilon_f = \langle J\rangle = \varepsilon H, \qquad H = \tfrac12\left(\beta_{x,1}\gamma_{x,2} - 2\alpha_{x,1}\alpha_{x,2} + \gamma_{x,1}\beta_{x,2}\right) \ge 1 \]

For \(\alpha_x = 0\) in both and \(\beta_{x,1}/\beta_{x,2} = r\), \(H = (r + 1/r)/2\): 1.017 for \(r = 1.2\), 1.25 for \(r = 2\). A 20 per cent error in \(\beta_x\) costs under 2 per cent of emittance; the steering error is usually the larger danger. A dispersion mismatch acts like a steering error that differs for each momentum: a particle of relative momentum error \(\delta\) has its own closed orbit, displaced by \(D_x\delta\) (chapter 16, section 10), and if the line delivers it with a different displacement it starts an oscillation about that orbit.

Electrons are forgiving. Synchrotron radiation damps their oscillations (chapter 20), so injection errors are forgotten in a few damping times, and lepton (electron and positron) rings inject on purpose with an offset in angle (betatron injection) or in momentum at a point with dispersion (synchrotron injection) and let the new particles damp into the stored beam.

6 Multi-turn injection

When a linac’s pulse is longer than one turn of the ring, or its current too low, a hadron ring injects over many turns. A set of three or four bumper magnets, the dipoles of a closed-orbit bump (section 3), holds the closed orbit close to the septum, so that the incoming beam lands near the orbit. Turn by turn the bump falls. The tune rotates each injected beamlet away from the septum, and the shrinking bump moves the orbit away, so successive beamlets fill rings of phase space from the inside out. With a fractional tune near 0.25, each turn turns a beamlet’s point in normalised phase space by \(2\pi\times0.25 = 90°\) about the orbit (the whole turns are invisible), so it comes back to the septum’s side every four turns; by then the orbit has moved away, and if it has moved far enough the beamlet misses the blade.

Liouville’s theorem limits the result: each turn occupies a new region, and the septum’s thickness leaves gaps. F. Tecker’s CAS lecture gives the rule

\[ \varepsilon_r \gtrsim 1.5\,N_t\,\varepsilon_i \]

for \(N_t\) turns of injected emittance \(\varepsilon_i\). Ten turns give at least 15 times the emittance. Particles that come back to the septum are lost on it, up to 30 to 40 per cent, and the acceptance limits the process to 10 to 20 turns.

Pre-requisite (continued): Distribution functions, Vlasov equation, beam temperature

Pre-requisite. Chapter 7 described a beam by its density \(f\) in phase space and showed that, under a Hamiltonian, \(f\) is carried unchanged along each particle’s path. This section derives the same law from counting particles in a box, which makes room for a source: particles created in phase space, as stripping creates protons. With the source in place one sees exactly how charge-exchange injection raises the density where a septum cannot, and by how much. Section 7 uses it. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, Statistical Physics, §3 (the phase points as a gas obeying a continuity equation); Tong, Classical Mechanics, section 10.2 (Liouville’s theorem and equation); and Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, section III.8 A1 (strip injection).

Counting in a box. Take one degree of freedom, with the canonical pair \((x, p)\) (a position and the momentum that goes with it in Hamilton’s equations), and a beam of many particles with density \(f(x, p, t)\): the number of particles in a small rectangle of sides \(dx\) and \(dp\) is \(f\,dx\,dp\). The units of \(f\) are particles per unit area of the phase plane; in the accelerator’s \((x, x')\), per m rad. Each particle moves with the phase-plane velocity \((\dot x, \dot p)\). Fix the rectangle in the plane and count what changes in a time \(dt\).

  • Particles cross the left edge, at \(x\), at the rate \(f\dot x\,dp\), and leave by the right edge, at \(x + dx\), at the rate \(f\dot x\,dp\) evaluated there. The net gain is \(-\partial(f\dot x)/\partial x\;dx\,dp\,dt\).
  • In the same way the bottom and top edges give \(-\partial(f\dot p)/\partial p\;dx\,dp\,dt\).
  • Particles may also be created inside the rectangle, at a rate \(S\) per unit area of the plane per second.

Divide by \(dx\,dp\,dt\):

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial(f\dot x)}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial(f\dot p)}{\partial p} = S . \]

This is the continuity equation of a fluid, written in the phase plane instead of in space, and with a source. It holds for any motion.

Where the Hamiltonian enters. Expand the derivatives of the products. The terms in which \(f\) is not differentiated are \(f\) times the divergence of the flow, \(\partial\dot x/\partial x + \partial\dot p/\partial p\). Under a Hamiltonian, \(\dot x = \partial H/\partial p\) and \(\dot p = -\partial H/\partial x\), so the divergence is \(\partial^2H/\partial x\,\partial p - \partial^2H/\partial p\,\partial x = 0\): the flow does not compress. What remains is

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial p}\frac{\partial H}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \lbrace f, H\rbrace = S , \]

with the Poisson bracket in Tong’s convention. This is the equation of section 7. The left side is the rate of change of \(f\) seen by an observer riding with a particle, so

\[ \frac{df}{dt}\bigg|_{\text{along a path}} = S . \]

With no source, this is chapter 7’s statement: the density carried by each particle never changes. With a source, it rises by exactly what is created at the particle’s position. Three remarks.

  • The source must be something outside the Hamiltonian. Stripping is one: an H⁻ ion, which belongs to another species with its own density, becomes a proton at the foil. The incoming ions have their own equation, with a sink \(-S\) at the foil, and the sum of the two densities is not carried by any one Hamiltonian, because the two species bend oppositely in the same field.
  • Friction is another way out, through the divergence instead of the source. If \(\dot p = -\partial H/\partial x - cp\), the divergence is \(-c\), and then \(df/dt = +cf\) along a path: the density grows as the beam shrinks. Radiation damping in an electron ring (chapter 20) and cooling (chapter 24) work this way.
  • A septum adds no source term. Protons from the linac and protons in the ring are the same species in the same field from the moment they share an aperture, so the whole collection obeys the source-free equation, and the largest density anywhere can never exceed the largest density brought in.

Stacking with a source. Make it concrete. Let the incoming H⁻ beam cover a small region \(D\) of the ring’s phase plane with density \(f_i\) at the foil, and let stripping turn nearly all of it into protons. Put \(D\) on the orbit, matched to the ring’s ellipse, so that the protons already there rotate within \(D\) and come back to it each turn. Every passage through the foil adds \(f_i\), and after \(N_t\) turns

\[ f \approx N_t f_i \]

in \(D\), while \(D\) keeps its area. Multi-turn injection through a septum must put each turn in a fresh region: after \(N_t\) turns the area is at least \(N_t\) times, in practice about \(1.5N_t\) times, that of one turn (section 6), and the density is at most \(f_i\). For the same number of turns charge exchange gives a beam about \(1.5N_t\) times brighter. In practice the bump is moved during injection, so that the protons are painted over a chosen region rather than piled on one spot; the source term lets one choose the final distribution instead of having it dictated by the septum. Two things limit the pile: each passage of the stored protons through the foil scatters them, which is a diffusion term on the left side (the multiple-scattering section below), and the foil heats.

Exercise V2. For the damped motion \(\dot x = p/m\), \(\dot p = -kx - cp\), with \(k\) and \(c\) positive constants, show that the phase-space area of any group of particles shrinks as \(e^{-ct}\). Why can no arrangement of magnets do this?

Answer

The divergence of the flow is \(\partial\dot x/\partial x + \partial\dot p/\partial p = 0 - c\). Along a path the source-free continuity equation gives \(df/dt = -f\times(-c) = cf\), so the density grows as \(e^{ct}\); since the number of particles is fixed, their area shrinks as \(e^{-ct}\). A magnetic (or any static) field acts through a Hamiltonian, whose flow has zero divergence, so it cannot shrink areas; damping needs a force that depends on the velocity in a way no Hamiltonian gives, such as the loss of momentum to radiation.

Pre-requisite. Chapter 3 solved \(dN/dx = -N/\lambda\), a quantity lost in proportion to what is left, and a balance with a constant source; chapter 2 started the subject. This section solves a chain of two such losses, where the second population is fed by the first, by the integrating factor, and reads off where the middle population peaks and how fast it dies. Section 7 uses it for H⁻ ions that become H⁰ atoms and then protons in a carbon foil, to choose the foil’s thickness. Taught from Tong, Classical Mechanics, section 2.5.5 (an equation with linear drag solved by an integrating factor), with the decay chains of radium in chapter 1 as the physical model.

The chain. A beam of H⁻ enters a foil. Measure depth by \(n\), the number of carbon atoms per unit area crossed so far (cm\(^{-2}\)), so that the foil’s own thickness drops out of the equations. Let \(f_-\), \(f_0\) and \(f_+\) be the fractions of the beam that are H⁻, H⁰ and protons at depth \(n\). With the cross-sections of section 7, an H⁻ ion has probability \(\sigma_-\,dn\) of being changed in the next layer \(dn\), where \(\sigma_- = \sigma_{-0} + \sigma_{-+}\), and an H⁰ atom has \(\sigma_{0+}\,dn\). Counting what enters and leaves each population:

\[ \frac{df_-}{dn} = -\sigma_-f_-, \qquad \frac{df_0}{dn} = \sigma_{-0}f_- - \sigma_{0+}f_0, \qquad f_+ = 1 - f_- - f_0, \]

with \(f_-(0) = 1\) and \(f_0(0) = 0\). The last line is the bookkeeping: nothing is lost, only changed. The cross-sections are areas per atom, cm\(^2\), so \(\sigma n\) is a pure number.

The first population. The first equation is chapter 3’s decay, the simplest equation of change (F1, section 10): a rate of loss proportional to what is left gives \(f_- = e^{-\sigma_-n}\).

The second, by an integrating factor. Put the first solution into the second equation and move the loss term to the left:

\[ \frac{df_0}{dn} + \sigma_{0+}f_0 = \sigma_{-0}e^{-\sigma_-n} . \]

The left side is almost the derivative of a product. Multiply both sides by \(e^{\sigma_{0+}n}\), the integrating factor:

\[ e^{\sigma_{0+}n}\frac{df_0}{dn} + \sigma_{0+}e^{\sigma_{0+}n}f_0 = \frac{d}{dn}\left(e^{\sigma_{0+}n}f_0\right) = \sigma_{-0}e^{(\sigma_{0+} - \sigma_-)n} . \]

Integrate from 0 to \(n\), using \(f_0(0) = 0\):

\[ e^{\sigma_{0+}n}f_0 = \frac{\sigma_{-0}}{\sigma_{0+} - \sigma_-}\left(e^{(\sigma_{0+} - \sigma_-)n} - 1\right), \qquad f_0 = \frac{\sigma_{-0}}{\sigma_{0+} - \sigma_-}\left(e^{-\sigma_-n} - e^{-\sigma_{0+}n}\right) . \]

This is section 7’s formula. The method works for any equation \(dy/dn + ay = g(n)\) with constant \(a\): multiply by \(e^{an}\), and the left side becomes the derivative of \(e^{an}y\).

Reading the answer.

  • A rise and a fall. \(f_0\) starts at zero, rises while the H⁻ feed it, and falls once they are used up. Setting \(df_0/dn = 0\) gives \(\sigma_-e^{-\sigma_-n} = \sigma_{0+}e^{-\sigma_{0+}n}\), so the peak is at \(n^* = \ln(\sigma_-/\sigma_{0+})/(\sigma_- - \sigma_{0+})\).
  • The slower loss rules the tail. At large \(n\) the faster exponential has gone, and \(f_0 \approx \sigma_{-0}e^{-\sigma_{0+}n}/(\sigma_- - \sigma_{0+})\) when \(\sigma_- > \sigma_{0+}\). Whatever feeds it, the H⁰ fraction cannot die faster than H⁰ itself is stripped. For a clean beam of protons the second step, the slower one, sets the foil.
  • Equal rates. If \(\sigma_{0+} = \sigma_-\) the formula reads \(0/0\). Go back to the integrated form: the right side of the product equation is the constant \(\sigma_{-0}\), so \(f_0 = \sigma_{-0}n\,e^{-\sigma_-n}\), peaked at \(n = 1/\sigma_-\).

Pre-requisite: Multiple scattering

Pre-requisite. This section explains why a fast proton that crosses a thin foil comes out with a small random change of direction, and how large it is. It builds the rms scattering angle from single Rutherford deflections added at random, shows where the logarithm in the formula comes from, introduces the radiation length \(X_0\) as the material constant that collects the dependence on the foil, and ends with Highland’s formula and its newer form after Lynch and Dahl, which section 7 uses for the stripping foil, together with the emittance growth that the scattering causes. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §20 (small-angle scattering, the deflection as transverse momentum over momentum); the Rutherford scattering section of chapter 1; Livingston and Blewett, Particle Accelerators, section 14-3 (the radiation length); and Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, exercise 2.3.12 (the scattering formula, the radiation length formula and the emittance growth at a stripping foil).

The idea. A carbon foil of 200 µg/cm² is about a micrometre thick and holds \(10^{19}\) atoms per cm². A proton crossing it passes within an atom’s size of some thousands of nuclei (\(n\pi a^2 \approx 2000\) within the screening radius \(a\) met below), and is pulled sideways a little by each, in a direction set by where the nucleus happened to be. Each pull is tiny and the directions are random. The sum is a random walk in angle: by the central limit theorem (F6, section 7) its distribution is close to a Gaussian, with an rms that grows as the square root of the number of encounters, so as the square root of the thickness. A rare close encounter gives a larger angle by itself; such single scatters make a tail beyond the Gaussian core.

One encounter. Let the proton have charge \(ze\) (\(z = 1\)), momentum \(p\) and speed \(v = \beta c\), and pass a nucleus of charge \(Ze\) at impact parameter \(b\). For a small deflection the path is nearly straight, and the deflection angle is the transverse momentum it picks up divided by \(p\) (Landau and Lifshitz, §20). Integrating the transverse part of a Coulomb force \(k/r^2\) along a straight line at distance \(b\), travelled at speed \(v\), gives an impulse \(2k/(bv)\): with the closest approach at \(t = 0\), the distance is \(r = \sqrt{b^2 + v^2t^2}\) and the transverse part of the force is \((k/r^2)(b/r)\), so

\[ \Delta p_\perp = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{kb\,dt}{(b^2 + v^2t^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{k}{bv}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{du}{(1 + u^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{2k}{bv}, \]

with \(u = vt/b\) and the last integral equal to 2 (put \(u = \tan w\); it becomes \(\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos w\,dw\)); the same follows from chapter 1’s \(b = (d/2)\cot(\theta/2)\), which for small angles is \(\theta = d/b = 2k/(mv^2b)\). Here

\[ k = \frac{zZe^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = zZ\,r_em_ec^2 , \]

where \(r_e = 2.818\) fm is the classical electron radius and \(m_ec^2 = 0.511\) MeV; the second form is only a convenient way to write the constant. So

\[ \theta(b) = \frac{2k}{bpv} = \frac{2zZ\,r_e\,m_ec^2}{b\,\beta cp} . \]

The result holds at any speed: a relativistic proton sees the nucleus’s field squeezed into a shorter time, but stronger by the same factor \(\gamma\), and the impulse is unchanged. For a 160 MeV proton (\(\beta cp = 296.7\) MeV) on carbon, \(2zZr_em_ec^2/\beta cp = 5.82\times10^{-17}\) m, so \(\theta = 58\) µrad at \(b = 1\) pm and 0.58 mrad at \(b = 100\) fm.

Adding them up. Look at the angle projected on one plane, \(\theta_x\), since that is what changes \(x'\). An encounter whose deflection points at azimuth \(\varphi\) contributes \(\theta\cos\varphi\). The azimuths are random, so the contributions have mean zero and mean square \(\theta^2\langle\cos^2\varphi\rangle = \tfrac12\theta^2\). Independent contributions add their mean squares (chapter 1; F6, section 3). Encounters with impact parameter between \(b\) and \(b + db\) are those whose nucleus lies in a thin ring of radius \(b\) and width \(db\) round the proton’s path, of area \(2\pi b\,db\). A foil of \(n\) atoms per unit area gives \(n\,2\pi b\,db\) encounters with impact parameters between \(b\) and \(b + db\), so the rms projected angle \(\theta_0\) obeys

\[ \theta_0^2 = \frac12\int\theta(b)^2\,n\,2\pi b\,db = \pi n\left(\frac{2k}{pv}\right)^2\int_{b_{\min}}^{b_{\max}}\frac{db}{b} = \pi n\left(\frac{2k}{pv}\right)^2\ln\frac{b_{\max}}{b_{\min}} . \]

The integral diverges at both ends, slowly, so the answer depends on where the encounters stop counting, but only through a logarithm. Two effects set the ends, and both are easier to state as angles, since \(\theta \propto 1/b\) turns the ratio of impact parameters into the inverse ratio of angles.

  • Small angles: screening. Far from the nucleus its charge is hidden by the atom’s electrons, beyond about \(a = 0.885\,a_0Z^{-1/3}\) (chapter 1’s Thomas-Fermi radius; 25.8 pm for carbon). For a fast proton the cut is set by waves rather than by orbits: an obstacle of size \(a\) cannot scatter a wave of reduced wavelength \(\hbar/p\) into angles much smaller than \(\chi_a = \hbar/(pa)\) (the de Broglie section). At 160 MeV, \(\hbar/p = 0.35\) fm and \(\chi_a = 13.4\) µrad.
  • Large angles: what happens at least once. The number of encounters in the foil with deflection larger than \(\theta\) is \(n\pi b(\theta)^2 = n\pi(2k/pv\theta)^2\). It equals 1 at the angle \(\chi_c\) with \(\chi_c^2 = \pi n(2k/pv)^2\). Larger deflections happen, on average, less than once per proton: they are the single scatters of the tail, not part of the Gaussian core, and the core’s width must not count them.

The electrons of the atom scatter too: \(Z\) of them, each of charge 1, add \(Z\times1^2\) to the nucleus’s \(Z^2\), so \(Z^2\) becomes \(Z(Z + 1)\). Then

\[ \chi_c^2 = 4\pi n\,z^2Z(Z + 1)\left(\frac{r_e\,m_ec^2}{\beta cp}\right)^2, \qquad \theta_0^2 \approx \chi_c^2\ln\frac{\chi_c}{\chi_a} . \]

Read it. The square of the angle grows in proportion to the number of atoms crossed, as a random walk must. It falls as \(1/(\beta cp)^2\), so stiff beams scatter less. It grows as \(Z^2\) per atom, but per gram as \(Z(Z + 1)/A\), about \(Z/2\): light materials scatter least for a given mass. And the logarithm grows with \(\chi_c\), so slowly with thickness: \(\theta_0\) grows a little faster than the square root of the thickness, because a thicker foil promotes more of the rare large deflections into the core.

At work. Section 7’s case is 25 crossings of 200 µg/cm² of carbon, \(x = 5\) mg/cm², at 160 MeV. Then \(n = 0.005\times6.022\times10^{23}/12.011 = 2.51\times10^{20}\) cm\(^{-2}\), which gives \(\chi_c = 0.177\) mrad, \(\ln(\chi_c/\chi_a) = 2.58\) and \(\theta_0 = 0.28\) mrad. The sharp cut-offs make this estimate rough; the fitted formulas below give 0.33 to 0.35 mrad.

The radiation length. The formula needs \(n\), \(Z(Z + 1)\) and a screening logarithm, all properties of the material. One tabulated constant collects them. High-energy electrons in matter lose energy mainly by radiating in the fields of nuclei (bremsstrahlung). That process depends on the same field of the same screened atom, and its strength per atom also goes as \(Z(Z + 1)\) with a screening logarithm. The radiation length \(X_0\) is the thickness in which a high-energy electron’s energy falls by a factor \(e\) (Livingston and Blewett, section 14-3). In g/cm², approximately (Lee’s exercise 2.3.12),

\[ X_0 = \frac{716.4\,A}{Z(Z + 1)\ln(287/\sqrt Z)}\ \mathrm{g/cm^2}, \]

with \(A\) the molar mass in g/mol. For carbon this gives 43.0 g/cm² against the tabulated 42.70; for aluminium 24.3. Using the theory of bremsstrahlung, \(1/X_0 \approx 4\alpha r_e^2(N_A/A)Z(Z + 1)\ln(183Z^{-1/3})\), with \(\alpha = 1/137.036\) the fine-structure constant. Put \(n = N_Ax/A\), with \(x\) in g/cm², into \(\chi_c^2\) and the material constants become \(x/X_0\):

\[ \theta_0 \approx \frac{\sqrt{\pi/\alpha}\;m_ec^2}{\beta cp}\,z\sqrt{\frac{x}{X_0}}\,\sqrt{\frac{\ln(\chi_c/\chi_a)}{\ln(183Z^{-1/3})}}, \qquad \sqrt{\pi/\alpha}\;m_ec^2 = 10.6\ \mathrm{MeV} . \]

The last root is a slowly varying number near 1 (0.75 for the stripping foil, 1.24 for a whole radiation length of carbon). The radiation length is a property of electrons radiating, yet it is used for protons scattering: the reason is only that both processes are set by the same screened nuclear field, so one table serves both.

Highland’s formula. V. L. Highland (1975) fitted this shape to Molière’s full theory of multiple scattering:

\[ \theta_0 = \frac{13.6\ \mathrm{MeV}}{\beta cp}\,z\sqrt{\frac{x}{X_0}}\left[1 + 0.038\ln\frac{x}{X_0}\right] . \]

The 13.6 MeV absorbs \(10.6\) MeV times a typical value of the last root, and the bracket mimics its slow growth with thickness. G. R. Lynch and O. I. Dahl (1991) moved the charge and speed into the logarithm, \(\ln[xz^2/(X_0\beta^2)]\); this is the form the Particle Data Group gives now. For relativistic particles the two agree; at \(\beta = 0.52\) the second is 8 per cent larger. The fit is good to about 11 per cent for \(10^{-3} < x/X_0 < 100\). Stripping foils lie below that range, at \(10^{-4}\) and less, so foil estimates carry an uncertainty of tens of per cent, which is why section 7 quotes both forms and why foils are tested with beam.

Two cautions in using it. First, \(x\) is the total thickness crossed. Because of the logarithm, \(\theta_0^2\) is not additive: evaluating one crossing and multiplying by \(\sqrt{N_h}\) gives a different (smaller) answer than evaluating \(N_hx\). Second, \(\theta_0\) describes the Gaussian core, about 98 per cent of the particles; the rare single scatters form a halo that a collimator or the aperture eventually removes.

What it does to the beam. A kick changes only \(x'\). Take a particle at \((x, x')\) at the foil, where the Twiss functions are \(\beta_{x,f}\) and \(\alpha_{x,f}\), and give it a random \(\Delta x'\) of mean zero and mean square \(\theta_0^2\). Its action \(J = \tfrac12(\gamma_xx^2 + 2\alpha_xxx' + \beta_xx'^2)\) changes by

\[ \Delta J = (\alpha_{x,f}x + \beta_{x,f}x')\,\Delta x' + \tfrac12\beta_{x,f}\,\Delta x'^2 . \]

Averaged over the beam and over the random kicks, which are independent of where the particle was, the first term vanishes and the second gives \(\tfrac12\beta_{x,f}\theta_0^2\). The rms emittance is the average action, so

\[ \Delta\varepsilon = \tfrac12\beta_{x,f}\,\theta_0^2, \qquad \Delta\varepsilon_n = \tfrac12\beta_{x,f}\,\beta\gamma\,\theta_0^2, \]

in each plane: section 7’s formula. It is the random-kick growth of chapter 18, and it is proportional to \(\beta_{x,f}\), which is why stripping foils sit where the betatron function is small. For the 25 crossings above, Highland’s 0.33 mrad and \(\beta\gamma = 0.608\) give 0.032 µm of normalised emittance per metre of \(\beta_{x,f}\).

Pre-requisite: Thermal radiation and the Stefan-Boltzmann law

Pre-requisite. This section explains how a hot body in a vacuum loses heat: by the light it radiates, at a rate per unit area of \(\epsilon\sigma_{SB}T^4\). It shows where the fourth power comes from, what the emissivity \(\epsilon\) means, and how to find the temperature at which a body heated at a steady rate settles. Section 7 uses it for the stripping foil, which has nothing else to cool it, and “Where it stops” for the limit it sets. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, Statistical Physics, §63 (black-body radiation), with light as particles (F7), the energy scale \(k_BT\) and heat capacity (F6).

Every warm body glows. The atoms of a solid jiggle with thermal energies of about \(k_BT\), and moving charges radiate (F4, section 17). At room temperature the light is far infrared, about 10 µm, and invisible; a stove ring at 1000 K glows dull red; a foil at 1500 K glows orange, though most of its power is still in the infrared, near 1.9 µm. In air a hot body also loses heat by conduction and convection to the gas. In a vacuum there is no gas, and a thin foil held only at its edges conducts little heat to its frame. Radiation is all that is left.

Why \(T^4\). Think of the light inside a closed box whose walls are at temperature \(T\) as a gas of photons (F7, section 4). A typical photon carries an energy of a few \(k_BT\), so its frequency is proportional to \(T\) and its wavelength to \(1/T\). The number of photons that fit in the box goes as the volume divided by the cube of the wavelength, so as \(T^3\). The energy per unit volume is the number times the energy each, so it goes as \(T^3\times T = T^4\). The power that leaves through a small hole, or that a surface in balance with the box gives out, is the energy density times a speed, \(c\), so it also goes as \(T^4\). Planck’s counting of the photons, done exactly, gives the constant: the power radiated per unit area of a perfectly black surface is

\[ \frac{P}{A} = \sigma_{SB}T^4, \qquad \sigma_{SB} = \frac{\pi^2k_B^4}{60\hbar^3c^2} = 5.670\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-4}} , \]

the Stefan-Boltzmann law, with \(T\) in kelvin. The steep power is the point: doubling the temperature multiplies the radiated power by 16.

Emissivity. A real surface radiates less than a black one at the same temperature, by a factor \(\epsilon\) between 0 and 1, its emissivity. (Do not confuse it with the emittance \(\varepsilon\).) A surface that absorbs well also emits well, so dull black graphite has \(\epsilon\) near 0.8 to 0.9, and polished metal 0.05 or less. A body also absorbs the radiation of its surroundings at \(T_w\), so the net loss per unit area is \(\epsilon\sigma_{SB}(T^4 - T_w^4)\); for a foil at 1500 K in a room-temperature chamber, \(T_w^4\) is \((300/1500)^4 = 0.16\) per cent of \(T^4\) and can be dropped.

The balance. A foil of area \(A\) radiates from both faces, \(2A\). If it absorbs a power \(P_{\mathrm{in}}\), its temperature changes as

\[ m c\,\frac{dT}{dt} = P_{\mathrm{in}} - 2\epsilon\sigma_{SB}AT^4 , \]

with \(m\) its mass (kg) and \(c\) its specific heat capacity (J/(kg K)). Under a steady heating it settles where the two sides balance, \(dT/dt = 0\):

\[ T = \left(\frac{P_{\mathrm{in}}/A}{2\epsilon\sigma_{SB}}\right)^{1/4} . \]

Because of the fourth root, the settled temperature is forgiving: doubling the beam power raises it by only \(2^{1/4} = 1.19\). But the foil fails at a fixed temperature, so the beam power it can take grows only as the fourth power of that temperature, and no faster.

At work: the SNS foil. Section 7 finds that each 1 GeV pulse deposits \(5.0\times10^3\) J/m² at the hottest spot of the foil, 60 times a second. On average that is \(P_{\mathrm{in}}/A = 3.0\times10^5\) W/m². With \(\epsilon = 1\) the foil settles at a mean of

\[ T = \left(\frac{3.0\times10^5}{2\times5.670\times10^{-8}}\right)^{1/4} = 1275\ \mathrm{K}, \]

and with \(\epsilon = 0.5\) at 1517 K. The pulses ride on top: each raises the foil by about 750 K, and between pulses the \(T^4\) loss, fastest when the foil is hottest, takes it back down. Stepping the balance equation through the pulses, with each pulse’s heat delivered over 1 ms, gives peaks of 1640 to 1940 K and troughs of 990 to 1250 K over \(\epsilon\) from 1 to 0.5 and \(c\) from 1.8 to 2.1 J/(g K): section 7’s “about 1700 to 2000 K”.

7 Charge-exchange injection

The trick. Liouville’s theorem says that the density of points in phase space is constant along the motion of a system with a Hamiltonian (Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §46; Tong, Classical Mechanics, section 10.2). It describes a fixed set of particles moving under one Hamiltonian. In continuity form, the distribution \(f(x, p, t)\) of protons obeys

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \lbrace f, \mathcal{H}\rbrace = S(x, p, t) \]

where \(\lbrace\,,\rbrace\) is the Poisson bracket (in Tong’s sign convention) and \(S\) is a source. With a septum, the new protons and the old move under the same Hamiltonian from the moment they share an aperture; the only way to bring the new ones near the orbit is through a region the old ones do not occupy. H⁻ ions, hydrogen atoms carrying an extra electron, have the opposite charge to protons. In the field of a chicane, a short row of dipoles that bend the circulating beam out and back again so that it ends on its original line, they bend the other way, so they can be steered along a path that meets the circulating protons at a chosen point, with the same position and angle. A thin foil at that point strips both electrons. Stripping is not part of either Hamiltonian; it is the source term \(S\). Protons are created where protons already are, and the density at that point rises. Liouville’s theorem is not broken; it does not apply across the change of species.

Two details follow from the physics. The proton has the H⁻ ion’s velocity but not its mass. Momentum is \(p = \gamma mv\), and \(\gamma\) and \(v\) are unchanged, so the momentum drops in proportion to the mass, by the fraction \(2m_e/m_{H^-} = 1.09\times10^{-3}\) (the H⁻ ion’s mass is \(m_p + 2m_e\) less a binding energy of a few eV, F7, section 11); a 160 MeV H⁻ beam gives 159.83 MeV protons, and the ring is set for that. The stripped electrons, called convoy electrons because they leave the foil travelling with the beam, have the same velocity: at 160 MeV their kinetic energy is 87 keV and momentum 0.31 MeV/\(c\), a rigidity of 0.001 T m, so they curl up in the nearest field and must be led onto a safe surface.

Stripping efficiency. In carbon, an H⁻ ion loses one electron (becoming H⁰) with cross-section \(\sigma_{-0}\), or both at once with \(\sigma_{-+}\), and H⁰ loses its electron with \(\sigma_{0+}\). For a foil of \(n\) atoms per unit area, the fractions obey \(df_-/dn = -(\sigma_{-0} + \sigma_{-+})f_-\), \(df_0/dn = \sigma_{-0}f_- - \sigma_{0+}f_0\). With \(\sigma_- = \sigma_{-0} + \sigma_{-+}\):

\[ f_- = e^{-\sigma_- n}, \qquad f_0 = \frac{\sigma_{-0}}{\sigma_{0+} - \sigma_-}\left(e^{-\sigma_- n} - e^{-\sigma_{0+} n}\right) \]

At 160 MeV, scaling from measurements at 200 MeV, B. Goddard and colleagues take \(\sigma_{-0} = 17.72\), \(\sigma_{-+} = 0.31\) and \(\sigma_{0+} = 6.92\), all in \(10^{-19}\) cm². A carbon foil of 1 µg/cm² holds \(N_A/(12.011\ \mathrm{g/mol}) \times 10^{-6}\) g \(= 5.01\times10^{16}\) atoms/cm². For the 200 µg/cm² foil of the PS Booster, \(n = 1.00\times10^{19}\) cm\(^{-2}\), which gives \(f_- = 1.4\times10^{-8}\), \(f_0 = 1.55\times10^{-3}\) and 99.85 per cent protons. A 50 µg/cm² foil would let 26 per cent through as H⁰. The H⁰ leaves the ring on a straight line and goes to a dump inside the last chicane magnet, together with the H⁻ that miss the foil (about 1 per cent at the Booster).

What the foil costs. The circulating protons cross the foil again each turn while the injection bump is on. Each crossing scatters them (multiple Coulomb scattering) and costs them energy. For \(N_h\) crossings of a foil of thickness \(x\) and radiation length \(X_0\) (both in g/cm²; \(X_0 = 42.70\) g/cm² for carbon), Highland’s formula for the rms projected scattering angle, in the form Goddard and colleagues use, is

\[ \theta_0 = \frac{13.6\ \mathrm{MeV}}{\beta c p}\sqrt{\frac{N_h x}{X_0}}\left(1 + 0.038\ln\frac{N_h x}{X_0}\right) \]

and the normalised emittance grows by \(\Delta\varepsilon_n = \tfrac12\beta_{x,f}\,\beta\gamma\,\theta_0^2\), with \(\beta_{x,f}\) the betatron function at the foil. For 25 crossings of 200 µg/cm² at 160 MeV (\(p = 570\) MeV/\(c\), \(\beta = 0.520\), \(\beta\gamma = 0.608\)), \(\theta_0 = 0.33\) mrad and \(\Delta\varepsilon_n = 0.032\) µm per metre of \(\beta_{x,f}\). (The Particle Data Group’s current form, after Lynch and Dahl, has \(N_h x/(X_0\beta^2)\) inside the logarithm; it gives 0.35 mrad and 0.037 µm per metre.) Goddard and colleagues found 0.15 to 0.25 µm, against about 2 µm wanted for LHC beams. Hence foils sit at small \(\beta_{x,f}\), and the painting is arranged so that each proton crosses as few times as possible. Each crossing loses \(dE/dx \cdot x = 4.8\ \mathrm{MeV\,cm^2/g} \times 200\ \mu\mathrm{g/cm^2} = 0.96\) keV (the stopping power per unit areal density, from Bethe’s formula of chapter 1), a momentum change of \(3.2\times10^{-6}\), small against a bucket height of \(\pm4\times10^{-3}\). Nuclear scattering removes less than about \(10^{-4}\) of the beam.

Heat. The foil is heated by the protons and by the convoy electrons and cools only by radiation (the Stefan-Boltzmann law, just above). The PS Booster’s foil is expected to rise by 280 K for one injection of \(1.3\times10^{13}\) protons and to settle near 650 K. At the Spallation Neutron Source (SNS) at Oak Ridge, a 1 GeV, 60 Hz linac fills a ring with \(1.5\times10^{14}\) protons in 1 ms, 1.4 MW of beam. Each proton crosses the foil 7 to 10 times, and the peak foil hit density, counting the convoy electrons, is \(4.5\times10^{13}\) per mm² per pulse. A rough model puts real numbers to this. A 1 GeV proton loses 1.98 MeV cm²/g in carbon (Bethe’s formula with mean excitation energy 78 eV), so a 350 µg/cm² foil absorbs 0.69 keV per hit and \(5.0\times10^3\) J/m² per pulse. The foil’s areal mass is 3.5 g/m² and carbon’s heat capacity near 1000 to 2000 °C is about 1.8 to 2.1 J/(g K), so one pulse raises it by about 750 K. Radiating from both faces with emissivity 0.5 to 1 (the balance worked in the pre-requisite above), the foil settles into a cycle with peaks of about 1700 to 2000 K over these ranges of emissivity and heat capacity. Goddard and colleagues note that other machines run foils above 1500 K. Carbon foils fail mainly by sublimation, carbon atoms leaving the solid straight as vapour, which thins the foil and becomes rapid above a threshold temperature, so these numbers are close to a hard limit (Cousineau and colleagues). The SNS uses nanocrystalline diamond foils, 17 × 25 mm and 300 to 350 µg/cm², which curl after about 300 C of injected charge.

Painting. Because the foil can put protons anywhere in phase space, the way a brush puts paint anywhere on a canvas, the injection bump is moved during injection to paint the distribution the next machine wants: a uniform one for low space charge, or a small core for a bright LHC beam. At the PS Booster four kickers outside the injection region make a 35 mm bump that falls during injection, and a chicane of four dipoles (126 mT m each) merges the H⁻ beam, arriving at 66 mrad, with the orbit at the foil. After injection the chicane falls in 5 ms so that the beam stops hitting the foil. The linac’s energy can be varied turn by turn to paint longitudinally as well.

8 Momentum stacking

The CERN Intersecting Storage Rings (ISR), two interleaved rings of 943 m (1.5 PS circumferences), collided protons from 1971. Luminosity needs current, and the ISR built its current in momentum. Each PS pulse of 20 bunches was captured by the ISR’s RF on an injection orbit at \(\delta = -2\) per cent and accelerated, by raising the RF frequency, to a higher momentum orbit near \(+2\) per cent. There the RF was switched off adiabatically, slowly compared with the particles’ synchrotron oscillations so that they follow without being disturbed, and the beam spread round the ring as a coasting band. The next pulse was carried up beside it. A few hundred pulses built a stack across the ring’s wide momentum aperture. S. Myers gives the maximum single-beam current as 57 A. At 26.6 GeV/\(c\) the revolution frequency is \(f_0 = \beta c/C = 317.7\) kHz, so 57 A is \(I/(ef_0) = 1.12\times10^{15}\) protons.

Stacking does not beat Liouville: each pulse occupies a fresh band of momentum. What it uses is the large momentum acceptance. Moving RF buckets through a stack also does something useful. An empty bucket of area \(A_b\) in the \((\phi, \delta)\) plane, moved down through a coasting band that fills \(2\pi\) of phase, cannot carry particles; by area conservation, the band must move up by \(\Delta\delta = A_b/2\pi\) to make room. This “phase displacement” was used to raise ISR stacks from 26.6 to 31.4 GeV/\(c\), and empty buckets moved part-way into a stack left markers visible on Schottky scans (the spectrum of the noise that a beam makes because it is a finite number of particles, whose lines show how the particles are spread in momentum; chapter 16, at the bench).

9 Top-up, off-axis and swap-out injection

Electron storage rings that serve as light sources lose beam steadily through Touschek scattering (two electrons of the same bunch collide and exchange momentum, throwing both outside the ring’s momentum acceptance; chapter 20, section 12) and gas collisions, with lifetimes of hours. In top-up operation the injector adds a little charge every few minutes so the current stays nearly constant, and with it the heat load on beamline optics. If the lifetime is \(\tau\) and the injection interval \(\Delta t \ll \tau\), the current varies by \(\Delta I/I = \Delta t/\tau\): between injections it decays as \(I e^{-t/\tau}\), and \(e^{-\Delta t/\tau} \approx 1 - \Delta t/\tau\) for small \(\Delta t/\tau\) (F1, section 8). That is 0.33 per cent for 2 minutes against 10 hours. The Advanced Photon Source (APS) reported its top-up experience in 1999, and the Swiss Light Source was designed for it and used it from its commissioning in 2001.

Classic top-up is off-axis: four kickers make a closed bump that moves the stored beam close to the septum for one turn, and the new bunch is placed at an amplitude of several millimetres where it damps. Imperfect bumps shake the stored beam, which users see. A nonlinear kicker, developed at BESSY II and since adopted at other rings, avoids this: its conductors are arranged so that the field is zero on the axis and peaks at the injected beam’s offset.

The newest rings have dynamic apertures, the largest amplitudes at which particles stay in the ring for many turns despite the sextupoles’ nonlinear fields (chapter 18, section 11), of only a few millimetres, too small to accept a beam at large amplitude. The APS Upgrade, whose seven-bend achromat lattice (seven bending magnets per cell instead of two, which lowers the emittance as the cube of the bend angle per magnet; chapter 20, section 10) reaches 42 pm rad, uses swap-out: a fast kicker removes a whole depleted bunch to a dump and puts a fresh, full bunch on axis in its place. Commissioning was approved on 10 April 2024. A “decoherence kicker” fires 250 turns before each swap to blow up the outgoing bunch vertically and spare the dump’s surface. Swap-out needs an injector that can make a full bunch in one shot, and kickers fast enough to touch only one bucket.

Pre-requisite (continued): Hamiltonian perturbation theory, resonances, KAM and chaos

Pre-requisite. Chapter 12 introduced resonances, and chapter 18 showed how averaging over turns turns a ring near a resonance into a slow Hamiltonian flow, with islands round the stable fixed points when the tune depends on amplitude (its section 9). This section adds what happens when the tune is swept slowly across a resonance: the islands are born at the centre and grow, and each particle that the growing separatrix reaches is shared out between the core and the islands in proportion to how fast their areas grow. For a fourth-order resonance with the right ratio of driving to detuning, that gives five equal beamlets. Section 10 uses it for multi-turn extraction. Taught from Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, sections VII.2 and VII.3 (higher-order resonances and the detuning from octupoles); the Handbook of Accelerator Physics and Engineering, section 4.10 (multi-turn extraction); and the adiabatic invariant of chapter 7.

The Hamiltonian. Take the horizontal tune near \(Q_x = 25/4\), the resonance of section 10. Seen once a turn, a particle advances by 6.25 revolutions of betatron phase; whole revolutions are invisible, so in a frame that turns a quarter of a revolution each turn it moves slowly, and chapter 18’s averaging gives a Hamiltonian in the action \(J\) (m) and the slow angle \(\varphi\), with the turn number \(N\) as the clock:

\[ H(J, \varphi) = \delta J + \tfrac12\alpha J^2 + GJ^2\cos4\varphi = \delta J + \tfrac12A(\varphi)J^2, \qquad A(\varphi) = \alpha + 2G\cos4\varphi . \]

Here \(\delta = 2\pi(Q_0 - 25/4)\) (rad per turn) is the distance of the small-amplitude tune \(Q_0\) from the resonance; \(\alpha\) (rad per turn per m of action) is the detuning, which makes the tune rise with amplitude, \(Q_x(J) = Q_0 + \alpha J/2\pi\), and comes from octupoles (magnets with eight poles, whose kick grows as the cube of the displacement, chapter 7); and \(G\) (same units) is the strength of the resonant driving term, which comes from octupoles directly and from sextupoles at second order. Take \(\alpha > 2G > 0\). Hamilton’s equations are \(d\varphi/dN = \partial H/\partial J = \delta + A(\varphi)J\) and \(dJ/dN = -\partial H/\partial\varphi = 4GJ^2\sin4\varphi\).

Fixed points. \(dJ/dN = 0\) needs \(\sin4\varphi = 0\), so \(\cos4\varphi = \pm1\): eight directions \(45°\) apart. \(d\varphi/dN = 0\) then needs \(J = -\delta/A(\varphi)\), which is positive only when \(\delta < 0\). So:

  • For \(\delta > 0\) there is no fixed point except the centre. Every particle circles, faster the larger its action. This is the beam before the sweep.
  • For \(\delta < 0\) there are four stable fixed points where \(\cos4\varphi = -1\), at \(J_s = |\delta|/(\alpha - 2G)\), and four unstable ones where \(\cos4\varphi = +1\), at \(J_u = |\delta|/(\alpha + 2G)\). At the first kind \(H\) is a minimum in both directions, at the second a saddle (chapter 18’s rule: the shape of \(H\) decides). Both move outward in proportion to \(|\delta|\).

The separatrix and its areas. The level curve through the saddles, \(H = -\delta^2/[2(\alpha + 2G)]\), is a quadratic in \(J\) at each angle. Its two roots are

\[ J_\pm(\varphi) = \frac{|\delta|}{A(\varphi)}\left(1 \pm \sqrt{\frac{2G\,(1 - \cos4\varphi)}{\alpha + 2G}}\right). \]

The inner curve \(J_-\) bounds the core round the centre; between \(J_-\) and \(J_+\) lie four islands, pinched together at the saddles where the square root vanishes. Since \(dX\,dP = dJ\,d\varphi\) (the change to action and angle keeps area), the areas in the phase plane are

\[ A_{\mathrm{core}} = \int_0^{2\pi}J_-\,d\varphi, \qquad A_{\mathrm{isl}} = \int_0^{2\pi}(J_+ - J_-)\,d\varphi . \]

Both are \(|\delta|/\alpha\) times a number that depends only on \(G/\alpha\). Evaluated numerically:

\(G/\alpha\) \(A_{\mathrm{core}}\,\alpha/\lvert\delta\rvert\) \(A_{\mathrm{isl}}\,\alpha/\lvert\delta\rvert\) islands’ share each island
0.1 3.90 5.03 56 % 14.1 %
0.2 3.11 7.48 71 % 17.7 %
0.3 2.62 10.47 80 % 20.0 %
0.4 2.27 16.41 88 % 22.0 %

Sweeping the tune. Start with \(\delta > 0\) and lower it slowly through zero. A particle of action \(J_0\) circles on a curve enclosing the area \(2\pi J_0\). While the change is slow compared with its circling, that area is an adiabatic invariant. The islands appear at the centre and the region inside the outer separatrix grows as \((A_{\mathrm{core}} + A_{\mathrm{isl}}) \propto |\delta|\). When it reaches \(2\pi J_0\), the separatrix arrives at the particle. Where does the particle go?

Liouville’s theorem answers. Each region bounded by the separatrix can grow only by phase points crossing into it, because the flow neither creates nor compresses area. So the area flowing into the core per turn is \(dA_{\mathrm{core}}/dN\), and into the islands \(dA_{\mathrm{isl}}/dN\). Particles meeting the separatrix arrive at all phases of their circling, so they are carried along with the area: the chance of going into the islands is

\[ P_{\mathrm{isl}} = \frac{dA_{\mathrm{isl}}/dN}{dA_{\mathrm{core}}/dN + dA_{\mathrm{isl}}/dN} = \frac{A_{\mathrm{isl}}}{A_{\mathrm{core}} + A_{\mathrm{isl}}}, \]

the last step because both areas grow in proportion to \(|\delta|\). The share does not depend on \(J_0\), nor on how the beam was distributed. Once inside, a particle circles its island centre and keeps that smaller area while the island moves out. For \(G/\alpha = 0.3\) the islands take 80 per cent, 20 per cent each by symmetry, and the core 20 per cent: five equal beamlets, the nominal split of section 10.

Checked by tracking. Hamilton’s equations for \(G/\alpha = 0.3\) were integrated for 2000 particles of a Gaussian beam while \(\delta\) fell steadily from \(+0.5\alpha\) to \(-6\alpha\) (in units with the beam’s rms action equal to 1). With a slow sweep the core kept 22 per cent and the islands 19.6, 18.7, 19.5 and 20.3 per cent, within the statistical scatter of about 1 per cent. A sweep 100 times faster left 37 per cent in the core: the sharing by area holds only when the sweep is slow.

Numbers for a ring. Take \(\alpha = 1000\) rad per turn per m, so a particle with \(J = 4.5\) µm has its tune raised by \(\alpha J/2\pi = 7.2\times10^{-4}\), and \(G = 300\). The whole beam, out to \(J_0 = 4.5\) µm, has crossed the separatrix once \(13.09|\delta|/\alpha = 2\pi J_0\), that is at \(|\delta| = 2.16\times10^{-3}\), a tune \(3.4\times10^{-4}\) below 6.25. Then \(J_u = 1.35\) µm and the island centres sit at \(J_s = |\delta|/400 = 5.4\) µm. Moving the tune further, to \(|\delta| = 8\times10^{-3}\), carries the centres out to \(J_s = 20\) µm, which at \(\beta_x = 20\) m is \(\sqrt{2J_s\beta_x} = 28\) mm from the axis, with the trapped particles still round them: the beamlets are separated and ready for the kicker and septum.

Exercise IS1. Using the table, what share of the beam ends in each island and in the core when \(G/\alpha = 0.2\)? Why can a machine not simply make \(G\) as large as possible?

Answer

Each island holds 17.7 per cent and the core \(3.11/(3.11 + 7.48) = 29\) per cent. As \(2G\) approaches \(\alpha\), \(A(\varphi) = \alpha - 2G\) at the island centres goes to zero and \(J_s = |\delta|/(\alpha - 2G)\) runs away: the islands become huge and the outer separatrix no longer closes, so particles are lost instead of trapped. Exercise IS2. Show that \(H = \delta J + \tfrac12A J^2\) at fixed \(\varphi\) has its minimum at \(J = -\delta/A\) with value \(-\delta^2/2A\), and use it to explain why the saddles lie where \(\cos4\varphi = +1\).

Answer

\(dH/dJ = \delta + AJ = 0\) at \(J = -\delta/A\), where \(H = -\delta^2/A + \delta^2/2A = -\delta^2/2A\). Along the ring of fixed points this minimum value is lowest where \(A\) is smallest, at \(\cos4\varphi = -1\), and highest where \(A\) is largest, at \(\cos4\varphi = +1\). So along \(\varphi\) the fixed points at \(\cos4\varphi = +1\) are maxima of \(H\) while along \(J\) they are minima: a saddle. Those at \(\cos4\varphi = -1\) are minima both ways: island centres.

10 Fast extraction and the boxcar

A fast single-turn extraction is injection in reverse: a bump brings the beam near the septum, the kicker rises in a gap left empty in the bunch train, the whole beam is deflected in one turn, and the septum bends it into the transfer line. Particles in the gap while the kicker rises are swept across the septum and lost. Filling a large ring with several fast extractions from a smaller one is “boxcar stacking”: each batch is placed after the last, and the gaps between batches must be as long as the kicker rise times. In the nominal scheme the LHC is filled by 12 injections from the SPS per ring.

Continuous transfer. The SPS is 11 PS circumferences round. In 1973 CERN proposed to fill it for fixed-target physics by extracting the PS beam over five turns, twice. A fast bump pushed the beam onto an electrostatic septum which sliced off a fifth of the beam on each of five turns, with the tune (fractional part 0.25) rotating a new slice towards the septum each turn. At 14 GeV/\(c\) a PS turn is 2.10 µs, so five turns make a train of 10.5 µs, and two such trains fill 21.0 µs of the SPS’s 23.1 µs turn, leaving gaps of about 1 µs for the SPS kickers. Continuous transfer lost about 15 per cent of the beam around the PS and gave slices of different emittance and trajectory.

Multi-turn extraction with islands. R. Cappi and M. Giovannozzi proposed in 2001 and published in 2002 a way to split the beam without a blade. Sextupoles and octupoles create four stable islands around the core near the fourth-order resonance \(4Q_x = 25\). The tune is moved slowly across the resonance and particles are trapped in the islands. The islands are then moved outward by changing the nonlinear fields. A kicker bump pushes one island across the septum on each of four turns, and the core goes on the fifth. Each beamlet holds a nominal 20 per cent of the beam, with similar emittance and optics. After about a month of operation in 2010 two problems remained: fluctuations of the splitting, traced to 5 kHz ripple from the power converters of the pole-face windings (coils laid on the faces of the PS’s main magnet poles, which trim the tunes and chromaticity), and activation of the magnetic septum. A passive “dummy septum” installed in 2013 to 2014 took the unavoidable losses during the kicker rise into a shielded section. Multi-turn extraction has fed the SPS fixed-target programme since September 2015.

Pre-requisite (continued): Fourier series and transforms

Pre-requisite. Chapter 8 split a signal that repeats every revolution into lines at the harmonics of the revolution frequency, and chapter 19 showed how modulating a carrier in amplitude or frequency puts sidebands beside it. This section applies both to a beam oscillating sideways: why its signal at a pickup has lines at \((n \pm q)f_0\), the betatron sidebands, which are the frequencies at which a transverse field can drive it; how a frequency-modulated drive covers a spread of tunes; and why the mains ripple of a power converter appears in the spill at 50, 100 and 150 Hz, with the higher harmonics weighted more. Section 11 uses all three, for RF knock-out and for ripple. Taught from Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, section III.7 A (the transverse spectrum of a particle) and appendix B, section I; Penrose, The Road to Reality, §9.1 (Fourier series); and the Parseval theorem of chapter 8.

One particle at a pickup. A particle of charge \(e\) passes a pickup once a turn, at times \(t_k = kT_0\), with \(T_0 = 1/f_0\) the revolution period. Its current there is a train of short pulses, \(i(t) = e\sum_k\delta(t - kT_0)\), with \(\delta(t)\) the Dirac delta, a pulse of unit area too short to resolve (chapter 8, exercise D1). Chapter 8’s Fourier series of a train of equal pulses has equal lines at every harmonic:

\[ i(t) = \frac{e}{T_0}\left(1 + 2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\cos n\omega_0t\right), \qquad \omega_0 = 2\pi f_0 . \]

A position pickup measures the charge times the displacement, \(d(t) = e\sum_kx_k\,\delta(t - kT_0)\). On turn \(k\) the displacement is \(x_k = a\cos(2\pi Qk + \phi_0)\), with \(a\) the amplitude at the pickup and \(Q\) the tune. Since only the values at \(t = kT_0\) matter, write \(x_k\) as the smooth function \(x(t) = a\cos(Q\omega_0t + \phi_0)\) sampled at those times. Then \(d(t) = x(t)\,i(t)\), and the product-to-sum rule \(\cos A\cos B = \tfrac12[\cos(A + B) + \cos(A - B)]\) turns each harmonic into two lines:

\[ d(t) = \frac{ea}{T_0}\left[\cos(Q\omega_0t + \phi_0) + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Big(\cos\big((n + Q)\omega_0t + \phi_0\big) + \cos\big((n - Q)\omega_0t - \phi_0\big)\Big)\right] . \]

The lines are at \(|n \pm Q|f_0\) for all integers \(n\). Write \(Q = Q_{\mathrm{int}} + q\), with \(q\) the fractional part. Shifting \(n\) by \(Q_{\mathrm{int}}\) shows that the same set of lines is \((m \pm q)f_0\) for integers \(m\): each revolution harmonic \(mf_0\) has a lower sideband at \((m - q)f_0\) and an upper one at \((m + q)f_0\). The integer part of the tune is invisible, as in chapter 11’s “what one detector sees”. This is chapter 19’s amplitude modulation: the betatron oscillation modulates the amplitude of each line of the revolution comb.

At work. In the SPS, \(f_0 = 43.35\) kHz. With an example tune \(Q_x = 26.62\), \(q = 0.62\), and the lines below 120 kHz are at \(0.38f_0 = 16.5\), \(0.62f_0 = 26.9\), \(1.38f_0 = 59.8\), \(1.62f_0 = 70.2\), \(2.38f_0 = 103.2\) and \(2.62f_0 = 113.6\) kHz.

Many particles. A beam whose particles have tunes spread over a band \(\Delta q\) (from chromaticity with momentum spread, or from amplitude) turns each line into a band \(\Delta q\,f_0\) wide, the same width at every \(m\) if the revolution frequencies are equal. In a ring with \(f_0 = 1.5\) MHz, a tune spread of 0.003 spreads each sideband over 4.5 kHz.

Driving at a sideband. Reverse the reasoning. A transverse field at the pickup’s place that oscillates at frequency \(f\) is felt only at the passages, so the particle sees it at the phases \(2\pi fkT_0\), that is it sees \(f/f_0\) modulo 1. The next section shows that the kicks add up when this matches the betatron phase advance, which is exactly when \(f\) lies on one of the lines \((m \pm q)f_0\). The lines a pickup sees are the frequencies at which a kicker can drive; one can choose whichever line suits the amplifier.

Frequency modulation. To drive a whole band of tunes, sweep the drive’s frequency over the band, \(f(t) = f_c + \tfrac12\Delta f\,s(t)\), with \(s(t)\) running between \(-1\) and \(+1\) at a modulation frequency \(f_m\). When \(\Delta f \gg f_m\), chapter 19’s spectrum of many Bessel-weighted lines spaced \(f_m\) apart fills the band \(\Delta f\), and the simple picture holds: the drive’s instantaneous frequency passes each particle’s sideband twice a period. HIMAC’s modulation over 5 kHz, in a ring with an example \(f_0\) of 1.5 MHz, covers \(\Delta q = 5/1500 = 0.0033\).

Ripple in the spill. A power converter fed from the 50 Hz grid has a ripple that repeats every 20 ms, so it is a Fourier series with lines at 50, 100, 150 Hz and so on. Let it reduce the distance to resonance by \(\delta Q_r(t) = \sum_n\delta Q_n\sin(n\omega t + \psi_n)\), with \(\omega = 2\pi\times50\) Hz. Section 11’s spill rate is proportional to the speed at which the triangle shrinks, \(\dot Q + d(\delta Q_r)/dt\), as long as that stays positive. Differentiating a Fourier series multiplies line \(n\) by \(n\omega\), so the spill is

\[ \text{rate} \propto 1 + \sum_nm_n\cos(n\omega t + \psi_n), \qquad m_n = \frac{n\omega\,\delta Q_n}{\dot Q} . \]

The higher harmonics are weighted by \(n\): a 150 Hz ripple modulates the spill three times as much as a 50 Hz ripple of the same size. The effective spill length quoted under “At the bench”, \((\int f\,dt)^2/\int f^2\,dt\), divided by the spill’s duration is the ratio of the squared mean to the mean square of the rate. By Parseval’s theorem the mean square of the bracket is \(1 + \tfrac12\sum_nm_n^2\), so

\[ \text{duty factor} = \frac{1}{1 + \tfrac12\sum_nm_n^2} . \]

At work. Bryant’s medical ring sweeps \(\dot Q = 0.01\) per second. A 50 Hz ripple of \(10^{-5}\) in tune gives \(m_1 = 2\pi\times50\times10^{-5}/0.01 = 0.31\) and a duty factor of 0.95; adding a 150 Hz ripple of half that size, \(m_3 = 0.47\), lowers it to 0.86.

Exercise FT1. A pickup in the SPS shows betatron lines at 16.5 and 26.9 kHz. Show that a tune of 26.38 would give exactly the same lines as 26.62. How could one tell them apart?

Answer

With \(q = 0.38\) the lines are \((m \pm 0.38)f_0\): \(0.38f_0 = 16.5\), \(0.62f_0 = 26.9\) (from \(m = 1\), lower), and so on, the same set as for \(q = 0.62\). Changing a quadrupole slightly and watching which way the lines move tells them apart, or comparing the phase at two pickups a known phase advance apart.

Pre-requisite (continued): Driven oscillator, resonance and quality factor

Pre-requisite. Chapter 7 drove an oscillator with a periodic force and found that energy is gained every cycle only when the drive keeps step with the motion, at the natural frequency. This section does the same for a betatron oscillation driven by a kicker that the particle meets once a turn. It shows that the kicks add up, and the amplitude grows in proportion to the number of turns, exactly when the drive frequency sits on a betatron sideband \((m \pm q)f_0\), and that a small mistuning turns the growth into a beat. Section 11 uses it for RF knock-out. Taught from Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, section III.3 C (the effect of an RF dipole field, RF knock-out); Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §22 (forced oscillations, and the beat near resonance).

The map with a kick. Use the normalised coordinates \((X, P)\) of section 3 at the kicker, where the betatron function is \(\beta_{x,k}\). On each passage the kicker changes the angle by \(\theta\cos(2\pi\nu k + \psi)\), where \(k\) counts turns, \(\nu = f/f_0\) is the drive frequency in units of the revolution frequency, and \(\theta\) (rad) is the peak kick. In normalised coordinates that is a change of \(P\) by

\[ \Delta P_k = A\cos(2\pi\nu k + \psi), \qquad A = \sqrt{\beta_{x,k}}\,\theta \quad (\mathrm{m^{1/2}}). \]

Between kicks the particle rotates by \(2\pi Q\). A rotation is simplest as a complex number (F5, sections 1 and 2: multiplying by \(e^{i\mu} = \cos\mu + i\sin\mu\) turns a number by the angle \(\mu\) without changing its size). Put \(z = X - iP\); section 3’s rotation turns \((X, P)\) into \((X\cos\mu + P\sin\mu,\ -X\sin\mu + P\cos\mu)\), which is \(z \to z\,e^{i\mu}\). The amplitude is \(a = |z| = \sqrt{2J}\). A kick changes \(z\) by \(-i\Delta P\).

Adding up the kicks. After \(N\) turns (kick, then rotate, each turn) the kick of turn \(k\) has been rotated \(N - k\) times:

\[ z_N = e^{i2\pi QN}\left(z_0 - i\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\Delta P_k\,e^{-i2\pi Qk}\right). \]

The factor in front is the free rotation and does not change \(|z|\); the bracket is the slowly changing amplitude. Write the cosine as two exponentials, \(\Delta P_k = \tfrac12A\left[e^{i(2\pi\nu k + \psi)} + e^{-i(2\pi\nu k + \psi)}\right]\). The sum then holds terms \(e^{i2\pi(\nu - Q)k}\) and \(e^{-i2\pi(\nu + Q)k}\).

  • If \(\nu - Q\) is an integer, the first term is 1 on every turn, since \(e^{i2\pi\times\text{integer}} = 1\). If \(\nu + Q\) is an integer, the second is. In either case the kicks add in step, the other term averages away, and the bracket grows by \(\tfrac12A\) each turn. From rest, \(a_N = \tfrac12NA\): the amplitude grows linearly, without limit, as an undamped oscillator driven at resonance does.
  • \(\nu - Q\) or \(\nu + Q\) an integer means \(\nu = m \pm q\) for some integer \(m\): the drive frequency lies on a betatron sideband, \(f = (m \pm q)f_0\), the lines that a pickup sees (the previous section). Any of them will do.
  • Off resonance by \(\Delta\nu\), the steps turn by \(2\pi\Delta\nu\) each turn, and the sum is a geometric series. With \(w = e^{i2\pi\Delta\nu}\), \(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}w^k = (w^N - 1)/(w - 1)\), and taking out half the angle from top and bottom, \(e^{i\theta} - 1 = e^{i\theta/2}\,2i\sin(\theta/2)\), so \(|\sum_ke^{i2\pi\Delta\nu k}| = |\sin(\pi N\Delta\nu)/\sin(\pi\Delta\nu)|\). The amplitude rises, falls back to zero after \(1/\Delta\nu\) turns, and never exceeds \(A/(2|\sin\pi\Delta\nu|) \approx A/(2\pi|\Delta\nu|)\). This is the beat of an oscillator driven near, but not at, its frequency (Landau and Lifshitz, §22).

So a drive held for \(N\) turns acts strongly on tunes within about \(1/N\) of its own and hardly at all on the rest, the turn-by-turn version of a resonance whose width is set by the time the drive lasts.

At work. Take \(\beta_{x,k} = 10\) m and \(\theta = 1\) µrad, so \(A = 3.16\times10^{-6}\) m\(^{1/2}\), and a tune \(Q = 6.62\). Tracking the kick-and-rotate map from rest with \(\nu = 0.62\), \(0.38\) or \(1.38\) gives \(a = 1.58\times10^{-3}\) m\(^{1/2}\) after 1000 turns and \(3.16\times10^{-3}\) after 2000, exactly \(\tfrac12NA\); in real space, \(a\sqrt{\beta_x}\) is 5 mm and then 10 mm at a point where \(\beta_x = 10\) m. A microradian a turn, added in step, takes a particle across a vacuum chamber in a few thousand turns, a few milliseconds. With \(\nu = 0.63\), mistuned by 0.01, the amplitude never exceeds \(5.0\times10^{-5}\) m\(^{1/2}\), 0.16 mm, and returns to zero every 100 turns.

Exercise DO1. With the numbers above, how many turns does it take to drive a particle from rest to the 25 mm of a septum at \(\beta_x = 10\) m? How long is that in a ring with \(f_0 = 1.5\) MHz?

Answer

\(a = 0.025/\sqrt{10} = 7.9\times10^{-3}\) m\(^{1/2}\), and \(N = 2a/A = 2\times7.9\times10^{-3}/3.16\times10^{-6} = 5000\) turns, 3.3 ms.

Exercise DO2. A particle starts at rest and is driven with \(\nu\) mistuned by \(\Delta\nu = 0.005\) from its sideband. What is its largest amplitude, in units of \(A\), and after how many turns is it reached?

Answer

\(|\sin(\pi N\Delta\nu)|\) is largest, 1, at \(N = 1/(2\Delta\nu) = 100\) turns, where the amplitude is \(A/(2\sin(\pi\times0.005)) = A/(2\times0.0157) = 31.8A\). At exact resonance 100 turns would give \(50A\); the mistuning costs a third.

Exercise DO3. Why does a drive at \(\nu = 0.38\) act on a particle of tune 6.62 even though \(0.38\) is not its fractional tune?

Answer

\(\nu + Q = 7.00\), an integer, so the second exponential, \(e^{-i2\pi(\nu + Q)k}\), is 1 on every turn. Seen once a turn, the two drives cannot be told apart: \(\cos(2\pi\times0.38k + \psi) = \cos(2\pi\times0.62k - \psi)\) for every integer \(k\), because the two phases add to a whole number of turns, \(2\pi k\). So a drive at \(0.38f_0\), the lower sideband of \(f_0\), kicks the particle exactly as a drive at \(0.62f_0\) would, in step at every passage.

Pre-requisite (continued): Diffusion and Fokker-Planck

Pre-requisite. Chapter 3 showed that a random walk spreads as the square root of the number of steps, and chapter 18 applied it to the betatron action under random kicks. This section shows how a drive that is perfectly regular can still produce such a random walk: when its frequency is swept across a beam with a spread of tunes, each particle is in step with it only during brief passages, and the passages leave steps of fixed size and random direction. The coherent growth of the previous section, amplitude in proportion to time, becomes diffusion, amplitude squared in proportion to time. Section 11 uses it for RF knock-out. Taught from Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, sections III.3 C (RF knock-out) and III.9 (emittance growth from random kicks), and Tong, Fluid Mechanics, the opening of chapter 7 (diffusion as a random walk).

One passage through resonance. Let the drive’s frequency rise steadily, \(\nu(k) = \nu_c + \dot\nu\,(k - k_c)\), with \(\dot\nu\) the change of \(\nu\) per turn, and let it cross a particle’s sideband at turn \(k_c\). In the sum of the previous section the step on turn \(k\) then turns by an angle that grows quadratically away from \(k_c\): the drive’s phase is \(2\pi\int\nu\,dk\), so the mismatch of phase is \(\pi\dot\nu(k - k_c)^2\) plus a constant. Near \(k_c\) the steps point the same way and add; far from it they wind round and cancel. The sum over the passage is a Fresnel integral, an integral of an oscillating exponential whose phase grows as the square of the variable. It is the Gaussian integral \(\int e^{-au^2}du = \sqrt{\pi/a}\) with the imaginary \(a = -i\pi\dot\nu\), which gives \(\sqrt{i/\dot\nu} = e^{i\pi/4}/\sqrt{\dot\nu}\) (the same step as in chapter 12’s crossing of a resonance); the sum may be replaced by the integral because the phase changes little from one turn to the next near \(k_c\):

\[ \sum_ke^{i\pi\dot\nu(k - k_c)^2} \approx \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\pi\dot\nu u^2}\,du = \frac{e^{i\pi/4}}{\sqrt{\dot\nu}} , \]

so one passage changes the complex amplitude \(z\) by a step of size

\[ |\Delta z| = \frac{A}{2}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{\dot\nu}} : \]

the effect of \(1/\sqrt{\dot\nu}\) turns of exact resonance. The step’s direction depends on the phase of the drive relative to the particle’s oscillation at the moment of crossing.

Many passages. A drive frequency-modulated over a band passes each particle’s sideband twice in every modulation period. The particle’s betatron phase at each crossing has no fixed relation to the drive’s, because the tunes differ from particle to particle and the crossings fall at times that are not commensurate with either period. So the steps point in random directions. By chapter 3’s random walk, steps of fixed size \(s\) in random directions add in mean square: after \(N_p\) passages

\[ \langle a^2\rangle = a_0^2 + N_p\,s^2, \qquad s = \frac{A}{2\sqrt{\dot\nu}} . \]

With \(a^2 = 2J\), the mean action grows linearly in time, at the rate \(\tfrac12s^2\) per passage: a diffusion in \(J\), which chapter 18’s Fokker-Planck equation describes, with particles reaching the separatrix one by one, those of large action first.

Checked by tracking. With \(A = 3.16\times10^{-6}\) m\(^{1/2}\) and a tune of 6.62, single passages at \(\dot\nu = 10^{-6}\), \(4\times10^{-6}\) and \(1.6\times10^{-5}\) per turn left amplitudes within 1, 2 and 5 per cent of \(A/(2\sqrt{\dot\nu})\), averaged over the starting phase. Fifty passages at \(\dot\nu = 4\times10^{-6}\), with a random phase at each, gave \(\langle a^2\rangle = 3.3\times10^{-5}\) m against \(N_ps^2 = 3.1\times10^{-5}\) m, averaged over 40 particles.

At work: a knock-out drive. Take an example ring with \(f_0 = 1.5\) MHz and a drive swept in a triangle over 5 kHz (\(\Delta\nu = 0.0033\)) at \(f_m = 1\) kHz. Then \(\dot\nu = 2\Delta\nu f_m/f_0 = 4.4\times10^{-6}\) per turn, and each passage acts like \(1/\sqrt{\dot\nu} = 470\) turns of exact resonance. With a kick of 0.1 µrad at \(\beta_{x,k} = 10\) m, \(A = 3.16\times10^{-7}\) m\(^{1/2}\) and \(s = 7.5\times10^{-5}\) m\(^{1/2}\). At 2000 passages a second the mean action grows by \(2000\times\tfrac12s^2 = 5.6\) µm per second. A beam of a few micrometres is walked out to a separatrix in about a second, smoothly, and the walk stops the moment the drive is switched off.

Exercise DI1. In the example, the modulation frequency is doubled to 2 kHz with the band unchanged. How do the step size, the number of passages per second, and the rate of growth of \(\langle J\rangle\) change?

Answer

\(\dot\nu\) doubles, so \(s \propto 1/\sqrt{\dot\nu}\) falls by \(\sqrt2\) and \(s^2\) halves; the passages per second double. The rate, passages times \(\tfrac12s^2\), is unchanged at 5.6 µm per second. The rate depends on the band and the drive strength, \(\propto A^2f_0/\Delta\nu\), not on how fast the band is swept, as long as the passages stay random.

Exercise DI2. Compare the coherent drive with the diffusive one. A particle exactly on resonance with the 0.1 µrad drive, held fixed, would reach \(J = 5\) µm after how many turns? How long does diffusion take, on average, at 5.6 µm per second?

Answer

\(a = \sqrt{2\times5\times10^{-6}} = 3.16\times10^{-3}\) m\(^{1/2}\), and \(N = 2a/A = 2\times3.16\times10^{-3}/3.16\times10^{-7} = 2\times10^4\) turns, 13 ms. Diffusion takes about 0.9 s. The swept drive is far less efficient for one particle, but it treats every particle in the band alike, which is what a spill needs.

11 Slow extraction on the third-integer resonance

The map. Let the horizontal tune be \(Q_x = p/3 + \Delta Q\) with \(p\) an integer not divisible by 3 and \(|\Delta Q| \ll 1/3\). Place one thin sextupole of integrated strength \(k_2\ell\) (m\(^{-2}\)), with \(k_2 = -(1/B\rho)\,\partial^2 B_y/\partial x^2\), at a point where the betatron function is \(\beta_x\). On the mid-plane its field is \(B_y = \tfrac12(\partial^2 B_y/\partial x^2)\,x^2\), and the force on a positive particle is \(-qvB_y\) in \(x\) (the same sign that makes a quadrupole with \(k > 0\) focus), so it kicks \(\Delta x' = -(\ell/B\rho)B_y = \tfrac12 k_2\ell\,x^2\). The minus sign in \(k_2\) is there only to keep the kick free of one; reversing the sextupole turns the triangle below through 180°. In normalised coordinates the kick is

\[ \Delta P = S X^2, \qquad S = \tfrac12\,\beta_x^{3/2}\,k_2\ell \quad (\mathrm{m^{-1/2}}) \]

Without the sextupole, three turns rotate a particle by \(3 \times 2\pi Q_x = 2\pi p + 6\pi\Delta Q\), almost the identity. So over three turns a particle moves only a little, and the changes can be treated as a slow flow in a time unit of three turns. Adding the three sextupole kicks, seen at phases 0, \(2\pi/3\) and \(4\pi/3\), and keeping terms to first order in \(\kappa = 6\pi\Delta Q\) and in \(S\), Bryant’s lecture (sections 7 and 8) derives the changes per three turns and shows they follow from the Kobayashi Hamiltonian:

\[ \mathcal{H}_K = \frac{\kappa}{2}\left(X^2 + P^2\right) + \frac{S}{4}\left(3XP^2 - X^3\right) \]

The first term is the slow rotation; the second has threefold symmetry. When \(\mathcal{H}_K = (2\kappa/3)^3/S^2\), it factorises into three straight lines, the separatrices (shown in the pre-requisite on Hamiltonians and drawn in Figure 21.1). They bound a stable triangle whose inscribed circle has radius

\[ h = \frac{2\kappa}{3S} = \frac{4\pi\,\Delta Q}{S} \]

The triangle has its corners at \(2h\) from the centre, and its area, the acceptance at this tune, is

\[ A = 3\sqrt3\,h^2 = 48\sqrt3\,\pi^2\,\frac{\Delta Q^2}{S^2} \]

Particles inside circulate stably on rounded triangles. Particles outside move out along the separatrices, jumping between the three arms on successive turns, and gain amplitude faster and faster. Far from the triangle the cubic term dominates. Bryant’s equations of motion, \(\Delta X_3 = \kappa P + \tfrac32 SXP\) and \(\Delta P_3 = -\kappa X + \tfrac34 S(X^2 - P^2)\) per three turns, then move a particle on an outgoing arm, at distance \(r\) from the centre, outward along the arm by \(\Delta r_3 \approx \tfrac34 S r^2\). At the sextupole the arms are not along \(X\): two of the outgoing arms lie at 30° to the \(X\) axis (the third is parallel to the \(P\) axis), and on the one in \(X > 0\), where \(P \approx X/\sqrt3\), the step in \(X\) is \(\tfrac32 S X\cdot X/\sqrt3 = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}SX^2\) and in \(P\) is \(\tfrac34 S(X^2 - X^2/3) = \tfrac12 SX^2\), so the step along the arm is \(SX^2\), which with \(X = r\sqrt3/2\) is the \(\tfrac34 Sr^2\) above (\(\kappa\) neglected). This \(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}SX^2\) is the step in \(X\) at the sextupole itself. Phase space in normalised coordinates only rotates from one point to another, so at a septum placed where the outgoing arm lies along the \(X\) axis, the step across the septum, the spiral step, is

\[ \Delta X_3 \approx \tfrac34\,S X^2, \qquad \text{or} \qquad \Delta x_3 \approx \tfrac34\,\frac{S}{\sqrt{\beta_{x,e}}}\,x^2 = \tfrac38\,\beta_x\,k_2\ell\,x^2 \]

in real space, with \(x\) the amplitude at the septum and \(\beta_{x,e}\) its betatron function; the last form is for \(\beta_{x,e}\) equal to the sextupole’s \(\beta_x\). Tracking a thin-sextupole map confirms both: at \(\Delta Q = 0.005\) the stable area found is 93 per cent of the formula, and the step along a separatrix matches \(\tfrac34 Sr^2\) to within a few per cent while the step is small compared with \(r\) and \(r\) is several times \(h\). The formulas are first order; real designs are checked by tracking. Resonances in general are the subject of chapter 18.

The extraction. As \(\Delta Q \to 0\) the triangle shrinks to a point. Bring the beam towards the resonance slowly and the particles at the largest amplitude become unstable first, then the smaller ones, until all have gone. An electrostatic septum is placed where the outgoing separatrix crosses it, at an amplitude where the spiral step is about 10 mm. The septum wires stop the particles that land on them; if arrivals are spread evenly across the step, the fraction lost is the effective septum thickness divided by the step: 0.1 mm over 10 mm is 1 per cent.

Two ways to drive the beam in. Move the tune with a quadrupole (the SPS’s historical “Q-sweep”), or keep the optics fixed and move the beam: accelerate it with a betatron core (an iron core threading the ring whose rising flux gives every particle a few volts a turn, the betatron’s principle of chapter 11), or change its momentum, so that with large chromaticity (tune that depends on momentum, chapter 16, section 12) each momentum reaches resonance in turn. C. Steinbach’s diagram shows this: plot amplitude against momentum (or tune); the waiting beam is a band, the resonance a sloping line, and the spill whatever crosses the line. Particles of different momenta leave on separatrices of different angles at the septum, unless the dispersion there is chosen to satisfy the Hardt condition, which makes all the separatrices meet at the septum with the same angle. (The separatrices of different momenta differ because the tune, and so the triangle, depends on momentum through the chromaticity, while the dispersion moves each momentum’s closed orbit sideways; the Hardt condition chooses the dispersion so that the two effects cancel at the septum.) At the SPS, Constant Optics Slow Extraction, which keeps the normalised optics constant while ramping the whole beam’s momentum, has been used since the 2018 run.

Ripple. The spill rate is the density of beam crossing into resonance times the speed of crossing. Write the distance to resonance as \(\Delta Q(t) = \Delta Q_0 - \dot Q t - \delta Q\sin\omega t\), where \(\dot Q\) is the programmed speed and \(\delta Q\) a ripple of angular frequency \(\omega\) from the power converters. Particles are extracted only while the triangle is shrinking, at a speed \(-d\Delta Q/dt = \dot Q + \omega\,\delta Q\cos\omega t\), so while that stays positive the rate is proportional to it. When \(\omega\,\delta Q\) exceeds \(\dot Q\), the speed turns negative for part of each cycle: the triangle grows again and the spill stops. It stays off a little longer, until the triangle has shrunk back below the smallest size it had before, because the particles between were already extracted; then it bursts. The spill is chopped (Figure 21.8). The critical ripple is

\[ \delta Q_{\mathrm{crit}} = \frac{\dot Q}{\omega} \]

Bryant’s example is a medical ring that sweeps \(\Delta Q = 0.01\) in 1 s near \(Q_x = 1.666\). At 50 Hz, \(\delta Q_{\mathrm{crit}} = 0.01/(2\pi\cdot50) = 3.2\times10^{-5}\), a relative ripple of \(1.9\times10^{-5}\) in the quadrupole current; at 1 kHz it is \(9.6\times10^{-7}\). Even a ripple of 30 per cent of the critical value modulates the spill between 0.7 and 1.3 of its mean. This is the legendary sensitivity of slow extraction.

Figure 21.8. The spill rate under a 50 Hz ripple in the tune, relative to the rate without ripple. Blue: a ripple of 0.3 of the critical value, which modulates the spill between 0.7 and 1.3. Red: 1.5 times the critical value. The triangle grows again for part of each 20 ms period, and the spill stays off from 7.4 to 15.5 ms until the triangle is smaller than it has ever been; then it jumps back and peaks at 2.5. Both curves average to 1, since the beam is extracted all the same; what changes is how evenly. The red curve is computed from the running minimum of \(\Delta Q(t)\). Remedies are quiet power converters, eddy currents in thick laminations that filter high frequencies, feedback, and “front-end” acceleration, such as empty RF buckets, that makes particles cross into resonance faster than the ripple moves them.

RF knock-out. Instead of moving the beam or the resonance, keep both fixed and heat the beam. A transverse RF electric field at a betatron sideband frequency, \(f = (n \pm q)f_0\) with \(q\) the fractional tune, drives coherent growth; a spread of tunes makes it diffusion. Particles grow in amplitude until they cross the separatrix. Switching off the RF stops the spill almost at once, so the beam can follow a patient’s breathing. K. Hiramoto and M. Nishi described a scheme of this kind in 1992, and at HIMAC in Chiba, which began clinical work on 21 June 1994, K. Noda and colleagues extracted 290 MeV/u carbon ions with an RF field of about 440 V at 1.012 MHz, frequency-modulated over 5 kHz to cover the tune spread and amplitude-modulated to flatten the spill.

12 Transfer lines and matching

A transfer line has no periodic solution. The Twiss parameters at its end depend on those at its start, propagated by the line’s transfer matrix for \((x, x')\), whose first row \(C, S\) holds the cosine-like and sine-like solutions at the end of the line and whose second row \(C', S'\) their derivatives \(d/ds\) (here \(C\) and \(S\) are not the circumference or the sextupole strength):

\[ \begin{pmatrix} \beta_x \\ \alpha_x \\ \gamma_x \end{pmatrix}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} C^2 & -2CS & S^2 \\ -CC' & CS' + SC' & -SS' \\ C'^2 & -2C'S' & S'^2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} \beta_x \\ \alpha_x \\ \gamma_x \end{pmatrix}_1 \]

At the end of the line, eight quantities must equal those of the receiving ring at the injection point: \(\beta_x\), \(\alpha_x\), \(D_x\), \(D_x'\) and \(\beta_y\), \(\alpha_y\), \(D_y\), \(D_y'\). Long lines have a regular FODO section (alternating focusing and defocusing quadrupoles, chapter 16, section 6) in the middle and independently powered matching quadrupoles at each end; the LHC’s two lines are about 3 km long. The geometry, six degrees of freedom of position and direction, must also join.

A transfer line is also the place to catch a beam that has gone wrong. Collimators set \(n\) beam sizes from the axis at several phases cut phase space into a polygon. With three collimators per plane 60° apart in phase, each cutting at \(\pm n\) along its own direction in normalised phase space, the six cuts make a regular hexagon whose inscribed circle has radius \(n\), and the largest amplitude that escapes is the radius to its corners,

\[ a_{\max} = \frac{n}{\cos(60^\circ/2)} \]

At the LHC’s lines the collimators are at 4.5σ, for which the formula gives 5.2σ; with 1.4σ of set-up accuracy for collimator and trajectory added, V. Kain gives a largest escaping amplitude of 6.9σ.

13 Beam dumps and machine protection

A stored beam carries \(U = n_b N E\). At the LHC’s design, \(n_b = 2808\) bunches of \(N = 1.15\times10^{11}\) protons at 7 TeV:

\[ U = 2808 \times 1.15\times10^{11} \times 7\times10^{12} \times 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J} = 362\ \mathrm{MJ} \]

per beam, the energy of 87 kg of TNT (at 4.184 MJ/kg). One SPS batch of 288 bunches at 450 GeV holds 2.4 MJ. The damage limit, simulated and measured at 450 GeV, is about \(2\times10^{12}\) protons, 144 kJ, 0.6 per cent of the full ring. So the end of every fill, and every fault, is a fast extraction that must not fail.

The LHC dump (JINST 2008, chapter 10). Fifteen kicker magnets (MKD) per ring, each giving 0.428 T m from an 18.5 kA pulse at up to 30 kV, deflect the beam horizontally by \(15 \times 0.428/23\,349 = 0.275\) mrad. The field rises in 3.0 µs and stays flat for at least 90 µs. Fifteen Lambertson septa (MSD) then lift the beam 2.4 mrad vertically over the arc cryostat. A Lambertson septum is a septum magnet whose thin wall is iron, not a current-carrying conductor: the field is in the iron-bounded region the extracted beam enters, while the circulating beam passes through a hole in the iron that the field does not reach. It bends at right angles to the kicker’s plane, here vertically after a horizontal kick, and needs no thin coil, so it can be strong and robust. The beam travels down a line of 975 m in all to a graphite absorber, the TDE: a segmented carbon cylinder 7.7 m long and 700 mm across, in a steel jacket filled with nitrogen and surrounded by about 900 t of shielding.

The kicker cannot rise through bunches, so the ring keeps an abort gap of 3 µs free of particles, 3.4 per cent of the 88.9 µs turn, and the dump trigger is synchronised to it. If the beam came out undiluted, its spot at the TDE, where \(\beta_x = 4990\) m, would have \(\sigma_x = \sqrt{\beta_x\varepsilon} = \sqrt{4990 \times 5\times10^{-10}}\ \mathrm{m} = 1.6\) mm. Ten dilution kickers (MKB), four horizontal and six vertical, driven by sinusoidal currents at 14.2 and 12.7 kHz, sweep the bunches in an “e” shape at no less than 10 mm/µs. The 85.9 µs of beam is spread along a path of at least 0.86 m, and the peak energy per unit area falls by the ratio of path length to \(\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_x\), about 220. (A round Gaussian spot of energy \(U\) has its peak at \(U/(2\pi\sigma_x^2)\); the same energy spread evenly along a line of length \(\ell\), with the same Gaussian width across it, has its peak at \(U/(\ell\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_x)\), the Gaussian having peak \(1/(\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma)\) per unit length. Exercise 10 works it.)

Reliability. The system is designed for a total failure no more often than once in \(10^6\) hours, about once a century. At least 14 of the 15 MKD must fire, and the system is built to work with one missing: 14 give 0.257 mrad. Each MKD generator has two parallel branches. If one switch fires on its own, a re-trigger fires the rest within 700 ns. The kicker strength must follow the beam energy to ±0.5 per cent; a beam energy tracking system reads the main dipole currents and dumps the beam if any element is out of step. Against what cannot be prevented, an asynchronous dump, one in which the kickers fire out of step with the abort gap and so rise through bunches, sweeping them across the aperture, movable graphite absorbers (TCDQ, 9 m long) and a fixed one (TCDS) protect the septa and the ring.

How it was researched and built

Kickers and septa. The AGS and the PS each built fast kickers in the early 1960s; the AGS kicker was a picture-frame magnet of 500 G switched in 200 ns, and CERN’s fast ejection first worked in May 1963, with single-bunch ejection used for emulsion studies of the neutrino beam. Barton noted in 1967 that CERN’s septa had reached fields comparable to Brookhaven’s ejector with a septum coil only 3 mm thick, and that closed-orbit bumps could bring the beam to a fixed septum instead of ramming the septum into the beam. Since then kicker design has turned on switches (thyratrons, then solid-state thyristor stacks, chosen for the LHC dump for reliability), on ferrites, and on circuit modelling: the MKI’s PFN was optimised in PSpice, first with an ideal transmission-line magnet and then with all the parasitic elements, and damping resistors of about 30 Ω across each cell were added to meet the ±0.5 per cent ripple.

Charge exchange. In the Novosibirsk experiments that followed the first accumulation of 1964, beams of 1 MeV neutral hydrogen were stripped in a supersonic hydrogen jet switched on only during injection. Dimov’s group accumulated proton currents up to the space-charge limit, about 300 mA, and in doing so first observed and explained the electron-proton instability, now called the electron-cloud effect. They later went beyond the space-charge limit with electron compensation. The method spread: it was tested on the ZGS at Argonne at 50 MeV in 1969, used on the Fermilab Booster at 200 MeV from 1978, the AGS from 1982, ISIS at the Rutherford Laboratory from 1984, and the SNS from 2006 (Dudnikov). CERN’s Booster converted in the 2019 to 2020 shutdown. A stripping-foil test stand in the Linac4 line compared foil types during Linac4’s reliability runs, and the new injection region, with a six-position foil changer, a screen 6 mm in front of the foil and titanium plates in the H⁰/H⁻ dump that measure the unstripped fraction, went in during the 2019 to 2020 shutdown. At SNS, nanocrystalline diamond foils grown by chemical vapour deposition on silicon have been made since 2003; because beam time for foil tests is scarce, Oak Ridge built a 30 keV electron-beam test stand whose 1.6 mA/mm² matches the heating of a 1.4 MW beam. The step beyond foils is laser stripping. At SNS a proof-of-principle experiment in 2006 stripped 90 per cent of a 7 ns slice of the 1 GeV H⁻ beam using a magnet, a laser and a second magnet; in 2017 the same group stripped about 95 per cent of 10 µs bursts with a 1 MW peak-power ultraviolet laser.

Slow extraction. After Hereward’s 1961 proposal for the PS, Y. Kobayashi and H. Takahashi gave the Hamiltonian that bears Kobayashi’s name at the 1967 accelerator conference in Cambridge, Massachusetts. Medical synchrotrons made slow extraction routine and demanding: the CERN-led Proton-Ion Medical Machine Study (PIMMS, 2000) worked out momentum-driven extraction with a betatron core for such rings, and the Japanese medical ring HIMAC put RF knock-out into use. At the SPS, after decades of Q-sweep, the push for more protons on target for a beam dump experiment led to COSE (2018), shadowing of the septum wires by a thin upstream diffuser (a tantalum wire array reduced losses by 15 per cent; a bent silicon crystal, which channels protons through the wire plane, by over 40 per cent), and separatrix folding with octupoles (also over 40 per cent). Combined, the crystal and folding cut losses by a little more than a factor of three. These were tested over 12 hours at the full \(3\times10^{13}\) protons per spill.

Multi-turn extraction. From proposal (2001) to operation (September 2015) took 14 years: theory and simulation, single-bunch tests in the PS in 2002 to 2004, a design report in 2006, a month of operation in 2010 that exposed the ripple and activation problems, a dummy septum built in the 2013 to 2014 shutdown, and then the rest of the commissioning. The 2016 progress report has eleven authors.

The LHC beam dump. The dump core material was chosen by Monte Carlo simulations of energy deposition, heat transfer and stress: carbon, for its high sublimation temperature and resistance to thermal shock, in sections of 1.73 g/cm³ and 1.1 g/cm³ graphite, with aluminium and iron at the back. It is designed for \(2\times10^4\) full-intensity dumps over 20 years. The shielding is made of yokes from the old ISR dipoles, partly filled with concrete.

How it is designed today. Optics of rings and lines are designed and matched in MAD-X or its successors (MAD-NG, Xsuite), with the injection and extraction elements as thin or thick kicks. Injection painting and space charge are simulated with codes such as PyORBIT; resonant extraction is tracked turn by turn through millions of turns, with power-converter ripple added from measured spectra. Particle-shower codes give the energy deposited in dumps, absorbers and septa, and finite-element codes the resulting temperatures and stresses. Kicker circuits are modelled in circuit simulators like SPICE. Standard choices: kicker and septum a quarter-wavelength apart near a defocusing quadrupole; collimators 90° from the device they guard; a gap in the bunch train for every kicker rise; and an interlock that refuses high-intensity beam until a low-intensity “probe” has gone round.

At the bench

Injection oscillations on the BPMs. At injection, an operator watches the trajectory of the first turns relative to the closed orbit. The LHC’s injection display shows the trajectory in the transfer line and the first turn minus the closed orbit over the first 3 km of the ring, covering many phases at once. A single turn-by-turn BPM shows a sine at the tune whose amplitude seems to die away over tens to hundreds of turns. It does not damp; the particles decohere, and the BPM sees only the centre of charge (Tecker). Because a septum error and a kicker error are 90° apart in phase, the two can be separated from the shape of the first-turn difference orbit and corrected with an optics model. After every LHC injection, automatic quality checks record the oscillations and the losses; a bad injection inhibits the next one.

Scanning kicker timing. The kicker must be on for the injected bunch and off for the stored ones. With a single bunch, the operator steps the kicker trigger delay in small steps and records the injection oscillation, or the transmission, at each delay. The plot reproduces the kicker’s waveform as the beam sees it: a rising edge, a flat top with its ripple, a falling edge. One sets the timing in the middle of the flat top. During the APS Upgrade’s commissioning in 2024 the first step was exactly this, scanning the injection kicker timing to maximise transmission. The beam then got as far as sector 28. First-turn trajectory correction to within ±2 mm let it go round five turns; with tuning, 15; with RF on, 80. A Bayesian optimiser, a program that tries settings, fits a statistical model of how the result depends on them, and picks each next try where the model expects the most gain, varying bumps, tunes and line settings, then gave the first stored beam, 40 µA with a 4 s lifetime, and found a +2 mm orbit bump at the injection point. That bump was the clue to an aperture restriction: the stored-beam vacuum chamber had bowed outward inside the septum magnet, eating its 3 mm design clearance. Keeping only the bump, the team stored 50 µA with a 10 minute lifetime and raised injection efficiency from 20 to 70 per cent. Earlier, beam had refused to go down the transfer line until the team found, by measuring the trajectory response, that five quadrupoles had been reconnected with reversed polarity: the original APS was designed for positrons, and its polarities had been switched to run electrons.

Extraction efficiency. Beam current transformers in the ring and in the line give the numbers: the ratio of charge measured in the line to the charge that left the ring. For a fast extraction this should be within the transformers’ accuracy of 100 per cent. For slow extraction, the losses are measured as signal on beam loss monitors near the electrostatic septum, normalised to the extracted protons, and followed shift by shift. At the SPS, aligning the five septum tanks to the beam used to take 8 hours of beam time; a numerical optimiser acting on the girder and anode positions now does it in about 40 minutes and 130 extractions.

The spill on a scintillation counter. A plastic scintillator with a photomultiplier (chapter 1) in the extracted beam, counting particles in time bins, shows the spill at every time scale. At the SPS, in a well-adjusted 4.8 s flat top, the extracted intensity rises over the first 500 ms (on purpose, while the RF structure fades) and then stays flat, with an effective spill length, \((\int f\,dt)^2/\int f^2\,dt\), of about 4450 ms on average over four weeks of 2018, a duty factor near 95 per cent. Zoom in and the 50, 100 and 150 Hz harmonics of the mains appear: uncorrected, the spill is almost 100 per cent modulated. A servo system adds current at these frequencies to cancel them, but the phase and amplitude drift and must be retuned; in 2018 the 50 Hz line was well corrected about 85 per cent of the time, against 60 per cent in 2017. Zoom further: the SPS turn, 43 kHz, carries the imprint of its filling from the PS, two trains of 10.5 µs and two gaps of 1 µs, which does not wash out in a second; and at 200 MHz the RF bunch structure remains in the extracted beam for several hundred milliseconds after the RF is switched off.

Septum wires. The SPS septum has five tanks, 3.15 m long, each with 2080 tungsten-rhenium wires (60 µm in the first two tanks, 100 µm in the rest). The field pulls the wires towards the cathode: measured with a theodolite (a surveyor’s telescope that measures angles precisely) through a viewport, the 60 µm wires moved 105 µm and the 100 µm wires 45 µm at 11 MV/m. Measured with beam, the septum’s effective thickness is about 500 µm, several times the wire diameter, from sag, misalignment and stray field. The wires and supports heat when beam is extracted, which is why the anode supports of the first three tanks are made of Invar, an iron-nickel alloy that hardly expands when heated (the last two are stainless steel). When fast-slow extractions of \(1.5\times10^{13}\) protons in under 10 ms were made for the old West Area neutrino beam, the septum was damaged relatively often. Loss accumulates as dose: in 2018 a high-voltage feedthrough on one tank broke and the main cable powering all of them failed. A tank out of line shows as extra loss on the loss monitors next to it; the alignment scans use exactly this signal. Tanks are exchanged during shutdowns, with dose to people planned in advance.

The beam-dump sequence. At the LHC the beam is dumped when any input to the beam interlock system withdraws its permit. The trigger waits for the abort gap, the 15 MKD and then the MKB fire, and the septa, always on, bend the beam into the dump line. Afterwards the external post-operational check (XPOC) analyses the event: each kicker’s current waveform against references calibrated at 18 energies from 400 GeV to 7.1 TeV, the beam instrumentation along the dump line, and an image of the swept beam on a screen just in front of the TDE, whose “e” skeleton is compared with the expected one. If XPOC finds a fault, injection is blocked until an expert clears it. On the way back in, the SPS will not extract a high-intensity beam unless the LHC already has beam circulating: a probe of fewer than \(5\times10^9\) protons, about a twentieth of a nominal bunch, goes first. Interlock masks that let operators set up with beam work only below the set-up intensity, \(10^{12}\) protons at 450 GeV, scaled down as \((E/450\ \mathrm{GeV})^{-1.7}\) at higher energy. The SPS extraction septum circuit has a current decay time of only 23 ms and bends by 12 mrad; a power failure 1 ms before extraction could move the beam by about 40σ in a line of 10σ aperture, so fast monitors detect current changes of \(10^{-4}\) in under 1 ms and withdraw the permit.

Where it stops

Each method in this chapter ends at a material limit.

Charge-exchange injection ends at the foil’s temperature. A foil that radiates its heat away reaches about 1700 to 2000 K at the SNS’s design power in the model above, and carbon foils fail by sublimation above a threshold temperature; Cousineau and colleagues estimate the practical limit on beam power density to be within an order of magnitude of what is used today. Painting more widely spreads the heat but enlarges the beam. Laser-assisted stripping, with no material in the beam, is the way past it, demonstrated for microsecond pulses in 2017 and still being extended towards millisecond pulses.

Slow extraction ends at the septum. A few per cent of a 400 GeV beam lost on wires is a few times \(10^{11}\) protons per spill, and the activation of the extraction region limits how many protons per year the SPS can extract, how often people can work there, and how long the high-voltage parts survive. A factor of four reduction per extracted proton was needed for a proposed beam dump facility. Shadowing with crystals, folding with octupoles and septa of lower atomic number are the current answers.

The dump ends at the energy in the beam. The LHC’s 362 MJ per beam needs 975 m of line, ten dilution kickers and a 7.7 m graphite core; the High-Luminosity LHC is designed for roughly twice the stored energy, and several parts of its dump system, including the entrance window of the dump block, had to be redesigned. For any larger collider the stored energy grows with both the energy and the number of particles, and so does the dump.

Beyond the joins between machines, the beam itself limits how much a ring can hold: space charge at low energy, wakefields and instabilities at high current. These are the subject of chapter 22. The colliders that the chains feed, and the luminosity they need, are in chapter 23; the cooling that makes antiproton accumulation possible is in chapter 24.

Exercises

1. The SPS injects into the LHC at 450 GeV/\(c\). Find the LHC dipole field at injection and the dynamic range to 6.8 TeV and to 7 TeV. (\(\rho = 2803.95\) m.)

Answer

\(B\rho = 3.3356 \times 450 = 1501.0\) T m, so \(B = 1501.0/2803.95 = 0.535\) T. The dynamic range is \(6800/450 = 15.1\) at 6.8 TeV and \(7000/450 = 15.6\) at 7 TeV.

2. Find the energy stored in one LHC beam at design (2808 bunches of \(1.15\times10^{11}\) protons at 7 TeV), and compare it with the set-up limit of \(10^{12}\) protons at 450 GeV.

Answer

\(U = 2808 \times 1.15\times10^{11} \times 7\times10^{12} \times 1.602\times10^{-19}\) J \(= 362\) MJ, or 86.6 kg of TNT. The set-up beam is \(10^{12} \times 450\times10^9 \times 1.602\times10^{-19} = 72\) kJ, 5000 times less, and half the measured damage limit of \(2\times10^{12}\) protons at 450 GeV.

3. At 14 GeV/\(c\) a proton goes round the PS (628.3 m) and the SPS (6911 m). Find the revolution periods and show that two five-turn extractions from the PS fit into the SPS with room for kicker gaps.

Answer

\(\beta = p/\sqrt{p^2 + m_p^2c^2} = 0.997762\). The PS period is \(628.3/(\beta c) = 2.10\) µs and the SPS period \(6911/(\beta c) = 23.1\) µs. Five PS turns are 10.5 µs; two such trains are 21.0 µs, leaving 2.1 µs, two gaps of about 1 µs for the SPS injection and extraction kickers.

4. A travelling-wave kicker has 33 cells of 101 nH and 4.04 nF and is driven by a PFN charged to 54 kV. Find its impedance, its fill time, its current, and the field if the kick per 2.7 m magnet is 0.3 T m. What deflection do four magnets give at 450 GeV/\(c\)?

Answer

\(Z = \sqrt{101/4.04}\ \Omega = 5.0\ \Omega\); the delay per cell is \(\sqrt{101\times10^{-9}\times4.04\times10^{-9}}\) s \(= 20.2\) ns, so the fill time is 667 ns. The magnet sees half the PFN voltage, 27 kV, so \(I = 5.4\) kA. The field is \(0.3/2.7 = 0.111\) T. Four magnets give 1.2 T m, which at \(B\rho = 1501\) T m is 0.80 mrad.

5. Show that a beam injected with its centre offset by \(a\) rms beam sizes ends, after filamentation, with emittance \(\varepsilon(1 + a^2/2)\). At LHC injection, with \(\varepsilon_n = 3.5\) µm and \(\beta_x = 100\) m, how large an offset gives 5 per cent growth?

Answer

Each particle’s action \(J = (X^2+P^2)/2\) is conserved, and after filamentation the phases are uniform, so \(\varepsilon_f = \langle J\rangle\). With \(X = X_0 + \Delta X\), \(P = P_0 + \Delta P\) and \(\langle X_0\rangle = \langle P_0\rangle = 0\), \(\langle J\rangle = \varepsilon + (\Delta X^2 + \Delta P^2)/2 = \varepsilon(1 + a^2/2)\). For 5 per cent, \(a = \sqrt{0.1} = 0.316\). With \(\gamma = 479.6\), \(\varepsilon = 7.30\) nm and \(\sigma_x = \sqrt{100 \times 7.30\times10^{-9}}\) m \(= 0.854\) mm, so the offset must stay below 0.27 mm.

6. For 160 MeV H⁻ on carbon take \(\sigma_{-0} = 17.72\), \(\sigma_{-+} = 0.31\) and \(\sigma_{0+} = 6.92\), in \(10^{-19}\) cm². Find the fractions of H⁻ and H⁰ leaving a 200 µg/cm² foil, and the fraction of protons.

Answer

\(n = 200 \times 5.014\times10^{16} = 1.003\times10^{19}\) cm\(^{-2}\). \(\sigma_- n = 18.03\times10^{-19} \times 1.003\times10^{19} = 18.08\), so \(f_- = e^{-18.08} = 1.4\times10^{-8}\). \(f_0 = [17.72/(6.92 - 18.03)](e^{-18.08} - e^{-6.94}) = 1.55\times10^{-3}\). The proton fraction is \(1 - f_- - f_0 = 0.9985\).

7. Space charge limits the Fermilab Booster to about \(4.4\times10^{12}\) protons per pulse at 400 MeV injection. If the limit scales with \(\beta\gamma^2\) at fixed emittance and tune shift, what would 800 MeV allow? Compare with the PIP-II aim of \(6.5\times10^{12}\).

Answer

At 400 MeV, \(\gamma = 1.4263\), \(\beta = 0.7131\), \(\beta\gamma^2 = 1.451\); at 800 MeV, \(\gamma = 1.8526\), \(\beta = 0.8418\), \(\beta\gamma^2 = 2.889\). The ratio is 1.99, so the limit would be about \(8.8\times10^{12}\). The aim of \(6.5\times10^{12}\) leaves margin for losses, which must fall per proton as the intensity rises.

8. A thin extraction sextupole with \(k_2\ell = 2\) m\(^{-2}\) sits where \(\beta_x = 10\) m. The largest particle in the beam has amplitude 10 mm there. At what distance \(\Delta Q\) from the third-integer resonance does extraction begin? What is the spiral step at 30 mm on a septum where \(\beta_x\) is also 10 m and the outgoing separatrix lies along \(x\), and what fraction is lost on a septum of effective thickness 0.1 mm placed there?

Answer

\(S = \tfrac12 \times 10^{3/2} \times 2 = 31.6\) m\(^{-1/2}\). The largest particle has \(X = 0.01/\sqrt{10} = 3.16\times10^{-3}\) m\(^{1/2}\). It first becomes unstable when the inscribed circle of the triangle reaches it, \(h = X\), so \(\Delta Q = SX/4\pi = 7.96\times10^{-3}\). The spiral step at 30 mm is \(\tfrac38 \times 10 \times 2 \times 0.03^2 = 6.75\) mm. The loss is about \(0.1/6.75 = 1.5\) per cent. (Strictly the circle first touches the triangle’s sides at \(h\); particles near the corners at up to \(2h\) survive a little longer.)

9. A medical ring sweeps the tune by 0.01 in 1 s near \(Q_x = 1.666\). Find the relative ripple in the quadrupole current, at 50 Hz and at 1 kHz, that chops the spill (take \(\Delta Q/Q \approx \Delta I/I\)). If the ripple is 30 per cent of the critical value, what are the spill’s maximum and minimum relative to its mean?

Answer

\(\delta Q_{\mathrm{crit}} = \dot Q/\omega = 0.01/(2\pi \times 50) = 3.18\times10^{-5}\), or \(1.9\times10^{-5}\) relative; at 1 kHz, \(1.59\times10^{-6}\), or \(9.6\times10^{-7}\) relative. At 30 per cent of critical the rate \(\dot Q(1 + 0.3\cos\omega t)\) runs between 0.7 and 1.3 of its mean, and \((\int f)^2/(T\int f^2) = 0.957\).

10. At the LHC dump block \(\beta_x = 4990\) m and the emittance at 7 TeV is \(5\times10^{-10}\) m. The dilution kickers sweep the beam at no less than 10 mm/µs, and the beam occupies all of the 88.9 µs turn except the 3 µs abort gap. Estimate the beam size, the length of the sweep and the reduction in peak energy per unit area. How far apart are successive bunches (25 ns spacing) on the block?

Answer

\(\sigma_x = \sqrt{4990 \times 5\times10^{-10}}\) m \(= 1.58\) mm. The beam lasts 85.9 µs, so the sweep is at least 0.86 m long. A line of charge of total length \(\ell\) has peak areal density \(U/(\ell\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma)\) instead of \(U/(2\pi\sigma^2)\) for a spot (taken as round; \(\beta_y\) at the block is 4670 m, so \(\sigma_y\) is close to \(\sigma_x\)), a ratio \(\ell/(\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma) = 217\). Bunches land \(25\ \mathrm{ns} \times 10\ \mathrm{mm/\mu s} = 0.25\) mm apart, much closer than \(\sigma_x\), so the trace is continuous.
