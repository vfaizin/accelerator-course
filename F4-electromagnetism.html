F4 Electricity and magnetism

1600 to 1888: Gilbert’s amber and lodestone, Franklin’s charge, Coulomb’s balance, Volta’s pile, Ørsted’s compass, Ampère’s currents, Faraday’s induction, and Maxwell’s equations, proved by Hertz. Charge, field, potential, current, magnetism and induction, joined into four equations whose waves are light. Everything an accelerator does to a particle, it does through these laws.

What this chapter assumes. High-school physics (force, energy, work), F1 Calculus (derivatives, integrals, the exponential decay of section 10, partial derivatives), and F2 Vectors and vector calculus (vectors, the dot and cross products, the Lorentz force, fields, the gradient, line and flux integrals, divergence, curl, and the theorems of Gauss and Stokes). The mathematics of F2 is not derived again here; this chapter is about the physics it describes. F3 Newtonian mechanics is useful for force and energy but not needed in detail.

What comes next. F5 Oscillations and waves takes the electromagnetic waves of section 17 further, with phasors for alternating currents. Inside the course, the pre-requisite sections go deeper: Gauss’s law and potentials of simple shapes and Faraday induction, inductance and magnetic energy in chapter 1, Biot-Savart and Ampère’s law in chapter 2, and Maxwell’s equations and the wave equation in chapter 13.

In one page

The problem. An accelerator is a machine for pushing charged particles: giving them energy, steering them round bends, and squeezing them into a narrow beam. Every one of those pushes is electric or magnetic. To design one, or even to read about one, you need to know how charges make fields, how fields push charges, what a voltage is and why a particle’s energy can be quoted in volts, how currents make magnetic fields, how a changing magnetic field makes an electric one, and how energy is stored in fields and carried by them.

The idea. Charges fill the space round them with an electric field; moving charges, that is currents, also make a magnetic field. A charge feels the fields where it is, with the Lorentz force \(q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})\). A static electric field is the downhill slope of a potential, so the energy a charge gains depends only on the voltage between start and end: one volt gives a charge \(e\) one electron-volt. Currents flow when a field pushes the free charges in a conductor; resistance turns their energy into heat; capacitors store charge and energy in an electric field, coils store energy in a magnetic field. A changing magnetic flux makes an electric field that circulates, which is how transformers, generators and the betatron work. Maxwell added one term, the displacement current, and the four laws then predict waves that travel at \(1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}\), the speed of light.

Equations to remember.

  • Coulomb’s law: \(F = \dfrac{q_1q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\), with charges \(q\) in coulombs (C), distance \(r\) in m, and \(\varepsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12}\) F/m.
  • Lorentz force: \(\mathbf{F} = q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})\), with \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) in V/m and \(\mathbf{B}\) in tesla (T).
  • Gauss’s law: \(\displaystyle\oint\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot d\mathbf{A} = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}\); a charged sphere outside, \(\mathcal{E} = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\); a line, \(\dfrac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0r}\); a sheet, \(\dfrac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\); between plates, \(\dfrac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\).
  • Potential: \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\nabla V\), energy gained \(W = q\,\Delta V\); between plates \(\mathcal{E} = V/d\); 1 eV \(= 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\) J.
  • Capacitance: \(C = Q/V\) in farads (F); plates \(C = \varepsilon_0A/d\), sphere \(C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R\); stored energy \(\tfrac12CV^2\); energy density \(\tfrac12\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}^2\) in J/m\(^3\).
  • Current: \(I = dQ/dt\) in amperes (A); \(I = nqv_dA\); Ohm’s law \(V = IR\), with \(R = \rho L/A\) in ohms (Ω); power \(P = IV = I^2R\) in watts.
  • RC circuit: \(V = V_0e^{-t/RC}\), time constant \(\tau = RC\).
  • A long straight wire: \(B = \dfrac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}\); a loop on its axis: \(B = \dfrac{\mu_0IR^2}{2(R^2 + z^2)^{3/2}}\); a long solenoid: \(B = \mu_0nI\); \(\mu_0 = 1.2566 \times 10^{-6}\) T m/A.
  • Force on a wire: \(\mathbf{F} = I\mathbf{L}\times\mathbf{B}\).
  • Faraday’s law: EMF \(= -\dfrac{d\Phi_B}{dt}\), flux \(\Phi_B = \int\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{A}\) in webers (Wb \(=\) T m\(^2\)); moving rod: EMF \(= BLv\).
  • Inductance: \(V = L\,dI/dt\), \(L\) in henries (H); energy \(\tfrac12LI^2\); energy density \(B^2/2\mu_0\); LR time constant \(L/R\).
  • Maxwell’s equations (section 16): \(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = \rho/\varepsilon_0\), \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0\), \(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t\), \(\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{J} + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}/\partial t\).
  • Light: \(c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0} = 2.998 \times 10^8\) m/s; in a wave \(\mathcal{E} = cB\); Poynting vector \(\mathbf{S} = \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\times\mathbf{B}/\mu_0\) in W/m\(^2\).

Numbers worth knowing.

  • The electron’s charge is \(e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\) C, so one coulomb is \(6.24 \times 10^{18}\) electrons, and a beam of 1 mA carries \(6.24 \times 10^{15}\) electrons a second.
  • Two protons push each other apart \(1.24 \times 10^{36}\) times harder than they attract by gravity. A handy product: \(e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) = 1.44\) MeV fm.
  • An electron from 2 kV has 2 keV \(= 3.20 \times 10^{-16}\) J and moves at \(2.65 \times 10^7\) m/s; a proton from 1 MV moves at \(1.38 \times 10^7\) m/s.
  • Air breaks down at about 3 MV/m. A sphere of radius 1 m can therefore be held at up to about 3 MV, and stores 55.6 J at 1 MV; a 1 cm ball sparks over at 30 kV.
  • Electrons in a copper wire carrying 1 A per mm\(^2\) drift at only 0.07 mm/s.
  • A long solenoid with 2000 turns per metre and 5 A has 12.6 mT inside. An iron magnet with a 10 cm gap needs 119 000 ampere-turns for 1.5 T.
  • A magnetic field of 1 T stores 0.40 MJ per cubic metre; the LHC’s 8.33 T stores 27.6 MJ per cubic metre, and each LHC dipole 6.93 MJ.
  • Sunlight at the Earth, 1361 W/m\(^2\), has an electric field of amplitude about 1 kV/m and a magnetic field of 3.4 μT.

The problem it solved

Amber and lodestone

Rubbed amber picks up straw, and the lodestone, a natural magnet, picks up iron; both were known in antiquity. William Gilbert, physician to Elizabeth I, studied them side by side in De Magnete (1600). He showed that the Earth itself is a great magnet, which is why a compass points north, and he coined the Latin electricus, “like amber”, for the power of rubbed bodies. To him the two effects were different things, and they stayed apart for two centuries.

Charge, and Coulomb’s law

In the 1740s Benjamin Franklin in Philadelphia described electricity as one fluid that could be in excess (positive) or short (negative), moved from body to body but never made or destroyed. That is the conservation of charge, and his signs are the ones used today. The Leyden jar, invented in 1745 to 1746, stored enough charge to give a serious shock: the first capacitor. In 1785 Charles-Augustin de Coulomb measured the force between small charged spheres with a torsion balance, a thin wire twisted by the force, and found it falls as the inverse square of the distance, like gravity.

Currents and magnets

In 1800 Alessandro Volta stacked discs of zinc and copper separated by brine-soaked card, and the pile gave the first steady current. In 1820 Hans Christian Ørsted saw a compass needle turn when a current flowed in a wire nearby: electricity and magnetism were linked. Within months Jean-Baptiste Biot and Félix Savart measured the field round a wire, and André-Marie Ampère showed that two currents push or pull on each other and built the mathematical theory of the forces between currents. Georg Simon Ohm published his law of resistance in 1827.

Induction

If a current makes magnetism, can magnetism make a current? Michael Faraday at the Royal Institution found in 1831 that it can, but only while the magnetism is changing. Joseph Henry in America found the same independently. In 1834 Emil Lenz gave the rule for the direction. Faraday, who had little mathematics, pictured the space round charges and magnets filled with lines of force, and thought of these lines as real.

Maxwell’s equations, and light

James Clerk Maxwell put Faraday’s lines into mathematics. In On Physical Lines of Force (1861 to 1862) he added a new term to Ampère’s law, the displacement current, and found that the equations then allowed waves whose speed, computed from electrical measurements, was close to the measured speed of light. In A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field (1865) he concluded that light is an electromagnetic wave, and his Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism followed in 1873. In 1884 John Henry Poynting showed how energy flows through the field. Oliver Heaviside cut Maxwell’s many equations down to the four used today (F2), and in 1887 to 1888 Heinrich Hertz in Karlsruhe made and detected electromagnetic waves with sparks across gaps, confirming Maxwell. The carrier of the charge was found last: J. J. Thomson identified the electron in 1897 (chapter 2), and Robert Millikan measured its charge with oil drops between 1909 and 1913. Since 2019 the SI system fixes \(e\) at exactly \(1.602176634 \times 10^{-19}\) C.

The physics

0. What this chapter assumes, and a quick check

  • Force and energy. Work is force times distance along the force; energy is the capacity to do work, in joules (J); power is energy per second, in watts (W \(=\) J/s). Kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12mv^2\) (at speeds well below light).
  • From F1. Derivatives as rates; integrals as sums; \(dN/dt = -N/\tau\) has the solution \(N = N_0e^{-t/\tau}\).
  • From F2. The dot and cross products; the force \(q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\) on a moving charge and the circle it makes; \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\nabla V\); line integrals \(\int\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{l}\) and flux integrals \(\int\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{A}\); divergence and Gauss’s theorem; curl and Stokes’s theorem.

Check yourself. (a) A 2 g mass moves at 3 m/s; what is its kinetic energy? (b) A quantity decays with \(\tau = 2\) s; what fraction is left after 4 s? (c) Find \((1, 0, 0)\times(0, 0, 1)\). (d) What is the flux of a uniform field \(\mathbf{F} = (0, 0, 5)\) through a square of side 2 lying flat in the \(x\)-\(y\) plane? Answers: (a) 9 mJ; (b) \(e^{-2} = 0.135\); (c) \((0, -1, 0)\); (d) 20.

1. Charge

The picture. Matter is made of atoms, and atoms of a small heavy nucleus of protons and neutrons with electrons round it. Protons carry a positive charge, electrons an equal negative charge, neutrons none. A body with as many electrons as protons is neutral; rubbing two materials together moves a few electrons from one to the other, leaving one positive and the other negative. Like charges repel, unlike charges attract.

Two facts about charge. It comes in whole multiples of the elementary charge

\[ e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}, \]

the size of the charge on a proton or an electron. (Quarks carry thirds of \(e\), but they are never found alone.) The unit, the coulomb (C), is the charge carried by a current of one ampere in one second; it is \(1/e = 6.24 \times 10^{18}\) elementary charges. And charge is conserved: the total charge of an isolated system never changes. Charges can be moved, and positive and negative pairs can be made or destroyed together (a photon can make an electron and a positron), but no process changes the total. Section 8 writes this as an equation, and section 16 shows Maxwell’s equations need it.

Conductors and insulators. In a metal, one or two electrons per atom are not tied to any atom and wander freely through the crystal: a metal is a conductor. In glass, ceramic, plastic or dry air, every electron is bound to its atom or molecule, and charge put on the surface stays where it was put: these are insulators, or dielectrics. Between them are semiconductors, and gases that become conductors when their molecules are broken into ions and electrons, as in a spark (chapter 4). An accelerator uses all of them: copper and aluminium for electrodes, coils and cavities, ceramic and glass for the insulators that hold them apart, and vacuum, the best insulator of all until the field pulls electrons out of the metal.

Charging by induction. Bring a positive rod near a neutral metal sphere without touching. The sphere’s free electrons are pulled to the near side, leaving the far side positive. Touch the far side to the earth, and electrons flow up from the earth to neutralise it; break the contact, take the rod away, and the sphere is left negative. Nothing touched the rod. This separation of charge in a conductor by a nearby charge is at work every time a beam passes a metal pipe: the beam’s charge draws an equal and opposite image charge onto the wall, which travels with it and is how beam monitors see it (chapter 8).

2. Coulomb’s law

The law. Two small charged bodies (point charges) \(q_1\) and \(q_2\), a distance \(r\) apart, push on each other along the line joining them with a force

\[ F = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2} . \]

A positive \(F\) (like charges) is a repulsion, a negative one an attraction. The constant \(\varepsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12}\) F/m is the permittivity of free space, and \(1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) = 8.988 \times 10^9\) N m\(^2\)/C\(^2\). The \(4\pi\) looks odd here, but it is put in so that it disappears from Gauss’s law (section 4), which is used far more often. As a vector, with \(\hat{\mathbf{r}}\) the unit vector from \(q_1\) to \(q_2\), the force on \(q_2\) is \(\mathbf{F} = q_1q_2\hat{\mathbf{r}}/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)\), and by Newton’s third law the force on \(q_1\) is equal and opposite.

How strong it is. Two charges of 1 C a metre apart would push with \(9 \times 10^9\) N, the weight of a million tonnes. A coulomb is an enormous charge to hold still; everyday static charges are microcoulombs or less. On the atomic scale the force dominates everything. In a hydrogen atom the electron is about \(a_0 = 5.29 \times 10^{-11}\) m from the proton, and the attraction is

\[ F = 8.988 \times 10^9 \times \frac{(1.602 \times 10^{-19})^2}{(5.29 \times 10^{-11})^2} = 8.24 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{N}, \]

which on the electron’s mass of \(9.11 \times 10^{-31}\) kg is an acceleration of \(9.0 \times 10^{22}\) m/s\(^2\). The gravitational pull between two protons, \(Gm_p^2/r^2\), is \(1.24 \times 10^{36}\) times weaker than their electric push at any distance, since both fall as \(1/r^2\). Gravity plays no part in this course except to make magnets sag.

Inside the nucleus. Two protons 1 fm (\(10^{-15}\) m) apart push each other with 231 N, on two particles each of \(1.7 \times 10^{-27}\) kg. Only the strong nuclear force, which acts over a few femtometres, holds a nucleus together against it. To bring a charged particle close enough to a nucleus to react, it must be given enough energy to climb this electric repulsion, the Coulomb barrier: the reason accelerators were built at all (chapter 1).

Where it fails. Coulomb’s law is exact for charges at rest. For moving charges it still gives the right electric field of a slow charge, but a fast charge’s field is squeezed into a disc at right angles to its motion, and a changing one sends out waves (section 17); Maxwell’s equations cover both, Coulomb’s law alone does not.

3. The electric field, and superposition

From force to field. Coulomb’s law says how two charges act on each other across empty space. A more useful way to think splits it in two. A charge \(Q\) fills the space round it with an electric field \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\), a vector at every point (F2, section 5). A charge \(q\) placed at a point feels the force

\[ \mathbf{F} = q\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} \]

from the field there. The field is the force per unit charge, in newtons per coulomb, which section 5 shows is the same as volts per metre (V/m). For a point charge \(Q\), dividing Coulomb’s force by \(q\),

\[ \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\,\hat{\mathbf{r}}, \]

pointing away from a positive \(Q\) and towards a negative one. The field is not only bookkeeping: it carries energy (section 6) and momentum, and when charges move it changes at the speed of light, not instantly (section 17). The particle in an accelerator never “sees” the distant charges on the electrodes; it feels only the field where it is.

Superposition. The fields of several charges add as vectors: the field of \(Q_1\) and \(Q_2\) together is the field of \(Q_1\) alone plus the field of \(Q_2\) alone. This is an experimental fact, true to very high accuracy, and it is what makes electromagnetism solvable: any arrangement of charge can be cut into small pieces, each treated as a point, and their fields added (an integral, F2 section 8).

Example: a dipole. Put \(+1\) nC at \(x = -5\) cm and \(-1\) nC at \(x = +5\) cm, a pair called an electric dipole. Find the field at the point \((0, 5\ \text{cm})\). Each charge is \(r = \sqrt{0.05^2 + 0.05^2} = 0.0707\) m away, and makes a field of size \(8.988 \times 10^9 \times 10^{-9}/0.005 = 1798\) V/m. The positive charge’s field points away from it, along \((1, 1)/\sqrt2\); the negative charge’s field points towards it, along \((1, -1)/\sqrt2\). The \(y\) parts cancel, the \(x\) parts add: \(2 \times 1798/\sqrt2 = 2542\) V/m, pointing along \(+x\), from the positive charge’s side towards the negative one’s. Far from a dipole the two fields nearly cancel, and the field falls as \(1/r^3\) instead of \(1/r^2\).

Field lines. Draw lines that run along the field everywhere (F2, section 5). For electrostatic fields they start on positive charges and end on negative ones (or at infinity), they never cross (the field has one direction at each point), and they are crowded where the field is strong. A dipole’s lines loop from \(+\) to \(-\). Faraday thought of the lines as stretched strings that pull the charges together and push sideways on each other, a picture section 6 makes exact in terms of energy.

4. Gauss’s law in use

The law. F2 (section 9) showed that the flux of a point charge’s field through any sphere round it is \(Q/\varepsilon_0\), whatever the radius, because the \(1/r^2\) of the field cancels the \(r^2\) of the area. By superposition this holds for any number of charges and any closed surface:

\[ \oint_S\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot d\mathbf{A} = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}, \]

where \(Q_{\text{enc}}\) is the total charge inside \(S\). Charges outside send as many field lines in as out, and contribute nothing. With Gauss’s theorem this is \(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = \rho/\varepsilon_0\), the first of Maxwell’s equations (F2, section 10).

How to use it. Gauss’s law is always true, but it gives the field only when symmetry tells you the field’s direction and that its size is the same all over a suitable surface. Then the flux is (field) × (area), and one line of algebra gives the field. Four shapes come up again and again.

A sphere. A charge \(Q\) spread over a sphere of radius \(R\) (on its surface, or through it with a density that depends only on radius). By symmetry the field is radial and the same all round any concentric sphere of radius \(r\). For \(r > R\): \(\mathcal{E}\cdot4\pi r^2 = Q/\varepsilon_0\), so

\[ \mathcal{E} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2} \qquad (r > R), \]

the field of a point charge at the centre. For a charge on the surface only, a sphere inside encloses nothing, and \(\mathcal{E} = 0\) inside.

Example: the Van de Graaff terminal. The high-voltage terminal of an electrostatic generator is a smooth metal sphere (chapter 6). Air breaks down when the field reaches about 3 MV/m (chapter 4). At the surface of a 1 m sphere that field means \(Q = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R^2\mathcal{E} = 3.34 \times 10^{-4}\) C, a surface density of \(\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E} = 2.66 \times 10^{-5}\) C/m\(^2\), and, by section 5, a voltage of 3 MV. More charge and the air round it sparks.

A line. A long straight line, or a thin round beam, with charge \(\lambda\) per metre (C/m). The field points straight out from the axis, and a cylinder of radius \(r\) and length \(L\) round it gives \(\mathcal{E}\cdot2\pi rL = \lambda L/\varepsilon_0\):

\[ \mathcal{E} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0r} . \]

The field falls as \(1/r\), more slowly than a point’s. F2 used this for the 10 mA, 2 kV electron beam: \(\lambda = I/v = 3.77 \times 10^{-10}\) C/m, and 6.8 kV/m at its 1 mm edge.

A flat sheet. A large flat sheet with charge \(\sigma\) per square metre (C/m\(^2\)). By symmetry the field points straight away from the sheet on both sides, with the same size at any distance. Take a short box (a “pillbox”) straddling the sheet, with faces of area \(A\) on each side. Flux leaves through both faces, \(2\mathcal{E}A\), and the charge inside is \(\sigma A\):

\[ \mathcal{E} = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} . \]

The field of an infinite sheet does not weaken with distance at all: the further you go, the more of the sheet you see, and the two effects cancel. Real sheets are finite, so this holds at distances small compared with their size.

Parallel plates. Two large sheets facing each other, a distance \(d\) apart, with \(+\sigma\) and \(-\sigma\). Between them the two fields, each \(\sigma/2\varepsilon_0\), point the same way (away from \(+\), towards \(-\)) and add; outside they point opposite ways and cancel:

\[ \mathcal{E} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\ \text{between}, \qquad 0\ \text{outside}. \]

A uniform field in a box: the simplest accelerating gap and the simplest deflector. Near the edges the field bulges out (the fringe field), and the formula holds only well inside.

Example: the deflecting plates. In chapter 2’s cathode-ray tube, two plates 5 mm apart with 100 V across them make a field of \(100/0.005 = 2.0 \times 10^4\) V/m (section 5). The plates carry \(\sigma = \varepsilon_0\mathcal{E} = 1.77 \times 10^{-7}\) C/m\(^2\), and an electron between them feels \(e\mathcal{E} = 3.2 \times 10^{-15}\) N, an acceleration of \(3.5 \times 10^{15}\) m/s\(^2\) (chapter 2).

Chapter 1’s pre-requisite on Gauss’s law adds the field inside a uniformly charged sphere, and why two charged spheres push like two points.

5. Potential, potential energy and the electron-volt

The picture. Lifting a stone stores energy that comes back when it falls, and it does not matter which way you carried it up: only the height counts. A static electric field behaves the same way. F2 (section 7) showed that the work done by a field that is a gradient depends only on the end points. So we can give every point a potential \(V\), an electrical height, such that the work done by the field on a charge \(q\) moving from \(A\) to \(B\) is

\[ W_{A\to B} = q\,[V(A) - V(B)] . \]

The potential is measured in volts (V), joules per coulomb. A positive charge rolls downhill in potential, gaining kinetic energy; an electron, with its negative charge, rolls uphill, towards higher potential. Only differences of potential matter; the zero is chosen for convenience, at infinity for isolated charges or at the earth (ground) for circuits. “Voltage” means a potential difference.

Field and potential. The field points downhill and its size is the slope (F2, section 6):

\[ \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\nabla V, \qquad V(A) - V(B) = \int_A^B\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot d\mathbf{l} . \]

In one dimension, \(\mathcal{E}_x = -dV/dx\). Between parallel plates the field is uniform, so the potential falls steadily across the gap and

\[ \mathcal{E} = \frac{V}{d} . \]

This is why the field is quoted in V/m: 2 kV across 1 cm is 200 kV/m. For a point charge, integrating \(Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)\) in from infinity, where \(V = 0\), gives

\[ V(r) = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}, \]

and the same outside any spherical charge. A sphere of radius \(R\) carrying \(Q\) is at \(V = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R)\), and its surface field is \(\mathcal{E} = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R^2) = V/R\). That simple ratio, surface field \(=\) voltage over radius, sets the size of every high-voltage terminal: at 3 MV/m a 1 m sphere holds 3 MV, a 1 cm ball only 30 kV.

Potential energy of two charges. The work needed to bring \(q_2\) from infinity to a distance \(r\) from \(q_1\) is \(q_2V\):

\[ U = \frac{q_1q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r} . \]

For two protons 1 fm apart this is \(2.31 \times 10^{-13}\) J, or 1.44 MeV in the unit below. Nuclear physicists use \(e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) = 1.44\) MeV fm to work out Coulomb barriers in their heads: an alpha particle (\(2e\)) and a gold nucleus (\(79e\)) touching at about 10 fm have \(2 \times 79 \times 1.44/10 = 23\) MeV of electric energy.

The electron-volt. A charge \(e\) falling through 1 V gains \(e \times 1\ \text{V} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\) J. This amount is one electron-volt, 1 eV. A particle of charge \(e\) accelerated through \(V\) volts gains \(V\) electron-volts, whatever path it took and whatever its mass: 1 keV \(= 10^3\) eV, 1 MeV \(= 10^6\) eV, 1 GeV \(= 10^9\) eV, 1 TeV \(= 10^{12}\) eV. That is why the energy of an accelerator is quoted in volts.

Example: the electron from 2 kV. An electron leaving a cathode at rest and reaching an anode 2 kV higher gains \(2000 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19} = 3.20 \times 10^{-16}\) J. Setting this equal to \(\tfrac12m_ev^2\):

\[ v = \sqrt{\frac{2eV}{m_e}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \times 2000}{9.109 \times 10^{-31}}} = 2.65 \times 10^7\ \text{m/s}, \]

9 per cent of the speed of light. (The exact relativistic answer, from chapter 1, is 0.3 per cent lower; the formula \(\tfrac12mv^2\) fails when the energy gained is no longer small compared with \(mc^2\), 511 keV for an electron.) A proton through 1 MV, \(mc^2 = 938\) MeV, gets \(v = 1.38 \times 10^7\) m/s, 4.6 per cent of \(c\).

Equipotentials. Surfaces of constant potential are equipotentials, like contour lines on a map. Moving along one takes no work, so the field has no component along it: field lines cross equipotentials at right angles. Between parallel plates the equipotentials are planes parallel to the plates; round a point charge, spheres. Electron-gun and lens designers think in equipotentials, because the shape of the electrodes sets their shape (chapter 7).

Conductors are equipotentials. In a conductor the free charges move whenever there is a field. When they have stopped moving, which takes much less than a nanosecond in a metal, the field inside must be zero; so the whole conductor is at one potential. Three consequences follow.

  • Any excess charge sits on the surface: by Gauss’s law, a surface drawn just inside the metal, where \(\mathcal{E} = 0\), encloses no charge.
  • The field just outside is at right angles to the surface (any part along it would move the surface charges), with size \(\mathcal{E} = \sigma/\varepsilon_0\) (a pillbox with one face inside the metal).
  • The field inside a closed, empty metal box is zero, whatever charges are outside. This Faraday cage is why the inside of a high-voltage terminal is a quiet place for an ion source and its electronics (chapter 6), and why a beam pipe shields the beam from the world.

Sharp points. Join a big sphere of radius \(R_1\) and a small one of radius \(R_2\) by a long wire. They share one potential, so \(Q_1/R_1 = Q_2/R_2\), and their surface fields are \(V/R_1\) and \(V/R_2\): the small sphere has the stronger field, in proportion \(R_1/R_2\). A pointed or sharply curved part of a conductor is a small radius, and the field there is large. That is why lightning conductors are pointed (to leak charge gently into the air), and why everything at high voltage in an accelerator is smooth and round (chapter 4).

Solving for the potential. Combining \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\nabla V\) with \(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = \rho/\varepsilon_0\) gives Poisson’s equation, \(\nabla^2V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0\) (F2, section 12). With conductors as fixed-potential boundaries, that is how real electrode shapes are designed; chapter 2’s pre-requisite teaches the methods.

6. Capacitance, and the energy in the field

Capacitance. Put charge \(+Q\) on one conductor and \(-Q\) on another. The field between them, and so the potential difference \(V\), is proportional to \(Q\): double the charge and every field line doubles. The ratio depends only on shape and size:

\[ C = \frac{Q}{V}, \]

the capacitance, in farads (F \(=\) C/V). A farad is huge; real capacitors are picofarads (pF, \(10^{-12}\) F) to microfarads (μF, \(10^{-6}\) F), and only special ones reach farads. Any arrangement of two conductors is a capacitor; for a single conductor, the other one is “infinity”, or the walls of the room.

Parallel plates. Plates of area \(A\) a distance \(d\) apart: \(\mathcal{E} = \sigma/\varepsilon_0 = Q/(\varepsilon_0A)\) and \(V = \mathcal{E}d\), so

\[ C = \frac{\varepsilon_0A}{d} . \]

Large plates close together store a lot of charge per volt. Chapter 2’s deflecting plates, 2 cm long and (say) 2 cm wide, 5 mm apart, have \(C = 8.854 \times 10^{-12} \times 4 \times 10^{-4}/0.005 = 0.71\) pF, and at 100 V carry \(7.1 \times 10^{-11}\) C.

An isolated sphere. \(V = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R)\), so

\[ C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R . \]

A sphere of 1 m has 111 pF; at 1 MV it holds \(1.11 \times 10^{-4}\) C.

Capacitors in parallel and in series. Connected side by side (in parallel), capacitors share one voltage, and their charges add: \(C = C_1 + C_2 + \dots\). Connected end to end (in series), they carry the same charge (what leaves one plate arrives on the next), and their voltages add, \(V = Q/C_1 + Q/C_2 + \dots\), so

\[ \frac{1}{C} = \frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2} + \dots \]

A series stack is weaker in capacitance but shares the voltage, so it can stand a voltage no single capacitor could. Four capacitors of 20 nF, each rated for 100 kV, in series make 5 nF that can stand 400 kV. Stacks like this are the backbone of the Cockcroft–Walton multiplier (chapter 5, and its pre-requisite on capacitor networks).

Energy stored. Charging a capacitor takes work, because each new bit of charge \(dq\) must be pushed onto a plate already at the voltage \(q/C\). From nothing to \(Q\):

\[ U = \int_0^Q\frac{q}{C}\,dq = \frac{Q^2}{2C} = \tfrac12CV^2 . \]

The factor \(\tfrac12\) is there because the voltage rose from 0 to \(V\) during the charging, and the average was \(V/2\). The 1 m terminal at 1 MV stores \(\tfrac12 \times 1.11 \times 10^{-10} \times (10^6)^2 = 55.6\) J, the energy of a spark when it flashes over. The 5 nF stack at 400 kV stores 400 J, enough to kill, which is why a high-voltage set is earthed with a grounding stick before anyone touches it.

The energy is in the field. Where is it stored? For parallel plates, \(\tfrac12CV^2 = \tfrac12(\varepsilon_0A/d)(\mathcal{E}d)^2 = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}^2 \times Ad\), and \(Ad\) is the volume between the plates, where the field is. So the field carries an energy per unit volume

\[ u_E = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}^2 \quad [\text{J/m}^3] . \]

This holds for any field, not only between plates: integrating \(u_E\) over all space round the 1 m sphere, with \(\mathcal{E} = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)\) and shells of volume \(4\pi r^2dr\), gives \(Q^2/(8\pi\varepsilon_0R) = 55.6\) J again. Air at its breakdown field of 3 MV/m holds only 40 J/m\(^3\); that low ceiling is why electrostatic machines are big. The energy density is also a pressure: the field pulls on a charged conductor’s surface with \(\tfrac12\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}^2\) newtons per square metre, Faraday’s stretched lines made exact. Chapter 1’s pre-requisite on capacitance and field energy uses these results for the electroscope and the terminal.

7. Dielectrics, briefly

The picture. Fill the gap of a capacitor with an insulator. Its electrons cannot leave their molecules, but the field pulls them a little one way and the nuclei a little the other: each molecule becomes a small dipole (section 3), and some molecules, like water, are dipoles already and turn to line up. The material is polarised. Its dipoles’ positive ends crowd against the negative plate and their negative ends against the positive plate, and these bound surface charges partly cancel the plates’ charge. The field inside, for the same charge on the plates, is smaller by a factor \(\varepsilon_r\), the relative permittivity (or dielectric constant), and so is the voltage. The capacitance is larger by the same factor:

\[ C = \frac{\varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0A}{d} . \]

Numbers. \(\varepsilon_r\) is 1.0006 for air, about 2.3 for polyethylene, 4 to 10 for glasses and ceramics, about 80 for water at low frequency. Chapter 2’s plates filled with polyethylene would have 1.6 pF instead of 0.71 pF.

Why it matters here. Insulators hold the high-voltage parts of an accelerator apart. Each has a dielectric strength, the field at which it breaks down, and in a stack of different insulators the field divides unevenly, crowding into the material with the lower \(\varepsilon_r\), often the gas or vacuum at a joint, which then fails first. The full treatment, with the displacement field \(\mathbf{D}\) and the boundary conditions, is chapter 4’s pre-requisite on dielectrics.

8. Current, current density and Ohm’s law

Current. A current is charge on the move. The current through a surface is the charge crossing it per second,

\[ I = \frac{dQ}{dt}, \]

in amperes (A \(=\) C/s). By convention it flows the way positive charge would move; in a metal wire the electrons actually go the other way, and in an electron beam the current runs backwards from the beam. A beam of 1 mA carries \(10^{-3}/1.602 \times 10^{-19} = 6.24 \times 10^{15}\) particles of charge \(e\) each second.

Current density and drift velocity. In a wire of cross-section \(A\), let there be \(n\) free charges per cubic metre, each of charge \(q\), drifting along at an average speed \(v_d\). In a time \(dt\) the charges within a length \(v_d\,dt\) cross a given plane, a number \(nAv_d\,dt\), so

\[ I = nqv_dA, \qquad \mathbf{J} = nq\mathbf{v}_d, \]

where the current density \(\mathbf{J}\) is the current per unit area, in A/m\(^2\), a vector along the flow. Copper has about one free electron per atom, \(n = 8.49 \times 10^{28}\) m\(^{-3}\). A current of 1 A in a wire of 1 mm\(^2\) needs

\[ v_d = \frac{I}{neA} = \frac{1}{8.49 \times 10^{28} \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \times 10^{-6}} = 7.4 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{m/s}, \]

0.07 mm a second. The electrons crawl; yet a lamp lights at once when switched on, because the field that pushes them is set up along the whole wire at nearly the speed of light, and all the electrons start moving together. In a beam it is the other way round: few particles, at nearly the speed of light. The 10 mA, 2 kV beam has only \(\lambda/e = 2.4 \times 10^9\) electrons per metre.

Conservation of charge, at a point. Take any closed surface. The current flowing out through it, \(\oint\mathbf{J}\cdot d\mathbf{A}\), must equal the rate at which the charge inside falls. With Gauss’s theorem (F2, section 10), and \(\rho\) the charge density,

\[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = -\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} . \]

This is the continuity equation: current flowing out of a point empties it of charge. Section 16 needs it.

Ohm’s law. In most conductors the current is proportional to the voltage driving it:

\[ V = IR, \]

with \(R\) the resistance in ohms (Ω \(=\) V/A). The picture: the field pushes each electron, but every \(10^{-14}\) s or so it collides with the vibrating ions of the crystal and loses its forward motion, so it reaches an average drift speed proportional to the field, as a ball falling through treacle reaches a steady speed. In terms of the material, \(\mathbf{J} = \sigma\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) with \(\sigma\) the conductivity, and a wire of length \(L\) and area \(A\) has

\[ R = \rho\frac{L}{A}, \]

with \(\rho = 1/\sigma\) the resistivity in Ω m (the same Greek letter as charge density; context tells them apart). Copper at room temperature has \(\rho = 1.68 \times 10^{-8}\) Ω m; 100 m of 1 mm\(^2\) copper wire has \(1.68 \times 10^{-8} \times 100/10^{-6} = 1.68\) Ω. Ohm’s law is not a law of nature like Coulomb’s but a property of many materials; it fails for diodes, vacuum tubes, gas discharges and beams, whose current depends on voltage in other ways. At low temperatures some metals lose their resistance completely: superconductors, which carry the LHC’s currents (chapter 17).

Power. A charge \(dQ\) falling through a voltage \(V\) gives up energy \(V\,dQ\); per second that is

\[ P = IV = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R} \]

in watts. In a resistor it becomes heat, in a beam it becomes the particles’ energy. Ten amperes through the 1.68 Ω wire above waste 168 W. The 10 mA beam from 2 kV carries \(0.01 \times 2000 = 20\) W, all of it delivered as heat where it stops; a 1 mA beam at 1 MeV carries 1 kW, and stopping it in a few millimetres of metal takes water cooling.

9. Circuits: Kirchhoff’s laws, and RC

Kirchhoff’s laws. A circuit is a network of wires and components. Two rules, given by Gustav Kirchhoff in 1845, solve any network.

  • The junction rule. At any junction the currents flowing in add up to the currents flowing out. This is conservation of charge: in a steady state, charge cannot pile up at a point.
  • The loop rule. Round any closed loop the voltages add up to zero. This is the fact that a static field is the gradient of a potential (F2, section 7): go round a loop and you come back to the same height. (Section 13 shows where it fails: in a loop whose magnetic flux is changing.)

Resistors in series and in parallel. In series, the same current passes through each and the voltages add: \(R = R_1 + R_2 + \dots\). In parallel, they share a voltage and the currents add:

\[ \frac{1}{R} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \dots \]

The opposite of the capacitor rules, because a capacitor’s voltage is \(Q/C\) while a resistor’s is \(IR\).

Example: a voltage divider. How do you measure a million volts with a meter that reads a thousand? Connect a chain of resistors totalling 1000 MΩ from the high-voltage terminal to a 1 MΩ resistor to ground. The same current, \(10^6/(1.001 \times 10^9) = 1.0\) mA, flows through both, and the small resistor has \(999\) V across it, a thousandth of the total, which an ordinary meter reads (chapter 4). The divider draws 1 mA from the terminal and dissipates 1 kW, which is why real ones use larger resistances.

The RC circuit. Connect a charged capacitor \(C\), at voltage \(V_0\), across a resistor \(R\). Current flows, \(I = V/R\), and it drains the capacitor: \(dQ/dt = -I\). With \(Q = CV\),

\[ C\frac{dV}{dt} = -\frac{V}{R} \quad\Rightarrow\quad V = V_0e^{-t/RC} . \]

This is F1’s decay equation (section 10) with the time constant \(\tau = RC\) (ohms times farads is seconds). After one \(\tau\) the voltage has fallen to 37 per cent, after \(\tau\ln2 = 0.69\tau\) to half, after \(4.6\tau\) to 1 per cent. Charging through \(R\) from a fixed voltage \(V_0\) runs the other way: \(V = V_0(1 - e^{-t/RC})\).

Example: the electrostatic generator. A belt carries a steady charging current \(I\) up to the 111 pF terminal of a 1 m sphere. To spread the voltage evenly, a resistor column of \(R = 10^{11}\) Ω runs from the terminal to ground, and leaks a current \(V/R\). The terminal’s charge grows at the difference:

\[ C\frac{dV}{dt} = I - \frac{V}{R} \quad\Rightarrow\quad V = IR\,(1 - e^{-t/RC}) . \]

With \(I = 10\) μA, the voltage starts rising at \(I/C = 90\) kV/s and settles at \(IR = 1\) MV, with \(\tau = RC = 1.11 \times 10^{-10} \times 10^{11} = 11.1\) s. It reaches 90 per cent of the final voltage after \(\tau\ln10 = 25.6\) s. A real machine also feeds the beam current and the corona losses, which add to the leak (chapter 6). Inductors, alternating currents and phasors are chapter 4’s pre-requisite on RLC circuits and F5.

10. The magnetic field, and its force

The picture. A compass needle near a wire turns when a current flows (Ørsted, 1820). Two parallel wires carrying currents the same way attract; opposite ways, they repel. Moving charges, and only moving charges, make a magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\), and feel one. A permanent magnet is no exception: its field comes from the electrons inside it, each spinning like a tiny current loop, all lined up (section 15).

The force on a moving charge. F2 (section 3) gave it: a charge \(q\) moving with velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) feels

\[ \mathbf{F} = q\,\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}, \]

at right angles to both the velocity and the field. The tesla is defined by it: 1 T is the field in which 1 C moving at 1 m/s across the field feels 1 N. The Earth’s field is about 50 μT, a fridge magnet’s about 5 mT, an iron electromagnet’s up to about 2 T, a superconducting magnet’s 8 to 16 T. Since the force is always sideways, it does no work: a magnetic field bends a charged particle’s path but never changes its speed (F2, section 4). In a uniform field a particle goes round a circle of radius \(R = p/(qB)\) at the cyclotron frequency \(qB/(2\pi m)\), the basis of chapter 10 and of every ring.

The force on a wire. A current is many moving charges. In a length \(L\) of wire there are \(nAL\) charges, each with velocity \(\mathbf{v}_d\), and the total force is \(nAL\,q\,\mathbf{v}_d\times\mathbf{B}\). Since \(nqv_dA = I\),

\[ \mathbf{F} = I\,\mathbf{L}\times\mathbf{B}, \]

with \(\mathbf{L}\) along the current. A wire of 1 m carrying 100 A across 1 T is pushed with 100 N. An LHC dipole conductor, carrying 11 850 A in 8.33 T, is pushed with \(11\,850 \times 8.33 = 98.7\) kN per metre, the weight of ten tonnes: the coils of a strong magnet must be clamped like a pressure vessel (chapter 17). This force on a current is how every electric motor works.

Two wires. A wire carrying \(I_1\) sits in the field \(\mu_0I_2/(2\pi d)\) of a parallel wire a distance \(d\) away (section 12), and so feels \(\mu_0I_1I_2/(2\pi d)\) per metre. For 1 A in each, 1 m apart, that is \(2 \times 10^{-7}\) N per metre; until 2019 this defined the ampere. Two beams of particles moving the same way are two currents that attract, while their charges repel; at the speed of light the two nearly cancel, which is why fast beams suffer less from their own space charge (chapter 22).

A current loop is a magnet. A flat loop of area \(A\) carrying current \(I\), in a uniform field, feels no net force (the pushes on opposite sides cancel) but feels a turning force, a torque, of up to \(IAB\), which turns it to face across the field. It behaves like a compass needle with magnetic moment \(m = IA\), in A m\(^2\). That is the principle of the moving-coil meter, and on the atomic scale the electron’s spin gives it a magnetic moment of its own (chapter 7’s pre-requisite on spin, and F7).

11. Where magnetic fields come from: Biot–Savart

The law. Biot and Savart found, and Ampère’s theory confirmed, that a short piece \(d\mathbf{l}\) of wire carrying current \(I\) makes at a point a distance \(r\) away the field

\[ d\mathbf{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,d\mathbf{l}\times\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{r^2}, \]

where \(\hat{\mathbf{r}}\) points from the wire piece to the point, and \(\mu_0 = 1.2566 \times 10^{-6}\) T m/A is the permeability of free space. Like Coulomb’s law, the field falls as \(1/r^2\); unlike it, it points at right angles to both the current and the line to the point, so the field lines circle round the current. The direction follows the right-hand rule: thumb along the current, fingers curl the way \(\mathbf{B}\) goes. The whole field of a circuit is the sum (integral) of the pieces, by superposition.

A loop, on its axis. A circular loop of radius \(R\) carrying \(I\); find the field at a point on its axis, a distance \(z\) from its centre. Every piece \(d\mathbf{l}\) is at the same distance \(\sqrt{R^2 + z^2}\) from the point, and at right angles to the line joining them, so each makes a field of size \(\mu_0I\,dl/(4\pi(R^2 + z^2))\). These fields point in a cone round the axis; going round the loop, their sideways parts cancel in opposite pairs, and only the part along the axis survives, a fraction \(R/\sqrt{R^2 + z^2}\) of each. Adding round the loop, \(\int dl = 2\pi R\):

\[ B = \frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\,\frac{2\pi R}{R^2 + z^2}\,\frac{R}{\sqrt{R^2 + z^2}} = \frac{\mu_0IR^2}{2(R^2 + z^2)^{3/2}} . \]

At the centre, \(z = 0\), it is \(\mu_0I/(2R)\). A coil of \(N\) turns multiplies this by \(N\). A coil of 100 turns of radius 20 cm carrying 1 A makes \(1.2566 \times 10^{-6} \times 100/(2 \times 0.2) = 314\) μT at its centre, six times the Earth’s field, and at \(z = R\) only \(2^{-3/2} = 0.354\) of that, 111 μT. Two such coils one radius apart (a Helmholtz pair) give a field that is very uniform in the middle, 450 μT here; J. J. Thomson used a pair to bend his cathode rays, and chapter 2 uses one to cancel the Earth’s field over a bench (chapter 2’s pre-requisite).

12. Ampère’s law

The law. For steady currents, F2 (section 11) gave the circulation of \(\mathbf{B}\) round any closed loop:

\[ \oint_C\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \mu_0I_{\text{enc}}, \]

where \(I_{\text{enc}}\) is the current passing through any surface bounded by the loop. With Stokes’s theorem this is \(\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{J}\). It follows from Biot–Savart (Griffiths, section 5.3) and plays the part for magnetic fields that Gauss’s law plays for electric ones: always true for steady currents, and quick to use when symmetry gives the field’s direction and makes its size constant along a suitable loop.

A long straight wire. A circle of radius \(r\) round the wire: \(B\cdot2\pi r = \mu_0I\), so

\[ B = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r} . \]

1 A at 1 cm gives 20 μT. A beam is a current too: a 1 A proton beam has 20 μT at 1 cm from it, which a beam current monitor picks up (chapter 8).

A long solenoid. A coil wound tightly along a long tube, with \(n\) turns per metre each carrying \(I\). Inside, the field runs along the axis and is uniform; outside, the fields of the two sides of each turn nearly cancel, and it is very small. Take a rectangular loop with one side of length \(\ell\) inside along the axis and the opposite side outside. Only the inside side contributes, \(B\ell\); the sides crossing the winding are at right angles to the field. The loop encloses \(n\ell\) turns, each with current \(I\):

\[ B\ell = \mu_0n\ell I \quad\Rightarrow\quad B = \mu_0nI . \]

The field does not depend on the coil’s radius or on where inside you are. 2000 turns per metre with 5 A give \(1.2566 \times 10^{-6} \times 2000 \times 5 = 12.6\) mT. Solenoids of this kind focus the beams of electron guns and low-energy linacs (chapter 7).

Why air coils are not enough. For 1.5 T, a typical cyclotron field, an air-cored solenoid needs \(nI = B/\mu_0 = 1.19 \times 10^6\) ampere-turns per metre. Copper at a safe current density of a few A/mm\(^2\) would need a winding half a metre thick per metre of length, and the power to drive it. Iron (section 15) reduces this by a large factor.

Where it fails. Ampère’s law in this form holds only for steady currents. When charge is piling up somewhere, as on a capacitor plate while it charges, it gives contradictory answers; section 16 shows the cure.

13. Magnetic flux, and \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0\)

Flux. The magnetic flux through a surface is the flux of \(\mathbf{B}\) (F2, section 9):

\[ \Phi_B = \int_S\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{A}, \]

in webers (Wb \(=\) T m\(^2\)). For a uniform field straight through a flat loop of area \(A\), \(\Phi_B = BA\). A field of 0.5 T through an orbit of radius 0.1 m is a flux of \(0.5 \times \pi \times 0.1^2 = 0.0157\) Wb.

No magnetic charges. Break a bar magnet in two and you get two magnets, each with a north and a south pole; no one has ever isolated a single magnetic pole. Magnetic field lines never begin or end: they close on themselves, running round currents. So the magnetic flux out of any closed surface is zero:

\[ \oint_S\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{A} = 0, \qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0 . \]

This is the second of Maxwell’s equations, the magnetic Gauss’s law. One consequence is used all the time: the flux through a loop is the same for every surface that has the loop as its edge, so “the flux through a loop” has one meaning. Another is that the flux in an iron yoke must come back round: what crosses a magnet’s gap returns through the iron (chapter 17).

14. Induction: Faraday and Lenz

The discovery. Faraday wound two coils on one iron ring. A steady current in the first did nothing to the second; but at the moment the first was switched on or off, a meter on the second kicked. A magnet pushed into a coil did the same, and a magnet held still did nothing. What counts is change.

Faraday’s law. Round any loop, the electromotive force (EMF), the work done per unit charge by the electric field going once round, is

\[ \text{EMF} = \oint_C\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}, \]

minus the rate of change of the magnetic flux through the loop. EMF is in volts: 1 V is 1 Wb/s. For a coil of \(N\) turns the flux passes through each turn and the EMF is \(N\) times larger. With Stokes’s theorem it is the third of Maxwell’s equations:

\[ \nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t} . \]

What it means. A static electric field has no circulation (section 5): carried round a loop, a charge gains nothing. A changing magnetic field makes an electric field whose lines close on themselves, swirling round the changing flux, and a charge carried round gains energy every time. No wire is needed; the field is there in empty space. Kirchhoff’s loop rule fails in such a loop. This is the one way round the rule that a static field cannot give energy on a closed path.

Lenz’s rule. The minus sign says which way: the EMF drives a current whose own magnetic field opposes the change of flux. Push a magnet’s north pole into a coil, and the induced current makes the coil’s near face a north pole, pushing back. If the sign were the other way, the induced current would increase the flux, which would induce more current, and energy would come from nothing. Lenz’s rule is conservation of energy.

Example: the betatron. An electron goes round an orbit of radius 0.1 m inside which the average field rises by 0.5 T in 2 ms. The flux changes by \(0.0157\) Wb, so the EMF round the orbit is \(0.0157/0.002 = 7.85\) V, an electric field of \(7.85/(2\pi \times 0.1) = 12.5\) V/m along the orbit. Small; but an electron near the speed of light goes round \(9.5 \times 10^5\) times in the 2 ms, gaining 7.85 eV each time, about 7.5 MeV in all. This is Kerst’s betatron (chapter 11).

Eddy currents. A changing field through a solid lump of metal drives currents round inside it, which heat it and, by Lenz’s rule, oppose the change. That is why the iron of a pulsed or ramped magnet is built of thin insulated sheets (laminations), which cut the current paths; and why a beam pipe of metal inside a fast-ramping magnet must be thin or made of ceramic. Fields at radio frequencies cannot enter a good conductor more than a few micrometres: the skin effect of chapter 13’s pre-requisite on fields in conductors.

Motional EMF. A metal rod of length \(L\) slides at speed \(v\) along two rails, across a field \(B\) at right angles to rod, rails and motion. Each free charge \(q\) in the rod moves with the rod and feels \(qvB\) along the rod, and so is pushed from one end to the other. The work done per unit charge along the rod is

\[ \text{EMF} = BLv . \]

The same answer comes from Faraday’s law: the circuit of rod and rails grows in area by \(Lv\) each second, so \(d\Phi_B/dt = BLv\). Faraday’s law covers both a field that changes and a circuit that moves. An aircraft with a 60 m wingspan flying at 250 m/s through a vertical Earth’s field of 50 μT has 0.75 V between its wing tips. A coil of \(N\) turns and area \(A\) spinning at angular rate \(\omega\) in a field \(B\) sees a flux \(BA\cos\omega t\) and an EMF \(NBA\omega\sin\omega t\): the alternating-current generator. With 100 turns of 0.01 m\(^2\) in 0.5 T at 50 Hz, the peak EMF is 157 V.

The deeper reading of Faraday’s law in chapter 1 adds the “two-to-one rule” of the betatron and the induction linac.

15. Inductance, magnetic energy, LR circuits and iron

Self-inductance. A current \(I\) in a coil makes a field, and so a flux through its own turns, proportional to \(I\). The ratio of the total flux linkage to the current is the coil’s inductance:

\[ L = \frac{N\Phi_B}{I}, \]

in henries (H \(=\) Wb/A). When the current changes, so does the flux, and Faraday’s law puts an EMF across the coil that opposes the change:

\[ V = L\frac{dI}{dt} . \]

An inductor resists changes of current as a mass resists changes of speed. For a long solenoid of \(N\) turns, length \(\ell\) and cross-section \(A\), \(B = \mu_0NI/\ell\) and \(\Phi_B = BA\), so

\[ L = \frac{\mu_0N^2A}{\ell} . \]

1000 turns on a tube of radius 2 cm and length 50 cm have \(L = 3.16\) mH.

Energy stored. To raise the current, a supply must push against the back EMF, delivering power \(VI = LI\,dI/dt\). From zero to \(I\):

\[ U = \int_0^IL\,i\,di = \tfrac12LI^2 . \]

As with the capacitor, the energy is in the field. For the solenoid, \(\tfrac12LI^2 = \tfrac12(\mu_0N^2A/\ell)I^2 = (B^2/2\mu_0)\times A\ell\), and \(A\ell\) is the volume inside, so a magnetic field stores

\[ u_B = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0} \quad [\text{J/m}^3] . \]

At 1 T that is 0.40 MJ per cubic metre; at the LHC’s 8.33 T, 27.6 MJ per cubic metre. Compare air’s 40 J/m\(^3\) at its electric breakdown field: a magnetic field stores some ten thousand times more energy per volume than any electric field in air, which is why rings bend with magnets, not electrodes (chapter 1). The energy density is also a pressure, \(B^2/2\mu_0\), pushing the coils of a magnet outwards. One LHC dipole, with \(L = 98.7\) mH at 11 850 A, stores \(\tfrac12 \times 0.0987 \times 11\,850^2 = 6.93\) MJ; if a superconducting coil loses its superconductivity, that energy must be taken out safely in less than a second (chapter 17).

The LR circuit. Connect a coil of inductance \(L\) and resistance \(R\) to a supply of fixed voltage \(V_0\). The loop rule, now with the coil’s EMF included, gives \(V_0 = IR + L\,dI/dt\). The current rises towards \(V_0/R\) as

\[ I = \frac{V_0}{R}\,(1 - e^{-tR/L}), \]

with time constant \(\tau = L/R\); switch the supply out (with the coil shorted through its resistance) and it decays as \(e^{-tR/L}\). A magnet of 0.5 H and 0.1 Ω on a 100 V supply has \(\tau = 5\) s, and settles at 1000 A after about \(\tau\ln10 = 11.5\) s to reach 90 per cent; its initial rate of rise is \(V_0/L = 200\) A/s. The bigger the inductance, the more voltage it takes to ramp the current fast. The Bevatron’s magnet stored 78 MJ at 8400 A, an inductance of \(2 \times 78 \times 10^6/8400^2 = 2.2\) H; with its 15 kV supply, the current could rise at most at \(15\,000/2.2 = 6800\) A/s, and the ramp to full current took at least 1.2 s (chapter 15).

Magnetic materials and iron. In most materials, the atoms’ magnetic moments (section 10) are weak or point at random, and the material hardly changes a field. In iron, nickel and cobalt (ferromagnets) the moments line up with each other in regions called domains, and an applied field turns whole domains to point along it. The iron’s own field then adds to the coil’s and multiplies it by the relative permeability \(\mu_r\), hundreds to thousands. The iron’s contribution is limited: once every domain is lined up, the iron is saturated, at about 2 T, and further current adds only what an air coil would. And a ferromagnet remembers: after the current is switched off, some field remains (hysteresis), which is why accelerator magnets are cycled the same way each time (chapter 15).

The iron electromagnet. An accelerator dipole is a C or H shape of iron with a gap of height \(g\) where the beam goes, driven by a coil of \(N\) turns carrying \(I\). Ampère’s law round the iron and across the gap is easiest written with the field \(H = B/(\mu_0\mu_r)\), in A/m, whose circulation is the current: \(\oint H\,dl = NI\). In the iron, \(\mu_r\) is large and \(H\) tiny; nearly all of \(NI\) is spent across the gap, where \(H = B/\mu_0\). So

\[ B \approx \frac{\mu_0NI}{g} . \]

The iron’s job is to carry the flux round and back, cheaply; the gap sets the ampere-turns. A cyclotron magnet with 1.5 T across a 10 cm gap needs \(NI = Bg/\mu_0 = 1.19 \times 10^5\) ampere-turns, a tenth of what the metre-long air solenoid of section 12 needed per metre. Above 2 T the iron saturates, and the LHC’s 8.33 T comes from superconducting coils, with the iron only as a return path. The full theory is chapter 8’s pre-requisite on magnetic circuits and iron.

16. The displacement current, and Maxwell’s four equations

A gap in Ampère’s law. Charge a capacitor through a wire carrying current \(I\). Draw a loop round the wire. Ampère’s law says the circulation of \(\mathbf{B}\) round it is \(\mu_0I\), where \(I\) is the current through any surface bounded by the loop. A flat disc is cut by the wire: \(\mu_0I\). But a bag-shaped surface with the same edge, passing between the capacitor’s plates, is cut by no current at all: zero. The law gives two answers. The same trouble appears in the mathematics: the divergence of a curl is zero (F2, section 11), so \(\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{J}\) demands \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = 0\), which contradicts the continuity equation of section 8 wherever charge is piling up.

Maxwell’s cure. Between the plates there is no current, but there is a changing electric field. As the plates charge, \(\mathcal{E} = Q/(\varepsilon_0A)\) grows, and \(\varepsilon_0A\,d\mathcal{E}/dt = dQ/dt = I\): the changing field through the gap carries exactly the current that the wire carries to the plate. Maxwell added this displacement current to Ampère’s law:

\[ \oint_C\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \mu_0I_{\text{enc}} + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt}, \]

with \(\Phi_E = \int\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot d\mathbf{A}\) the electric flux through the surface. Now both surfaces give \(\mu_0I\). In differential form,

\[ \nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{J} + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}{\partial t}, \]

and taking the divergence of both sides now gives \(0 = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} + \partial\rho/\partial t\), the continuity equation: the new term is exactly what charge conservation requires. In words, a changing electric field makes a magnetic field, just as a changing magnetic field makes an electric one.

Example: an RF gap. In an accelerating cavity the field across a gap of area 0.01 m\(^2\) swings with amplitude 1 MV/m at 200 MHz, \(\mathcal{E} = \mathcal{E}_0\sin\omega t\) with \(\omega = 2\pi \times 200 \times 10^6\) s\(^{-1}\). The displacement current through the gap has amplitude \(\varepsilon_0\omega\mathcal{E}_0A = 8.854 \times 10^{-12} \times 1.257 \times 10^9 \times 10^6 \times 0.01 = 111\) A. It makes a magnetic field circling the gap, and real currents of the same size flow in the cavity’s walls: at radio frequencies the displacement current is not small at all (chapter 13). In a steady or slowly changing circuit, it is negligible, and Ampère’s original law is fine.

Maxwell’s equations. All of electromagnetism, in four equations. In integral form, for any closed surface \(S\) or any loop \(C\) bounding a surface:

\[ \oint_S\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot d\mathbf{A} = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0} \]

\[ \oint_S\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{A} = 0 \]

\[ \oint_C\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \]

\[ \oint_C\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \mu_0I_{\text{enc}} + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} \]

and in differential form, at every point:

\[ \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0, \]

\[ \nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}, \qquad \nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{J} + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}{\partial t} . \]

What each says:

  1. Gauss’s law. Electric field lines start and end on charges; the flux out of a surface counts the charge inside. (Coulomb’s law, section 4.)
  2. No magnetic charges. Magnetic field lines never end; they close on themselves. (Section 13.)
  3. Faraday’s law. A changing magnetic field makes a circulating electric field. When nothing changes, the electric field has no circulation and is the gradient of a potential. (Section 14.)
  4. The Ampère–Maxwell law. Currents and changing electric fields make a circulating magnetic field. (Sections 12 and 16.)

Add the Lorentz force, \(\mathbf{F} = q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})\), which says what the fields do to charges, and nothing else is needed for the classical theory: every field in every chapter of this course is a solution of these equations for some arrangement of charges, currents and boundaries. The equations are linear in the fields and sources, so superposition holds. They also hold unchanged at any speed: special relativity was found in 1905 by taking them seriously (chapter 1’s pre-requisite on relativistic motion). Inside matter they are often written with \(\mathbf{D}\) and \(\mathbf{H}\) to hide the bound charges and currents of sections 7 and 15; the forms above, with all charges and currents counted, are always true.

17. Electromagnetic waves

Why there must be waves. By equations 3 and 4, a changing magnetic field makes an electric field, and a changing electric field makes a magnetic field. Each can keep the other going, with no charges at all, and the disturbance moves off through empty space. Here is the simplest case, done with F1’s partial derivatives.

The wave equation. In empty space (\(\rho = 0\), \(\mathbf{J} = 0\)), suppose the electric field points along \(y\) and the magnetic field along \(z\), and both depend only on \(x\) and \(t\): \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = (0, \mathcal{E}_y(x, t), 0)\) and \(\mathbf{B} = (0, 0, B_z(x, t))\). Both divergences are zero. The curls, from F2’s formula with only \(x\) derivatives surviving, are \(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = (0, 0, \partial\mathcal{E}_y/\partial x)\) and \(\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = (0, -\partial B_z/\partial x, 0)\). So equations 3 and 4 become

\[ \frac{\partial\mathcal{E}_y}{\partial x} = -\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial t}, \qquad \frac{\partial B_z}{\partial x} = -\mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}_y}{\partial t} . \]

Differentiate the first with respect to \(x\), and use the second (the order of partial derivatives does not matter, F1 section 9):

\[ \frac{\partial^2\mathcal{E}_y}{\partial x^2} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial x} = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\mathcal{E}_y}{\partial t^2} . \]

This is the wave equation. Any shape \(f\) moving along \(x\) at speed \(v\), \(\mathcal{E}_y = f(x - vt)\), satisfies it if \(v^2 = 1/(\mu_0\varepsilon_0)\): by the chain rule, \(\partial^2/\partial x^2\) gives \(f''\) and \(\partial^2/\partial t^2\) gives \(v^2f''\). So the field moves at

\[ c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1.2566 \times 10^{-6} \times 8.854 \times 10^{-12}}} = 2.998 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}, \]

the speed of light, computed from the constants of electricity and magnetism. This was Maxwell’s discovery: light is an electromagnetic wave. Radio waves, the RF power that feeds cavities, X-rays and the synchrotron radiation of chapter 20 are the same thing at other wavelengths.

The magnetic part. Putting \(\mathcal{E}_y = f(x - ct)\) into the first equation, \(\partial B_z/\partial t = -f'\), which is satisfied by \(B_z = f(x - ct)/c\). So in the wave the two fields are in step, at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel, with

\[ \mathcal{E} = cB . \]

An electric field of 1 kV/m goes with a magnetic field of only 3.3 μT. F5 develops waves fully, with sine waves, wavelength and frequency, and chapter 13’s pre-requisite on Maxwell’s equations and the wave equation takes them into cavities and waveguides.

18. Energy flow: the Poynting vector

Energy on the move. Fields store energy, \(\tfrac12\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}^2 + B^2/2\mu_0\) per cubic metre (sections 6 and 15). When fields change, that energy moves. Poynting showed, from Maxwell’s equations, that the energy flows with the vector

\[ \mathbf{S} = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\times\mathbf{B}, \]

whose size is the power crossing a square metre at right angles to it, in W/m\(^2\). Its direction, at right angles to both fields, is the direction the energy travels. The derivation, and the full energy balance called Poynting’s theorem, is in chapter 13’s pre-requisite on Poynting’s theorem.

In a wave. With \(\mathcal{E} = cB\), the two energy densities are equal: \(B^2/2\mu_0 = \mathcal{E}^2/(2\mu_0c^2) = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}^2\). The wave of section 17 has \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) along \(y\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) along \(z\), so \(\mathbf{S}\) points along \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\times\hat{\mathbf{z}} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}\), the way the wave goes, with size \(\mathcal{E}B/\mu_0 = \varepsilon_0c\,\mathcal{E}^2\): the energy density, \(\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}^2\), carried at speed \(c\). For a sine wave of amplitude \(\mathcal{E}_0\) the average of \(\sin^2\) is \(\tfrac12\) (F1), so the average power per area, the intensity, is \(\tfrac12\varepsilon_0c\mathcal{E}_0^2\). Sunlight at the Earth delivers 1361 W/m\(^2\), so

\[ \mathcal{E}_0 = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 1361}{8.854 \times 10^{-12} \times 2.998 \times 10^8}} = 1013\ \text{V/m}, \]

with a magnetic amplitude of \(\mathcal{E}_0/c = 3.4\) μT.

In a wire. The Poynting vector says something surprising about an ordinary wire. Take 10 A in 1 mm\(^2\) copper (radius 0.564 mm), with 0.0168 Ω per metre. Along the wire there is a field \(\mathcal{E} = IR/\ell = 0.168\) V/m pushing the current; round it, \(B = \mu_0I/(2\pi r) = 3.54\) mT at the surface. \(\mathbf{S}\) points radially into the wire, with size \(\mathcal{E}B/\mu_0 = 474\) W/m\(^2\). Over a metre of surface, \(2\pi r\) square metres, that is \(474 \times 2\pi \times 0.000564 = 1.68\) W, exactly the \(I^2R\) heating of a metre of wire. The energy that heats a wire does not travel inside it with the electrons; it flows in through the space round it, from the fields. In a coaxial cable or a waveguide carrying RF power to a cavity, the energy likewise travels in the field between the conductors, and the metal only guides it (chapter 13).

The physics is the same; the equations look different by factors of \(4\pi\) and \(c\). This course uses SI throughout, but the accelerator literature quotes fields in kilogauss (1 kG \(= 0.1\) T) well into the 1970s.

Exercises

  1. An alpha particle (charge \(2e\)) is 30 fm from the centre of a gold nucleus (charge \(79e\)). Find the force on it and the electric potential energy, in MeV.
Answer

\(F = 8.988 \times 10^9 \times 2 \times 79 \times (1.602 \times 10^{-19})^2/(30 \times 10^{-15})^2 = 40.5\) N. \(U = 2 \times 79 \times 1.44\ \text{MeV fm}/30\ \text{fm} = 7.58\) MeV. An alpha from a radioactive source, with 5 to 8 MeV, can get this close but no closer: Rutherford’s scattering (chapter 1).

  1. An electrostatic generator’s terminal is a sphere of radius 0.5 m in air, which breaks down at 3 MV/m. What is the highest voltage it can hold, the charge on it then, and its capacitance?
Answer

Surface field \(= V/R\), so \(V_{\max} = 3 \times 10^6 \times 0.5 = 1.5\) MV. \(C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R = 55.6\) pF, and \(Q = CV = 8.34 \times 10^{-5}\) C. Doubling the radius doubles the voltage; that, and pressurised gas, is why such machines grew large (chapter 6).

  1. A proton is accelerated from rest through 1 MV. What is its kinetic energy in eV and in joules, and its speed? Is the non-relativistic formula good enough?
Answer

1 MeV \(= 1.602 \times 10^{-13}\) J. \(v = \sqrt{2K/m_p} = \sqrt{2 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-13}/1.673 \times 10^{-27}} = 1.384 \times 10^7\) m/s, 4.6 per cent of \(c\). The relativistic answer is \(1.383 \times 10^7\) m/s, 0.08 per cent lower: 1 MeV is only a thousandth of the proton’s 938 MeV rest energy, so \(\tfrac12mv^2\) is good enough.

  1. Three 30 nF capacitors, each rated for 100 kV, are connected (a) in parallel and (b) in series. Give the capacitance, the highest voltage, and the energy stored at that voltage, in each case.
Answer
  1. Parallel: 90 nF, 100 kV, \(\tfrac12 \times 90 \times 10^{-9} \times 10^{10} = 450\) J. (b) Series: 10 nF, 300 kV, \(\tfrac12 \times 10^{-8} \times (3 \times 10^5)^2 = 450\) J. The energy is the same, three capacitors each holding 150 J; the arrangement only trades charge for voltage.
  1. A water-cooled copper bus bar of cross-section 5 cm\(^2\) and length 20 m carries 1000 A to a magnet. Find its resistance, the voltage across it, the power it wastes, and the electrons’ drift speed. (\(\rho = 1.68 \times 10^{-8}\) Ω m, \(n = 8.49 \times 10^{28}\) m\(^{-3}\).)
Answer

\(R = \rho L/A = 1.68 \times 10^{-8} \times 20/(5 \times 10^{-4}) = 6.72 \times 10^{-4}\) Ω. \(V = IR = 0.672\) V; \(P = I^2R = 672\) W. \(v_d = I/(neA) = 1000/(8.49 \times 10^{28} \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \times 5 \times 10^{-4}) = 1.47 \times 10^{-4}\) m/s.

  1. The terminal of a 1 m sphere (111 pF) is held at 1 MV and the belt is stopped. Its only leak is a resistor column of \(10^{11}\) Ω. How long until it is at 500 kV, and at 10 kV? What current flows at the start?
Answer

\(\tau = RC = 11.1\) s. \(V = V_0e^{-t/\tau}\), so half takes \(\tau\ln2 = 7.7\) s, and 1 per cent takes \(\tau\ln100 = 51\) s. The starting current is \(V_0/R = 10\) μA. A terminal can stay dangerously charged long after the machine is switched off; hence the grounding stick.

  1. A focusing solenoid on an electron gun has 500 turns wound along 10 cm of a tube 5 cm across, and carries 2 A. Find the field inside. Find also the field at the centre of a single flat coil of 500 turns, radius 5 cm, with the same current, and at 5 cm along its axis.
Answer

Solenoid: \(n = 5000\) per metre, \(B = \mu_0nI = 1.2566 \times 10^{-6} \times 5000 \times 2 = 12.6\) mT (strictly, a solenoid only twice as long as it is wide is not “long”: the field at its centre is less by the factor \(\ell/\sqrt{\ell^2 + D^2} = 0.89\), with \(D\) the diameter). Flat coil: \(B = \mu_0NI/(2R) = 1.2566 \times 10^{-6} \times 500 \times 2/0.1 = 12.6\) mT at the centre, and \(2^{-3/2}\) of that, 4.44 mT, at \(z = R = 5\) cm.

  1. A search coil of 50 turns and area 4 cm\(^2\) sits in the gap of a magnet whose field ramps at 1 T/s. What voltage does it give? How could you use it to measure the field?
Answer

EMF \(= NA\,dB/dt = 50 \times 4 \times 10^{-4} \times 1 = 0.020\) V. The voltage is the rate of change of \(NBA\); integrating it over time (electronically) gives \(NA\,\Delta B\), and so the change in field. This is how synchrotron magnets’ fields were tracked as they ramped (chapter 15).

  1. A room-temperature magnet has \(L = 0.5\) H and \(R = 0.1\) Ω and is connected to a 100 V supply at \(t = 0\). Find the final current, the time constant, the time to reach 90 per cent of the final current, and the energy stored at the end. What heat does the coil produce each second at full current?
Answer

\(I_{\text{final}} = V/R = 1000\) A. \(\tau = L/R = 5\) s; 90 per cent after \(\tau\ln10 = 11.5\) s. Stored energy \(\tfrac12LI^2 = \tfrac12 \times 0.5 \times 10^6 = 250\) kJ. Heat: \(I^2R = 100\) kW, all the supply’s power once the current is steady; hence water cooling.

  1. A radio wave has an electric field of amplitude 100 V/m. Find the magnetic field’s amplitude and the intensity. Check that \(1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}\) with the constants of this chapter gives the speed of light.
Answer

\(B_0 = \mathcal{E}_0/c = 100/2.998 \times 10^8 = 3.34 \times 10^{-7}\) T. Intensity \(\tfrac12\varepsilon_0c\mathcal{E}_0^2 = \tfrac12 \times 8.854 \times 10^{-12} \times 2.998 \times 10^8 \times 10^4 = 13.3\) W/m\(^2\). \(\mu_0\varepsilon_0 = 1.2566 \times 10^{-6} \times 8.854 \times 10^{-12} = 1.1127 \times 10^{-17}\) s\(^2\)/m\(^2\), whose inverse square root is \(2.998 \times 10^8\) m/s.
