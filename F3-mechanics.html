F3 Newtonian mechanics

1632 to 1835: Galileo’s falling bodies and moving ship, Huygens’s collisions and pendulum, Newton’s Principia, Leibniz’s living force, Euler’s equations and Coriolis’s work. How bodies move under forces: momentum and energy and why they are conserved, collisions seen from the laboratory and from the centre of mass, rotation and orbits, the oscillator, and the frames in which all of it holds. Every particle in an accelerator obeys these laws until it goes fast, and the course’s relativity starts from them.

What this chapter assumes. High-school physics (force, speed, acceleration, energy, \(F = ma\) in one direction), F1 Calculus (derivatives, integrals, Taylor series, the equations \(\dot N = -\lambda N\) and \(\ddot x = -\omega^2 x\)) and F2 Vectors and vector calculus (components, dot and cross products, velocity and acceleration as vectors, circular motion, the gradient, line integrals and conservative fields).

What comes next. F4 Electricity and magnetism supplies the forces; F5 Oscillations and waves takes the oscillator of sections 9 and 10 further with complex numbers. In the chapters, the pre-requisites on Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics and on Lorentz transformations in chapter 1 start where sections 11 and 12 stop, and the oscillator pre-requisites of chapter 7 go on from sections 9 and 10.

In one page

The problem. A particle in an accelerator is pushed by electric fields and steered by magnetic ones, and the machine works only if its builders can say where the particle will be, how fast it will go and how much energy it will carry, at every point and every moment. Before Newton there was no general way to go from the forces on a body to its motion. There were good rules for special cases (Galileo’s falling bodies, Kepler’s planets), but no law that covered a stone, a planet and a pendulum alike.

The idea. A body’s momentum, mass times velocity, changes only when a force acts, at a rate equal to the force: \(\mathbf{F} = d\mathbf{p}/dt\). Forces come in equal and opposite pairs, so in a closed system the total momentum never changes. A force acting through a distance does work, which changes the body’s kinetic energy; if the force is the gradient of a potential energy, the sum of kinetic and potential energy never changes. These two conservation laws solve collisions without knowing the forces during the collision, and they show that a collision between a moving particle and one at rest wastes energy on moving the centre of mass. The same laws applied to turning give angular momentum and its conservation, which shape every orbit. Near the bottom of any potential well, motion is a simple harmonic oscillation, with friction it decays, and pushed in step it resonates. All of this holds in every frame moving uniformly, and fails near the speed of light.

Equations to remember.

  • Kinematics: \(\mathbf{v} = d\mathbf{r}/dt\), \(\mathbf{a} = d\mathbf{v}/dt\); at constant \(\mathbf{a}\), \(\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}_0 + \mathbf{a}t\) and \(\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_0 + \mathbf{v}_0t + \tfrac12\mathbf{a}t^2\). Position \(\mathbf{r}\) in m, velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) in m/s, acceleration \(\mathbf{a}\) in m/s\(^2\), time \(t\) in s.
  • Newton’s second law: \(\mathbf{F} = \dfrac{d\mathbf{p}}{dt} = m\mathbf{a}\), with momentum \(\mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{v}\) (kg m/s), force \(\mathbf{F}\) in newtons (N = kg m/s\(^2\)), mass \(m\) in kg. Impulse: \(\Delta\mathbf{p} = \int\mathbf{F}\,dt\).
  • Work and kinetic energy: \(\Delta W = \int\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{l}\), with kinetic energy \(W = \tfrac12 mv^2 = p^2/2m\) in joules (J). Power \(P = \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{v}\) in watts (W = J/s).
  • Potential energy \(U\) (J): \(\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U\), and then \(W + U\) is constant. For a charge \(q\) at potential \(V\), \(U = qV\); for two charges, \(U = q_1q_2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)\).
  • Collisions: the centre of mass moves at \(\mathbf{V}_{cm} = (m_1\mathbf{v}_1 + m_2\mathbf{v}_2)/(m_1 + m_2)\); a body of kinetic energy \(W\) striking a body of mass \(m_2\) at rest makes available \(W_{cm} = W\,m_2/(m_1 + m_2)\). Reduced mass \(\mu = m_1m_2/(m_1 + m_2)\).
  • Rotation: angular momentum \(\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p}\) (kg m\(^2\)/s), torque \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}\) (N m), \(d\mathbf{L}/dt = \boldsymbol{\tau}\). A rigid body: \(L = I\omega\), \(W = \tfrac12 I\omega^2\), moment of inertia \(I = \sum m r^2\) (kg m\(^2\)).
  • Orbits under an attraction \(k/r^2\) (\(k\) in J m): circular speed \(v^2 = k/(mr)\), energy \(E = W + U = -k/2r\) (negative means bound).
  • Oscillator: \(m\ddot x = -kx\) gives \(x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\), \(\omega = \sqrt{k/m}\), energy \(\tfrac12 kA^2\); near a minimum of any \(U(x)\), \(k = U''(x_0)\). With damping, energy falls as \(e^{-\omega_0t/Q}\); driven at \(\omega_0\), the amplitude is \(Q\) times the static deflection, over a width \(\Delta\omega = \omega_0/Q\).
  • Galilean change of frame: \(\mathbf{r}' = \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{V}t\), \(\mathbf{v}' = \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{V}\), \(\mathbf{a}' = \mathbf{a}\).

Numbers worth knowing.

  • An electron in a field of 200 kV/m feels a force \(3.6 \times 10^{15}\) times its weight. Gravity never matters for a beam’s path inside a machine.
  • A 10 mA beam of 2 kV electrons stopped in a screen pushes it with 1.5 μN; a 1 mA beam of 10 MeV protons with 0.46 mN.
  • A 1 mA beam of 10 MeV protons carries 10 kW; the 590 MeV, 2.4 mA proton cyclotron at PSI carries 1.4 MW.
  • A polonium-210 nucleus decaying at rest sends its alpha off with 5.30 MeV and the lead nucleus back with 0.10 MeV: the light fragment takes almost all the energy.
  • A 1 MeV proton can give an electron at most 2.18 keV in one collision; an alpha cannot be turned by more than 0.0079° by an electron. Large-angle scattering needs a nucleus.
  • A turbomolecular pump rotor of 1 kg and 0.1 m across at 40 000 rpm stores 11 kJ.
  • The hydrogen atom, as a classical orbit at the Bohr radius: speed \(2.19 \times 10^6\) m/s, energy \(-13.6\) eV.
  • A copper cavity at 2856 MHz with \(Q_0 = 18\,400\) holds its stored energy for about 1 μs and responds over 155 kHz; a niobium one at 1.3 GHz with \(Q_0 = 10^{10}\) for 1.2 s, over 0.13 Hz.
  • Newton’s \(\tfrac12 mv^2\) puts a 1 MeV electron at twice the speed of light. It moves at 0.941\(c\).

The problem it solved

Falling, rolling and a moving ship

Aristotle taught that a body moves only while something pushes it, and that heavy bodies fall faster. Galileo Galilei tested motion with balls rolling down ramps, and in his Two New Sciences of 1638 set out that a falling body gains equal speed in equal times, so that its distance grows as the square of the time, and that a thrown body moves on a parabola: steady motion sideways, accelerated motion downwards, the two independent. In his Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems of 1632 he asked the reader to imagine a cabin below the deck of a ship moving smoothly: butterflies fly, drops fall into a bottle and fish swim exactly as they do when the ship is still. No experiment inside can tell whether the ship moves. That is the principle of relativity, three centuries before Einstein.

Collisions and the quantity of motion

René Descartes, in his Principles of Philosophy of 1644, held that the world’s total “quantity of motion”, size times speed, is conserved, and that a body left alone moves in a straight line. His rules for collisions were mostly wrong, because he ignored direction. In 1668 the Royal Society asked for the true laws of impact; John Wallis, Christopher Wren and Christiaan Huygens answered. Huygens showed, by watching the collision from a moving boat, that what is conserved is mass times velocity with its sign, and that in a collision of hard bodies the sum of mass times speed squared is conserved too. Huygens also built the first pendulum clock (1656) and, in his Horologium Oscillatorium of 1673, gave the pendulum’s period and the force needed to hold a body on a circle.

Newton’s Principia

In 1684 Edmond Halley asked Isaac Newton what path a planet would follow under a force falling as the inverse square of distance. Newton had the answer, an ellipse, and the question grew into the Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica of 1687. It opens with three laws of motion and builds on them the mechanics of the solar system, the tides and the pendulum. Newton wrote his laws in words and proved his results with geometry. Leonhard Euler, around 1750, wrote the second law as differential equations, force equals mass times acceleration along each of three axes, which is how it has been used since.

Living force and work

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz argued in 1686 that the true measure of a body’s motion is \(mv^2\), its vis viva (living force), not Descartes’s \(mv\). The quarrel ran for half a century before it was understood that both are conserved, in different circumstances, and measure different things: \(mv\) the effect of a force over a time, \(mv^2\) over a distance. Émilie du Châtelet brought Willem ’s Gravesande’s experiments (balls dropped into soft clay, making dents that grew with the square of the speed) into her Institutions de physique of 1740, and her French translation of the Principia, published in 1759, was the standard one for two centuries. In 1829 Gaspard-Gustave Coriolis named work (travail), force times distance, and put the factor \(\tfrac12\) into the kinetic energy so that the two are equal; in 1835 he worked out the extra forces seen from a rotating frame. The words “energy” (Thomas Young, 1807) and “potential energy” (William Rankine, 1853) came later, as did the proof, by James Joule in the 1840s, that heat is energy too.

The physics

0. What this chapter assumes, and a quick check

  • From school. Speed is distance over time; acceleration is the rate of change of speed; \(F = ma\) in one direction; weight is \(mg\) with \(g = 9.81\) m/s\(^2\); kinetic energy \(\tfrac12mv^2\) and potential energy \(mgh\).
  • From F1. Derivatives and integrals and the fundamental theorem; the chain rule; a Taylor series to second order, \(f(x_0 + h) \approx f(x_0) + f'(x_0)h + \tfrac12f''(x_0)h^2\); the solutions of \(\dot N = -\lambda N\) and \(\ddot x = -\omega^2x\).
  • From F2. Vectors in components; \(\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}\) and \(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}\); differentiating a vector component by component; acceleration \(v^2/R\) towards the centre on a circle; the gradient; the line integral \(\int\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{l}\), and that it does not depend on the path when \(\mathbf{F}\) is a gradient.

Check yourself. (a) A car accelerates from rest at 2 m/s\(^2\) for 5 s. How fast is it going and how far has it gone? (b) Differentiate \(x(t) = 3t^2 - 2t\) twice. (c) Find \((1, 0, 0)\times(0, 2, 0)\). (d) Find \(\nabla(1/r)\). Answers: (a) 10 m/s and 25 m; (b) \(\dot x = 6t - 2\), \(\ddot x = 6\); (c) \((0, 0, 2)\); (d) \(-\hat{\mathbf{r}}/r^2\) (F2, section 6).

1. Describing motion

The picture. Before asking what makes a body move, describe how it moves. A body small enough that its size does not matter is a particle, a point with a mass. Its position at time \(t\) is a vector \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) from an origin we choose. Its velocity is the rate of change of position, and its acceleration the rate of change of velocity (F2, section 4):

\[ \mathbf{v} = \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} = \dot{\mathbf{r}}, \qquad \mathbf{a} = \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} = \ddot{\mathbf{r}} . \]

Speed is the size of the velocity, \(v = |\mathbf{v}|\). The units are m, m/s and m/s\(^2\). A body at constant speed on a circle is accelerating, since its direction changes; a body whose speed is momentarily zero (a ball at the top of its throw) can still be accelerating.

Going backwards: integration. If the acceleration is known for all times, the fundamental theorem of F1 gives the velocity and then the position:

\[ \mathbf{v}(t) = \mathbf{v}_0 + \int_0^t\mathbf{a}(t')\,dt', \qquad \mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{r}_0 + \int_0^t\mathbf{v}(t')\,dt' . \]

Two constants enter, the starting position \(\mathbf{r}_0\) and the starting velocity \(\mathbf{v}_0\): the acceleration alone does not say where the body is or how fast it is going. When the acceleration is constant, the integrals are easy and give the school formulas, now derived:

\[ \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}_0 + \mathbf{a}t, \qquad \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_0 + \mathbf{v}_0t + \tfrac12\mathbf{a}t^2 . \]

Eliminating \(t\) in one direction gives the third, \(v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a(x - x_0)\).

Each direction on its own. The vector equation is three equations, one per component, and when the acceleration is constant they do not affect each other. Galileo’s projectile moves steadily sideways and accelerates downwards, and the two combine into a parabola.

Example: an electron through deflecting plates. This is chapter 2’s cathode-ray tube. An electron from a 2 kV gun enters along \(z\) at \(v_z = 2.65 \times 10^7\) m/s (section 4 derives this speed) between two plates 2 cm long and 5 mm apart, with 100 V across them. Inside, there is a uniform field \(\mathcal{E}_x = 100/0.005 = 2 \times 10^4\) V/m along \(x\) and nothing along \(z\). Section 2 gives the acceleration, \(a_x = e\mathcal{E}_x/m_e = 3.52 \times 10^{15}\) m/s\(^2\); \(v_z\) stays fixed. The electron spends \(t = 0.02/(2.65 \times 10^7) = 0.754\) ns between the plates. It leaves with

\[ v_x = a_xt = 2.65 \times 10^6\ \text{m/s}, \qquad x = \tfrac12a_xt^2 = 1.0\ \text{mm} . \]

So it leaves at the angle whose tangent is \(v_x/v_z = 0.1\), already displaced 1 mm. Beyond the plates there is no force, and it flies straight. On a screen 20 cm beyond the plates it lands \(1.0 + 200 \times 0.1 = 21\) mm off centre; the straight line it leaves on, traced back, starts from the middle of the plates, which is why chapter 2 writes the deflection as \((l/2 + L)\tan\theta\).

Example: what gravity does to it. The same electron also falls under gravity. Over 1 m of flight, which takes \(1/(2.65 \times 10^7) = 37.7\) ns, it falls \(\tfrac12gt^2 = 7 \times 10^{-15}\) m, less than the size of a nucleus. Section 2 shows why: the electric force is \(10^{15}\) times larger.

2. Newton’s three laws

The first law: inertia. A body on which no net force acts keeps its velocity: at rest it stays at rest, and moving it moves in a straight line at constant speed. This is not obvious; on Earth a rolling ball slows down, because friction acts. In the vacuum of a beam pipe, a particle that leaves the last accelerating gap coasts at constant speed until something acts on it. The first law also defines what we mean by a good frame of reference: an inertial frame is one in which it holds. A frame fixed to the laboratory is inertial to a very good approximation (section 11 shows how good); a frame fixed to a turning merry-go-round is not.

The second law: force and momentum. Define a body’s momentum as its mass times its velocity,

\[ \mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{v} \qquad [\text{kg m/s}] . \]

The second law says that a force changes the momentum, at a rate equal to the force:

\[ \mathbf{F} = \frac{d\mathbf{p}}{dt} . \]

For a body whose mass does not change, \(d\mathbf{p}/dt = m\,d\mathbf{v}/dt\), and this is the familiar \(\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}\). When several forces act, \(\mathbf{F}\) is their vector sum. The unit of force is fixed by the law: one newton (N) is the force that gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s\(^2\).

The law is three equations, one per axis, and it is a differential equation (F1, section 10): it says what the acceleration is, given the forces, and the motion follows by integrating twice from a starting position and velocity. That is the whole method of the course’s particle tracking, from chapter 2’s tube to the LHC: write down the forces, integrate.

Mass: inertia and weight. The mass \(m\) in the second law is inertial mass, a body’s resistance to having its velocity changed. Push 1 kg and 2 kg with the same force; the second accelerates half as fast. Weight is a different thing: the gravitational force on a body, \(m\mathbf{g}\), with \(g = 9.81\) m/s\(^2\) near the Earth’s surface. That the same \(m\) appears in both, so that all bodies fall alike, was Galileo’s discovery and Newton’s puzzle; it is now tested to better than one part in \(10^{14}\), and it is the starting point of Einstein’s theory of gravity. For the course it means only this: weight is a force like any other, \(mg\), and for particles it is negligible.

Example. An electron in the 200 kV/m field of F1’s 2 kV, 1 cm gap feels the force \(e\mathcal{E} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \times 2 \times 10^5 = 3.20 \times 10^{-14}\) N. Its weight is \(m_eg = 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 9.81 = 8.93 \times 10^{-30}\) N, smaller by \(3.6 \times 10^{15}\). A field of only \(m_pg/e = 1.0 \times 10^{-7}\) V/m would hold a proton up against gravity. Gravity matters for the magnets and the girders of a machine, never for the beam.

The second law for charges. The forces on a charge \(q\) are the electric and magnetic parts of the Lorentz force (F2, section 3), and Newton’s law becomes the equation of motion of every particle in the course:

\[ \frac{d\mathbf{p}}{dt} = q\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\right) . \]

Written with \(d\mathbf{p}/dt\) rather than \(m\mathbf{a}\), it stays true at any speed, provided the momentum is given its relativistic form \(\gamma m\mathbf{v}\) (section 11, and chapter 1). Newton’s own statement, “the change of motion is proportional to the motive force”, was in terms of momentum, and that is the form that survived.

The third law: action and reaction. When body A pushes on body B, B pushes on A with a force equal in size and opposite in direction. The two forces act on different bodies, so they never cancel each other in the motion of one body. The electron gun that throws electrons forward is pushed backwards; the Earth pulls an apple down and the apple pulls the Earth up, by the same 1 N, but the Earth’s acceleration is about \(10^{25}\) times smaller. The third law is what makes momentum conserved (section 3).

A caution for later. Between two moving charges, the magnetic forces are not always equal and opposite at each instant, because the field carries the news from one to the other at the speed of light. Momentum is still conserved once the momentum carried by the field itself is counted, which F4 and chapter 20 need. For bodies in contact, and for charges moving slowly, the third law holds as stated.

3. Momentum, impulse and the centre of mass

Impulse. Integrate the second law over the time a force acts:

\[ \Delta\mathbf{p} = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\mathbf{F}\,dt . \]

The right side is the impulse. A large force for a short time and a small force for a long time can give the same change of momentum. This is how the deflecting plates of section 1 work: the impulse \(e\mathcal{E}_xt\) gives the sideways momentum \(m_ev_x\). It is also how a collision is handled when the force during it is unknown: the impulse is fixed by the momenta before and after.

Example: the push of a beam. A beam of particles stopped in a target hands its momentum to the target. If \(\dot N\) particles arrive per second, each with momentum \(p\), the target receives momentum \(\dot Np\) per second, and by the second law that is a steady force \(F = \dot Np\). A beam current \(I_b\) is \(\dot N = I_b/e\) particles per second. For 10 mA of 2 kV electrons, \(\dot N = 6.24 \times 10^{16}\) per second, \(p = m_ev = 2.42 \times 10^{-23}\) kg m/s, and \(F = 1.5\) μN, the weight of 0.15 mg. For 1 mA of 10 MeV protons, \(p = \sqrt{2m_pW} = 7.32 \times 10^{-20}\) kg m/s and \(F = 0.46\) mN. Beams carry little momentum; it is their energy (section 4) that melts targets.

Conservation of momentum. Take two bodies that push on each other with forces \(\mathbf{F}_{12}\) (on 1 from 2) and \(\mathbf{F}_{21}\), and on which nothing outside acts. Then

\[ \frac{d}{dt}(\mathbf{p}_1 + \mathbf{p}_2) = \mathbf{F}_{12} + \mathbf{F}_{21} = 0 \]

by the third law. The total momentum does not change, however strong and complicated the forces between them. With many bodies the internal forces cancel in pairs in the same way, and only external forces change the total:

\[ \frac{d\mathbf{P}}{dt} = \mathbf{F}_{ext}, \qquad \mathbf{P} = \sum_i\mathbf{p}_i . \]

If there are no external forces, \(\mathbf{P}\) is constant. This is one of the two or three most useful facts in physics. It holds in collisions, explosions and decays, and it still holds, with relativistic momenta, at the LHC.

Example: an alpha decay. A polonium-210 nucleus at rest breaks into an alpha particle (mass 4 u) and a lead-206 nucleus (206 u), releasing \(Q = 5.41\) MeV as kinetic energy. (\(Q\) here is the energy of a reaction, as in chapter 1’s Q value; 1 u \(= 1.661 \times 10^{-27}\) kg, the atomic mass unit.) The total momentum was zero, so afterwards the two momenta are equal and opposite, \(p_\alpha = p_{Pb} = p\). The kinetic energy of each is \(W = p^2/2m\) (since \(\tfrac12mv^2 = (mv)^2/2m\)), so with the same \(p\) the energies are in the inverse ratio of the masses:

\[ \frac{W_\alpha}{W_{Pb}} = \frac{m_{Pb}}{m_\alpha}, \qquad W_\alpha = Q\,\frac{m_{Pb}}{m_{Pb} + m_\alpha} = 5.41 \times \frac{206}{210} = 5.30\ \text{MeV} . \]

The lead nucleus recoils with the remaining 0.10 MeV. The alpha moves at \(1.60 \times 10^7\) m/s, the lead at \(3.1 \times 10^5\) m/s, 51 times slower. Every polonium alpha has the same energy, because two bodies sharing a fixed energy with zero total momentum have no freedom left. (When beta decay was found to give electrons a spread of energies, Wolfgang Pauli concluded in 1930 that a third, unseen particle, the neutrino, must share the momentum.)

The centre of mass. For a set of particles of masses \(m_i\) at positions \(\mathbf{r}_i\), define the centre of mass, the mass-weighted average position:

\[ \mathbf{R}_{cm} = \frac{\sum_im_i\mathbf{r}_i}{M}, \qquad M = \sum_im_i . \]

Differentiating, \(M\dot{\mathbf{R}}_{cm} = \sum m_i\mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{P}\): the total momentum is the total mass times the velocity of the centre of mass. And then \(M\ddot{\mathbf{R}}_{cm} = \mathbf{F}_{ext}\). The centre of mass moves as if all the mass were there and all the external forces acted on it, whatever the parts do among themselves. A shell bursting in the air sends its centre of mass on the same parabola as before. A hydrogen atom’s centre of mass lies \(m_e/(m_e + m_p) = 1/1837\) of the way from the proton to the electron, \(2.9 \times 10^{-14}\) m from the proton: the proton barely moves, but not quite not at all, and the correction shows in the spectrum of hydrogen. With no external force, the centre of mass moves at constant velocity, and a frame moving with it is inertial: the centre-of-mass frame, in which the total momentum is zero. Section 6 uses it.

4. Work, kinetic energy and power

The picture. Momentum tells how a force acts over time. Asking how it acts over distance leads to energy. Push a body along with a force \(\mathbf{F}\) while it moves a small step \(d\mathbf{l}\). The force does work \(\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{l}\) (F2, section 2): only the part of the force along the motion counts. Over a whole path, the work is the line integral

\[ \Delta W = \int_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{l} \qquad [\text{J} = \text{N m}] . \]

The work–energy theorem. Let \(\mathbf{F}\) be the total force on a particle of constant mass. Along the path, \(d\mathbf{l} = \mathbf{v}\,dt\), and \(\mathbf{F} = m\,d\mathbf{v}/dt\), so

\[ \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = m\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}\cdot\mathbf{v}\,dt = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12m\,\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}\right)dt, \]

using \(\frac{d}{dt}(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}) = 2\mathbf{v}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{v}}\) (F2, section 4). The right side is an exact derivative, and the fundamental theorem gives

\[ \int_1^2\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \tfrac12mv_2^2 - \tfrac12mv_1^2 . \]

The work done by the total force equals the change of the kinetic energy,

\[ W = \tfrac12mv^2 = \frac{p^2}{2m} \qquad [\text{J}] . \]

(The course writes \(W\) for kinetic energy everywhere, keeping \(E\) for total energy; the context keeps it apart from the watt, W.) This explains the factor \(\tfrac12\): it is what makes the kinetic energy equal to the work done to reach it. It also settles Descartes and Leibniz: \(mv\) is the time integral of force and \(\tfrac12mv^2\) the distance integral.

Example: the 2 kV electron. An electron crossing a gap with 2000 V across it gains the work \(eV = 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \times 2000 = 3.20 \times 10^{-16}\) J, whatever the shape of the field in the gap (F2, section 7). Starting from rest, \(\tfrac12m_ev^2 = eV\), so

\[ v = \sqrt{\frac{2eV}{m_e}} = 2.65 \times 10^7\ \text{m/s} . \]

This is the speed used in section 1. It is 0.3 per cent too high: at 9 per cent of the speed of light, Newton’s formula has started to fail (section 11). The same energy unit, the work done on one electron charge by 1 V, is the electron-volt, \(1\ \text{eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\) J, in which the whole course measures particle energies. A proton of 1 MeV moves at \(\sqrt{2W/m_p} = 1.384 \times 10^7\) m/s, within 0.08 per cent of the true value.

Forces that do no work. If the force is always at right angles to the motion, \(\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0\) and the kinetic energy cannot change. The magnetic force \(q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\) is such a force. It bends a particle but never changes its speed: accelerators use electric fields to give energy and magnetic fields to steer.

Power. The rate of doing work is the power. Dividing \(\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{l}\) by \(dt\):

\[ P = \frac{dW}{dt} = \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{v} \qquad [\text{W} = \text{J/s}] . \]

For a beam, each particle of charge \(e\) gains \(eV\) and \(I_b/e\) of them arrive each second, so the power the accelerator puts into the beam is \(P = I_bV\). A 10 mA beam through 2 kV carries 20 W; a 1 mA beam of 10 MeV protons carries 10 kW, enough to melt any small target not cooled with water; the proton cyclotron at the Paul Scherrer Institute, 2.4 mA at 590 MeV, carries 1.4 MW, and chapter 22 is about why a beam of that power must lose less than a part in ten thousand of itself on the walls.

5. Potential energy, conservative forces and energy conservation

Stored work. Lift a 1 kg mass by 1 m. You do \(mgh = 9.81\) J of work against gravity; let go, and gravity gives it back as kinetic energy at the bottom. The work seems to be stored in the position. That is possible only when the work done by the force between two points does not depend on the path taken: otherwise one could go up one way and down another and come back with more energy than one started with.

Conservative forces. A force is conservative if its work between any two points is the same along every path. F2, section 7, showed when that happens: when the force is minus the gradient of a function,

\[ \mathbf{F} = -\nabla U . \]

The function \(U(\mathbf{r})\), in joules, is the potential energy. The minus sign makes the force point downhill in \(U\), towards lower potential energy. Then the work from point 1 to point 2 is \(\int\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = -[U(2) - U(1)]\), and the work–energy theorem gives

\[ W_2 - W_1 = -(U_2 - U_1) \quad\Rightarrow\quad W + U = E = \text{constant} . \]

This is conservation of energy for a particle: the sum \(E\) of kinetic and potential energy, the mechanical energy, does not change. (In chapter 1 and onward, \(E\) also includes the rest energy \(mc^2\); for slow particles that is a fixed constant added to both sides and changes nothing here.) The test for a conservative force is F2’s: zero curl. Only differences of \(U\) matter, so its zero can be put anywhere convenient.

The potential energies of the course.

  • Uniform gravity: \(U = mgh\), with \(h\) the height.
  • A charge in an electric potential: the force \(q\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -q\nabla V\), so \(U = qV\). A charge \(q\) falling through a potential difference \(V\) gains kinetic energy \(qV\), which is section 4’s result again.
  • Two point charges \(q_1\), \(q_2\) at distance \(r\): \(U = \dfrac{q_1q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}\), zero when they are far apart, positive (repulsive) for like charges. Its gradient is Coulomb’s force (F2, section 6). Gravity between masses \(M\) and \(m\) is the same shape with the opposite sign, \(U = -GMm/r\).
  • A spring, or anything pulled back in proportion to its displacement, \(F = -kx\): \(U = \tfrac12kx^2\), since \(-dU/dx = -kx\).

Reading a potential-energy curve. In one dimension, \(F = -dU/dx\), and energy conservation alone tells a great deal. Draw \(U(x)\), and a horizontal line at the energy \(E\). The particle can be only where \(U \le E\), since \(W = E - U\) cannot be negative. Where the line crosses the curve, \(W = 0\): the particle stops and turns back. These are turning points. A particle in a valley between two turning points is bound and goes back and forth; one whose energy is above the barrier on one side escapes. At a minimum of \(U\) the force is zero and nearby it points back towards the minimum: stable equilibrium. At a maximum the force is zero too, but nearby it points away: unstable. Section 9 takes the minimum further, and chapter 12 draws exactly this picture for particles riding an RF wave.

Example: how close an alpha gets to gold. An alpha particle (\(q_1 = 2e\)) of kinetic energy \(W = 7.69\) MeV heads straight for a gold nucleus (\(q_2 = 79e\)), held fixed. Far away, \(U = 0\) and \(E = W\). It slows as it climbs the Coulomb hill and stops where all its kinetic energy has become potential energy:

\[ W = \frac{2 \times 79\,e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,d} \quad\Rightarrow\quad d = \frac{158 \times 1.440\ \text{MeV fm}}{7.69\ \text{MeV}} = 29.6\ \text{fm}, \]

with \(e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0 = 1.440\) MeV fm (1 fm \(= 10^{-15}\) m). The gold nucleus has a radius of about 7 fm, so the alpha never touches it, and only the Coulomb force acts: this is why Rutherford’s scattering law worked so well (chapter 1). The recoil of the gold, ignored here, is the subject of section 6.

Forces that are not conservative. Friction always opposes the motion, so its work round any closed path is negative, and no \(U\) can describe it; the energy goes into heat (F6). The electric field induced by a changing magnetic field has curl and no potential: a charge going round a loop gains energy each turn, which is how the betatron accelerates (chapter 11). Energy is still conserved in both cases, once heat and the field’s own energy are counted; it is the mechanical energy \(W + U\) alone that is not.

6. Collisions

What is conserved. In a collision two bodies push on each other hard for a short time, with forces usually unknown. Without external forces the total momentum is conserved, always. The total kinetic energy is conserved only if none of it is turned into something else (heat, deformation, excitation of an atom, a new particle). A collision that keeps kinetic energy is elastic; one that does not is inelastic. Rutherford’s alphas scattering off gold, and electrons off atoms at low energy, are elastic; an alpha knocking a proton out of nitrogen is inelastic, since the masses themselves change.

Elastic collision in one dimension. A body of mass \(m_1\) and velocity \(v_1\) hits a body \(m_2\) at rest, head on. Momentum and energy give

\[ m_1v_1 = m_1v_1' + m_2v_2', \qquad \tfrac12m_1v_1^2 = \tfrac12m_1v_1'^2 + \tfrac12m_2v_2'^2 . \]

Rearrange both to \(m_1(v_1 - v_1') = m_2v_2'\) and \(m_1(v_1 - v_1')(v_1 + v_1') = m_2v_2'^2\), and divide: \(v_1 + v_1' = v_2'\). The bodies separate at the speed they approached. Putting this back into the momentum equation,

\[ v_1' = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_1, \qquad v_2' = \frac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_1 . \]

The fraction of the kinetic energy handed to the target is

\[ \frac{W_2'}{W_1} = \frac{m_2v_2'^2}{m_1v_1^2} = \frac{4m_1m_2}{(m_1 + m_2)^2} . \]

Three cases cover the course:

  • Equal masses: the projectile stops and the target takes everything. A neutron hitting a proton head on hands it all its energy, which is why hydrogen-rich water and paraffin slow neutrons best.
  • Light on heavy (\(m_1 \ll m_2\)): \(v_1' \approx -v_1\), the projectile bounces straight back with nearly its full speed, and the target takes a fraction about \(4m_1/m_2\). An alpha bouncing off gold keeps 92 per cent of its energy.
  • Heavy on light (\(m_1 \gg m_2\)): the projectile barely slows, and the target leaves at twice the projectile’s speed, carrying \(4m_2/m_1\) of its energy. A 1 MeV proton gives a free electron at most \(4 \times 0.511/938.3 \times 1\) MeV \(= 2.18\) keV. A charged particle crossing matter therefore loses its energy in many thousands of small steps to electrons, which is why it has a definite range (chapter 1).

Glancing collisions and the largest angle. In two or three dimensions the momentum equation is a vector equation, and the bodies can leave at angles. One result needs no algebra beyond this: a heavy projectile hitting a light target at rest can be turned by at most the angle whose sine is \(m_2/m_1\). For an alpha on an electron that is \(0.511/3727 = 1.37 \times 10^{-4}\) rad, or \(0.0079°\). When Geiger and Marsden saw alphas bounce back from gold foil in 1909, no collision with electrons could have done it: the alpha had met something heavier than itself. That was the nucleus.

The centre-of-mass frame. Watch the same collision from a frame moving with the centre of mass, at velocity

\[ \mathbf{V}_{cm} = \frac{m_1\mathbf{v}_1 + m_2\mathbf{v}_2}{m_1 + m_2} . \]

In this frame the total momentum is zero: the two bodies approach with equal and opposite momenta, and in an elastic collision leave with equal and opposite momenta of the same size, only turned. The collision is as simple as it can be. Section 11 shows that Newton’s laws hold equally in this frame, since it moves uniformly.

Where the energy goes. Split each velocity into the centre-of-mass velocity and a velocity relative to it, \(\mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{V}_{cm} + \mathbf{u}_i\). Then \(\sum m_i\mathbf{u}_i = 0\) (that is what centre of mass means), and when the kinetic energies are added the cross terms \(\mathbf{V}_{cm}\cdot\sum m_i\mathbf{u}_i\) vanish:

\[ W_{total} = \tfrac12(m_1 + m_2)V_{cm}^2 + \left(\tfrac12m_1u_1^2 + \tfrac12m_2u_2^2\right) . \]

The first part is the kinetic energy of the centre of mass. No collision can change it, since the total momentum is conserved. Only the second part, the energy in the centre-of-mass frame, can be turned into heat, excitation or new particles. For a body of mass \(m_1\) and kinetic energy \(W\) hitting \(m_2\) at rest, the centre of mass carries the fraction \(m_1/(m_1 + m_2)\), and what is left over is

\[ W_{cm} = W\,\frac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2} . \]

It can also be written \(W_{cm} = \tfrac12\mu v_{rel}^2\), with the reduced mass \(\mu = m_1m_2/(m_1 + m_2)\) and \(v_{rel}\) the speed of one body relative to the other.

Example: making oxygen from nitrogen. In 1919 Rutherford found that alphas knock protons out of nitrogen: \(^{4}\mathrm{He} + {}^{14}\mathrm{N} \to {}^{17}\mathrm{O} + p\). From the masses (chapter 1’s Q value), \(Q = -1.19\) MeV: the reaction needs 1.19 MeV, taken from the kinetic energy in the centre-of-mass frame. The alpha must therefore bring

\[ W_{threshold} = 1.19 \times \frac{4.003 + 14.003}{14.003} = 1.53\ \text{MeV}, \]

of which 0.34 MeV is spent only on moving the centre of mass. The 7.69 MeV alphas of radium C bring \(W_{cm} = 7.69 \times 14.003/18.006 = 5.98\) MeV, enough for the reaction and, more to the point, enough to come near the nitrogen nucleus through its Coulomb barrier.

Why colliders. Let two equal bodies, each of kinetic energy \(W\), meet head on. Their total momentum is zero, the laboratory is already the centre-of-mass frame, and all \(2W\) is available. To get the same \(2W\) by firing one at the other at rest, it needs \(W_{cm} = W_{lab}/2\), so \(W_{lab} = 4W\): twice the total energy of the two beams. Non-relativistically, colliding beams win by a factor of 2. Near the speed of light the gap becomes enormous, because the centre of mass then carries almost everything: the LHC’s 6.8 TeV protons colliding head on give 13.6 TeV, while the same beam on a fixed target would give only 113 GeV. Chapter 1 derives that from the relativistic version of exactly this argument, in section 5, and chapter 23 follows the consequences.

Perfectly inelastic collisions. If the two bodies stick together, they move off at \(\mathbf{V}_{cm}\), and all of \(W_{cm}\) is lost to heat. A slow electron captured by an ion, or a particle stopping in a target, are of this kind.

7. Rotation

Angular velocity. A body turning about a fixed axis turns through an angle \(\theta\) (radians) that changes at the angular velocity \(\omega = d\theta/dt\) (rad/s). A point at distance \(r\) from the axis moves on a circle at speed \(v = \omega r\). As a vector, \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) points along the axis, by the right-hand rule (fingers curl the way the body turns, thumb along \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\)), and the velocity of a point at position \(\mathbf{r}\) from a point on the axis is \(\mathbf{v} = \boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf{r}\). Revolutions per minute convert as \(\omega = 2\pi \times \text{rpm}/60\).

Torque. A door is easy to open by pushing at the handle and hard by pushing near the hinge: what turns a body is the force times its lever arm. The torque of a force \(\mathbf{F}\) applied at position \(\mathbf{r}\) (from a chosen origin) is

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F} \qquad [\text{N m}], \]

of size \(rF\sin\theta\), which is the force times the perpendicular distance from the origin to its line of action.

Angular momentum. The rotational partner of momentum is the angular momentum about the origin,

\[ \mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p} \qquad [\text{kg m}^2/\text{s}] . \]

Differentiate with the product rule: \(\dot{\mathbf{L}} = \dot{\mathbf{r}}\times\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{r}\times\dot{\mathbf{p}}\). The first term is \(\mathbf{v}\times m\mathbf{v} = 0\), since a vector crossed with itself is zero, and in the second \(\dot{\mathbf{p}} = \mathbf{F}\). So

\[ \frac{d\mathbf{L}}{dt} = \boldsymbol{\tau} . \]

Torque changes angular momentum as force changes momentum. For a system of particles the internal torques cancel in pairs (if the forces between particles act along the line joining them, as Coulomb’s and gravity’s do), and the total angular momentum changes only by external torques. With none, it is conserved.

Conservation of angular momentum. A force that always points towards (or away from) the origin, \(\mathbf{F} \parallel \mathbf{r}\), has zero torque about it, so \(\mathbf{L}\) is constant. Two things follow. The motion stays in one plane, the plane at right angles to the fixed \(\mathbf{L}\). And the line from the origin to the particle sweeps out area at a steady rate: in a short time \(dt\) it sweeps a thin triangle of area \(\tfrac12|\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{v}\,dt|\), so

\[ \frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{L}{2m} = \text{constant} . \]

This is Kepler’s second law, equal areas in equal times, and it holds for any force towards a centre, not only for gravity. A planet moves fastest when closest to the Sun; an alpha passing a nucleus swings round fastest at its closest approach, which chapter 1’s Rutherford scattering uses.

Example. A proton circling in a cyclotron’s uniform field feels the magnetic force pointing at the centre of its circle, so its angular momentum about that centre is fixed while the field alone acts. A 10 MeV proton in 1 T circles with radius \(R = p/(eB) = 0.457\) m (F2, section 4) and has \(L = pR = eBR^2 = 3.35 \times 10^{-20}\) kg m\(^2\)/s. Each accelerating gap adds momentum along the motion, a torque, and the orbit grows.

Rigid bodies: moment of inertia. A rigid body turning at \(\omega\) about a fixed axis is many particles, each moving at \(\omega r_i\). Its kinetic energy and its angular momentum along the axis are

\[ W = \sum_i\tfrac12m_i(\omega r_i)^2 = \tfrac12I\omega^2, \qquad L = \sum_im_ir_i(\omega r_i) = I\omega, \qquad I = \sum_im_ir_i^2 . \]

The moment of inertia \(I\) (kg m\(^2\)) plays the part of mass: it measures how hard the body is to set turning, and mass far from the axis counts most. (Here \(I\) is not a current.) For a thin ring of mass \(M\) and radius \(R\), \(I = MR^2\); for a uniform solid disc, cutting it into rings of width \(dr\) and mass \(M\,2\pi r\,dr/\pi R^2\) (F2, section 8),

\[ I = \int_0^R r^2\,\frac{2Mr\,dr}{R^2} = \tfrac12MR^2 . \]

The rotational second law is \(\tau = I\,d\omega/dt\) about a fixed axis.

Example: a turbomolecular pump. Chapter 3’s turbomolecular pumps spin rows of blades at up to 40 000 rpm. Model a rotor as a solid disc of 1 kg and 0.1 m across (a rough stand-in; real rotors are stacks of bladed discs). Then \(I = \tfrac12 \times 1 \times 0.05^2 = 1.25 \times 10^{-3}\) kg m\(^2\), \(\omega = 2\pi \times 40\,000/60 = 4189\) rad/s, the blade tips move at \(\omega R = 209\) m/s, and the stored energy is

\[ W = \tfrac12I\omega^2 = 11\ \text{kJ}, \]

the kinetic energy of a 1000 kg car at 17 km/h, held in a part the size of a hand. A motor giving a steady torque of 0.05 N m brings it up to speed in \(I\omega/\tau = 105\) s. That stored energy is why a rotor that touches its housing destroys the pump, and why a pump must be bolted down to take the torque of a sudden stop.

Conservation at work. A skater pulling in her arms lowers \(I\); with \(L = I\omega\) fixed, \(\omega\) rises. A spinning rotor resists being tilted, because tilting it means changing the direction of a large \(\mathbf{L}\), which needs a torque. The proton, the electron and many nuclei carry an intrinsic angular momentum, spin, which is not anything turning but obeys the same rules; F7 and chapter 18 meet it.

8. Central forces and orbits

The inverse-square force. Gravity and the Coulomb force both point along the line between two bodies and fall as \(1/r^2\). Write an attraction towards the origin as

\[ \mathbf{F} = -\frac{k}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf{r}}, \qquad U(r) = -\frac{k}{r}, \]

with \(k = GMm\) for gravity or \(k = |q_1q_2|/4\pi\varepsilon_0\) for opposite charges (J m). A repulsion has the opposite sign. (Here \(k\) is the strength of the force, not a spring constant.) The heavy body is held at the origin; if it can move, replace \(m\) by the reduced mass \(\mu\) of section 6, and the problem becomes the motion relative to the centre of mass.

Circular orbits. For a circle of radius \(r\), the attraction supplies the force \(mv^2/r\) towards the centre (F2, section 4):

\[ \frac{mv^2}{r} = \frac{k}{r^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad W = \tfrac12mv^2 = \frac{k}{2r} . \]

The potential energy is \(U = -k/r\), so the total is

\[ E = W + U = -\frac{k}{2r} = -W . \]

The energy is negative: the body is bound, and it would take \(+k/2r\) of work to free it. Lower orbits are faster and more tightly bound. The relation \(W = -U/2\) for a \(1/r\) potential (the virial theorem, here in its simplest case) recurs in the atom.

Example: the hydrogen atom, classically. Treat the electron as circling the proton at the Bohr radius \(a_0 = 5.29 \times 10^{-11}\) m, with \(k = e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0 = 2.307 \times 10^{-28}\) J m. Then

\[ v = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m_ea_0}} = 2.19 \times 10^6\ \text{m/s}, \qquad E = -\frac{k}{2a_0} = -2.18 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{J} = -13.6\ \text{eV} . \]

It takes 13.6 eV to ionise hydrogen, which is right. The speed is \(1/137\) of the speed of light, so the electron is slow and Newton is a fair guide. But the picture cannot be the whole truth: an electron going round is accelerating, and an accelerating charge radiates (chapter 1), so the orbit should collapse in about \(10^{-11}\) s. Why it does not is quantum mechanics (F7). For the electrons in a storage ring the same radiation is real and sets the energy limit of every electron ring (chapter 20).

Example: a low orbit. For a satellite just above the Earth, gravity is \(mg\), so \(mv^2/R_E = mg\) and \(v = \sqrt{gR_E} = 7.9\) km/s, once round in 84 minutes. Escaping needs \(E \ge 0\), so a speed \(\sqrt2\) times larger, 11.2 km/s.

Orbits that are not circles. With angular momentum conserved, the energy of a particle in a central force can be written in terms of \(r\) alone. Split the velocity into a radial part \(\dot r\) and a sideways part \(r\dot\phi\); then \(L = mr^2\dot\phi\) and

\[ E = \tfrac12m\dot r^2 + \frac{L^2}{2mr^2} + U(r) . \]

The middle term, the sideways kinetic energy written with the fixed \(L\), acts as an extra potential energy that grows steeply at small \(r\): the centrifugal barrier. The radial motion is a one-dimensional problem in the effective potential \(U_{eff} = L^2/2mr^2 + U(r)\), and section 5’s picture applies. For an attraction \(-k/r\), \(U_{eff}\) has a valley: an orbit with \(E < 0\) swings between two turning points, the nearest and farthest approach, and is an ellipse with the centre of force at one focus (Kepler’s first law; the derivation is in Kleppner and Kolenkow, chapter 10). Its energy depends only on the long semi-axis \(a\), \(E = -k/2a\), and its period \(t_p\) obeys \(t_p^2 = 4\pi^2ma^3/k\) (Kepler’s third law). An orbit with \(E > 0\) is a hyperbola: the particle comes in from far away, swings round and leaves. A repulsion has only that kind. Rutherford scattering is such a hyperbola, and chapter 1’s pre-requisite derives the angle from conservation of energy and angular momentum.

9. Simple harmonic motion

Why it is everywhere. Take any potential energy \(U(x)\) with a minimum at \(x_0\). Near the minimum, Taylor’s series (F1, section 8) gives

\[ U(x) \approx U(x_0) + U'(x_0)(x - x_0) + \tfrac12U''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2 . \]

At a minimum \(U'(x_0) = 0\), and \(U''(x_0) > 0\). Measure \(x\) from \(x_0\) and drop the constant; what is left is \(U = \tfrac12kx^2\) with \(k = U''(x_0)\), and the force is \(F = -kx\): a pull back towards the minimum, in proportion to the distance. Every smooth valley looks like this when the motion is small. That is why the oscillator is the most important system in physics, and why a particle displaced slightly from the ideal path of an accelerator oscillates about it.

The motion. Newton’s law \(m\ddot x = -kx\) is F1’s oscillation equation, \(\ddot x = -\omega^2x\) with

\[ \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \qquad [\text{rad/s}] . \]

Its solution, \(x = A'\cos\omega t + B'\sin\omega t\), is better written with an amplitude \(A\) and a phase \(\phi\). By the addition formula, \(A\cos(\omega t + \phi) = A\cos\phi\cos\omega t - A\sin\phi\sin\omega t\), which is the same with \(A' = A\cos\phi\) and \(B' = -A\sin\phi\). So

\[ x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi), \qquad \dot x = -A\omega\sin(\omega t + \phi) . \]

The amplitude \(A\) is the largest displacement; the phase \(\phi\) says where in its cycle the motion was at \(t = 0\). Both are fixed by the start: a body released from rest at \(x = A\) has \(\phi = 0\). The motion repeats after a time \(t_p = 2\pi/\omega\), the period, and \(f = \omega/2\pi\) times per second, the frequency in hertz. The period does not depend on the amplitude: big and small swings take the same time. That was Galileo’s observation of a swinging lamp, and it is exactly true only for a perfectly linear force.

Energy. With \(x\) and \(\dot x\) above,

\[ E = \tfrac12m\dot x^2 + \tfrac12kx^2 = \tfrac12mA^2\omega^2\sin^2(\omega t + \phi) + \tfrac12kA^2\cos^2(\omega t + \phi) = \tfrac12kA^2, \]

since \(m\omega^2 = k\) and \(\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1\). The energy sloshes between kinetic and potential twice per cycle, and on average each has half. At the centre the body moves fastest, \(v_{max} = A\omega\); at the ends it stops.

Phase space. Plot \(x\) across and \(\dot x/\omega\) up. Since \(x^2 + (\dot x/\omega)^2 = A^2\), the point goes round a circle of radius \(A\) once per period, clockwise. Each oscillator is a point on its circle, its amplitude the radius and its phase the angle. A beam of many particles with different amplitudes and phases fills an area in this plane, and that area, the emittance, is one of the central quantities of the course (phase space, chapter 7).

Example: the pendulum. A mass \(m\) on a light rod of length \(\ell\) swung to angle \(\theta\) from the vertical has height \(\ell(1 - \cos\theta)\) above the bottom and \(U = mg\ell(1 - \cos\theta)\). The distance along its arc is \(s = \ell\theta\). Taylor: \(1 - \cos\theta \approx \theta^2/2\), so \(U \approx \tfrac12(mg/\ell)s^2\), a spring of stiffness \(k = mg/\ell\), and

\[ \omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{\ell}}, \qquad t_p = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{g}} . \]

A 1 m pendulum has a period of 2.006 s. The mass has cancelled: every pendulum of the same length keeps the same time, the equality of inertial and gravitational mass of section 2. The approximation fails as the swing grows, because the true \(U\) rises more slowly than \(\theta^2\); the period lengthens, by about \(\theta_0^2/16\) for an amplitude \(\theta_0\) in radians. At 10° that is 0.19 per cent; at 30° the rule gives 1.71 per cent and the exact answer is 1.74 per cent; at 90° it gives 15 per cent against the true 18 per cent. A particle in an RF bucket is exactly this pendulum, and near the top of its swing the period grows without bound (chapter 12).

Example: a proton in a focusing field. F2’s electric quadrupole, with \(V_0 = 10\) kV on electrodes \(a = 2\) cm from the axis, has the potential \(V = V_0x^2/a^2\) along \(x\), so a proton has \(U = eV_0x^2/a^2\) and

\[ k = U'' = \frac{2eV_0}{a^2} = 8.01 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{N/m}, \qquad \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m_p}} = 6.92 \times 10^7\ \text{rad/s}, \]

a frequency of 11.0 MHz. A proton swinging with an amplitude of 1 mm carries \(\tfrac12kA^2 = eV_0(A/a)^2 = 25\) eV of oscillation energy, however fast it moves along the axis. Along \(y\), the same quadrupole has \(U'' < 0\), a hilltop, and the proton is pushed away (Earnshaw, F2 section 12). The betatron oscillations of every ring are oscillations of this kind about the ideal orbit, with alternating quadrupoles making a valley in both planes (chapter 16).

10. Damped and driven oscillation

Damping. Real oscillations die away. Add a drag force opposite and proportional to the velocity, \(-b\dot x\), as for a body moving slowly through a viscous fluid, or a current through a resistor in an electrical circuit (F5). Newton’s law becomes

\[ m\ddot x + b\dot x + kx = 0, \qquad\text{or}\qquad \ddot x + \frac{\omega_0}{Q}\dot x + \omega_0^2x = 0, \]

with \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}\) the undamped frequency and the quality factor \(Q = m\omega_0/b\), a pure number: large \(Q\) means light damping.

How the energy decays. The drag does work at the rate \(-b\dot x \cdot \dot x\) (section 4), so the oscillator’s energy falls as

\[ \frac{dE}{dt} = -b\dot x^2 . \]

When the damping is light (\(Q \gg 1\)), the motion over one cycle is still nearly \(A\cos\omega_0t\) with \(A\) changing slowly, and \(\dot x^2\) averages to half its peak, \(\tfrac12A^2\omega_0^2 = E/m\). So, averaged over a cycle,

\[ \frac{dE}{dt} = -\frac{b}{m}E = -\frac{\omega_0}{Q}E \quad\Rightarrow\quad E = E_0\,e^{-\omega_0t/Q} . \]

This is F1’s decay equation. The energy falls with time constant \(\tau_E = Q/\omega_0\), and since energy goes as \(A^2\), the amplitude falls twice as slowly, with time constant \(2Q/\omega_0\). In one period \(2\pi/\omega_0\) the energy lost is \(2\pi E/Q\), which gives the definition used for cavities:

\[ Q = 2\pi\,\frac{\text{energy stored}}{\text{energy lost per cycle}} . \]

The energy falls to \(1/e\) in \(Q/2\pi\) cycles. (Solving the equation exactly gives \(x = A_0e^{-\omega_0t/2Q}\cos(\omega_1t + \phi)\) with \(\omega_1 = \omega_0\sqrt{1 - 1/4Q^2}\): the damping also lowers the frequency slightly. When \(Q < \tfrac12\) there is no oscillation at all, only a slow creep back. Chapter 7’s pre-requisite does this.)

Example: cavities. An accelerating cavity is an oscillator of electric and magnetic field, and its walls are its damping (chapter 13). A copper cell at 2856 MHz with \(Q_0 = 18\,400\) (\(Q_0\) is the course’s symbol for a cavity’s quality factor from its walls alone) loses its energy with \(\tau_E = Q_0/\omega_0 = 1.03\) μs, after about 2900 cycles. A niobium cavity at 1.3 GHz, superconducting at 2 K, has \(Q_0 \approx 10^{10}\) and, left to itself, would ring for \(\tau_E = 1.2\) s.

Driving. Now push the oscillator with a force that oscillates at a frequency \(\omega\) of our choosing, \(F_0\cos\omega t\):

\[ \ddot x + \frac{\omega_0}{Q}\dot x + \omega_0^2x = \frac{F_0}{m}\cos\omega t . \]

After the start-up dies away (in a time of order \(2Q/\omega_0\)), the oscillator moves at the drive frequency. Try \(x = C\cos\omega t + D\sin\omega t\). Putting it in and collecting the \(\cos\omega t\) and \(\sin\omega t\) terms separately (each must balance, since they are independent functions) gives two equations:

\[ (\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)C + \frac{\omega\omega_0}{Q}D = \frac{F_0}{m}, \qquad (\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)D - \frac{\omega\omega_0}{Q}C = 0 . \]

Solving them, the amplitude \(\sqrt{C^2 + D^2}\) is

\[ A(\omega) = \frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + (\omega\omega_0/Q)^2}} . \]

Resonance. Read it in three ranges. Driven slowly (\(\omega \ll \omega_0\)), \(A = F_0/m\omega_0^2 = F_0/k\): the static stretch of a spring under force \(F_0\). Driven fast (\(\omega \gg \omega_0\)), \(A \approx F_0/m\omega^2\), small: the body cannot follow. Driven at its own frequency, the first term in the root vanishes, \(C = 0\), and

\[ A(\omega_0) = Q\,\frac{F_0}{k} . \]

The response is \(Q\) times the static one: this is resonance. With \(C = 0\) the motion is \(\sin\omega_0t\), a quarter cycle behind the force, so the velocity is exactly in step with the force and the force does positive work all the time. That is the secret of a swing pushed once per swing, and of every resonant accelerator from the cyclotron on (chapter 9): small pushes, each in step, add up.

The width. Near resonance, \(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 \approx 2\omega_0(\omega_0 - \omega)\) and \(\omega\omega_0/Q \approx \omega_0^2/Q\). The two terms under the root are equal, and \(A^2\) (proportional to the stored energy) has fallen to half its peak, when \(|\omega_0 - \omega| = \omega_0/2Q\). So the resonance has a full width at half power of

\[ \Delta\omega = \frac{\omega_0}{Q}, \qquad \Delta f = \frac{f_0}{Q} . \]

A high \(Q\) means a large response over a narrow band, and a long ring-down: both are the same fact, the width times the decay time \(\tau_E\) being 1. The copper cell above responds over \(2856/18\,400\) MHz \(= 155\) kHz; the niobium cavity over 0.13 Hz, so narrow that in practice it is coupled strongly to its power line to widen it (chapter 13). The driven-oscillator pre-requisite in chapter 7 derives the full resonance curve and the phase, and F5 does all this more briefly with complex numbers.

11. Frames of reference

Galilean relativity. Let a frame \(S'\) move at constant velocity \(\mathbf{V}\) relative to a frame \(S\), their origins together at \(t = 0\). A point at \(\mathbf{r}\) in \(S\) is at

\[ \mathbf{r}' = \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{V}t, \qquad t' = t \]

in \(S'\). Differentiating, \(\mathbf{v}' = \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{V}\): velocities subtract. Differentiating again, \(\mathbf{a}' = \mathbf{a}\), since \(\mathbf{V}\) is constant. Accelerations are the same in both frames, masses and forces between bodies (which depend on their separations) are the same, so \(\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}\) holds in \(S'\) if it holds in \(S\). Every frame moving uniformly relative to an inertial frame is inertial, and none is special: Galileo’s ship. Momentum and kinetic energy do differ between frames (\(\mathbf{p}' = \mathbf{p} - m\mathbf{V}\)), but if they are conserved in one frame they are conserved in all. Section 6 used this freedom when it moved to the centre-of-mass frame.

Frames that accelerate. In a frame that accelerates, the first law fails: a body with no force on it seems to accelerate. Newton’s law can be rescued by adding fictitious forces, which come from the frame and not from any body. In a frame accelerating at \(\mathbf{a}_0\), every body seems to feel \(-m\mathbf{a}_0\), which is why passengers lurch forward when a bus brakes. In a frame rotating at \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) there are two:

  • the centrifugal force \(m\omega^2r\), outward from the axis, for a body at distance \(r\);
  • the Coriolis force \(-2m\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf{v}\), sideways to a body’s velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) in the rotating frame.

(Their derivation, by differentiating a vector in a turning frame, is in Kleppner and Kolenkow, chapter 9.) The Earth turns at \(\omega = 7.29 \times 10^{-5}\) rad/s, so the laboratory is not exactly inertial: the centrifugal acceleration at the equator is 0.034 m/s\(^2\), and Foucault’s pendulum of 1851 showed the Coriolis force turning its plane of swing. For a 2 kV electron the Coriolis acceleration is \(2\omega v = 3.9 \times 10^3\) m/s\(^2\), while the Earth’s magnetic field gives it \(2.3 \times 10^{14}\) m/s\(^2\): for beams the laboratory is inertial. Rotating frames return in accelerator physics as a convenience: the coordinates \(x\), \(y\), \(s\) of every ring turn with the reference orbit, and the equations of motion in them carry terms of this kind (chapter 16).

Where Galileo breaks. The rule \(\mathbf{v}' = \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{V}\) says that light, too, should move at different speeds in different frames. Michelson and Morley in 1887 found that it does not: light has the same speed \(c\) in every inertial frame. Newton’s mechanics also fails directly. Its kinetic energy \(\tfrac12mv^2\) has no ceiling, so with enough energy any speed can be reached: a 1 MeV electron, by \(v = \sqrt{2W/m_e}\), would move at 1.98\(c\). In 1964 William Bertozzi timed electrons from an electrostatic generator and a linear accelerator over a measured distance, at energies up to 15 MeV, and found that their speed crept towards \(c\) and never passed it, while their energy, measured by the heat they deposited in a target, kept growing; a 1 MeV electron moves at 0.941\(c\). Galileo’s frames are the low-speed limit of Einstein’s, and Newton’s formulas are the low-speed limits of the relativistic ones, good to 1 per cent for an electron up to 6.8 keV and for a proton up to 12.5 MeV (chapter 1). Chapter 1’s pre-requisite on Lorentz transformations replaces the transformation of this section, and section 2.1 there rebuilds momentum and energy. What survives unchanged: momentum and energy are conserved, \(\mathbf{F} = d\mathbf{p}/dt\), the work–energy theorem, and the magnetic force doing no work.

12. From forces to action

Two ways to say the same thing. Newton’s law works from instant to instant: given where a body is and how fast it moves now, the force says what it will do next. There is another way, which looks at the whole path at once. Fix where a body starts at time \(t_1\) and where it ends at \(t_2\). Of all the paths that join them, the body takes the one for which the action

\[ S = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}(W - U)\,dt \qquad [\text{J s}] \]

is stationary: no small change of the path changes \(S\) to first order, as no small step from the bottom of a valley changes the height to first order. This is the principle of least action, found in rough forms by Pierre-Louis Maupertuis and Euler in 1744, made exact by Joseph-Louis Lagrange in his Mécanique analytique of 1788 and by William Rowan Hamilton in 1834 and 1835. Fermat had found the same kind of law for light in 1662: light takes the path of least time.

A check with numbers. Throw a 1 kg ball straight up so that it lands 2 s later. The true path is \(x = \tfrac12gt(2 - t)\), rising 4.9 m. Its action, with \(W = \tfrac12m\dot x^2\) and \(U = mgx\), is \(S = -g^2/3 = -32.08\) J s. Now bend the path a little, keeping the ends fixed: \(x + \epsilon\sin(\pi t/2)\) with \(\epsilon = 0.1\) m. The action becomes larger by 0.0123 J s, and with \(\epsilon = 0.2\) m by 0.0493 J s: four times as much for twice the bend. The change grows as \(\epsilon^2\), with no part in proportion to \(\epsilon\): the true path is a stationary point, and every nearby path has more action.

Why bother. For a single particle in a simple force, the principle only repeats Newton (chapter 1 shows it gives exactly \(m\ddot x = -dU/dx\)). It earns its place in three ways the course needs. It works in any coordinates, such as the curved ones that follow a ring. It makes the link between symmetry and conservation plain: if the laws are the same at every place, momentum is conserved; if the same at every time, energy is; if the same in every direction, angular momentum is (Emmy Noether’s theorem of 1918). And a single number for a whole path is easy to make the same in every frame, which is how relativity and the magnetic force enter cleanly. From the action come the Lagrangian \(W - U\) and the Hamiltonian, the energy written in terms of position and momentum, and from the Hamiltonian the phase-space picture of section 9 in which beams are described.

All of that is taught in chapter 1’s Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics, which begins exactly where this section stops.

Exercises

  1. A proton starts at rest and crosses a gap of 10 cm with 100 kV across it, in a uniform field. Find its acceleration, its final speed and the time it takes.
Answer

\(\mathcal{E} = 10^5/0.1 = 10^6\) V/m, so \(a = e\mathcal{E}/m_p = 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \times 10^6/1.673 \times 10^{-27} = 9.58 \times 10^{13}\) m/s\(^2\). By energy, \(v = \sqrt{2eV/m_p} = 4.38 \times 10^6\) m/s (or from \(v^2 = 2ad\)). Starting from rest with constant acceleration, the average speed is \(v/2\), so \(t = 2d/v = 2 \times 0.1/4.38 \times 10^6 = 45.7\) ns, the same as \(v/a\).

  1. An electron from a 1 kV gun enters plates 3 cm long and 1 cm apart, with 40 V across them. Find the tangent of the angle at which it leaves, the sideways displacement at the exit, and the spot’s displacement on a screen 20 cm beyond the plates.
Answer

\(v_z = \sqrt{2e \times 1000/m_e}\); time in the plates \(t = l/v_z\); \(a_x = eV_d/(m_ed)\). Then \(v_x/v_z = a_xt/v_z = eV_dl/(m_edv_z^2) = lV_d/(2dV_a) = 0.03 \times 40/(2 \times 0.01 \times 1000) = 0.06\), an angle of \(3.4°\). At the exit \(x = \tfrac12a_xt^2 = 0.9\) mm, half of \(l\tan\theta\). On the screen: \(0.9 + 200 \times 0.06 = 12.9\) mm, which is \((l/2 + L)\tan\theta = 215 \times 0.06\) mm.

  1. Radium-226 at rest decays to radon-222 and an alpha particle, releasing \(Q = 4.871\) MeV. Using mass numbers for the masses, find the kinetic energy of each fragment and the speed of the radon.
Answer

Equal and opposite momenta, so \(W_\alpha = Q \times 222/226 = 4.785\) MeV and \(W_{Rn} = 0.086\) MeV. (The measured main alpha line is 4.784 MeV.) The radon moves at \(\sqrt{2W/m} = \sqrt{2 \times 0.0862 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-13}/(222 \times 1.661 \times 10^{-27})} = 2.7 \times 10^5\) m/s.

  1. The 5.30 MeV alphas of polonium-210 are aimed straight at (a) a gold nucleus (\(Z = 79\)) and (b) a copper nucleus (\(Z = 29\)), held fixed. How close do they get? Compare with the nuclear radii \(1.2A^{1/3}\) fm, \(A = 197\) and \(63\).
Answer

\(d = 2Z \times 1.440/5.30\) fm. (a) \(158 \times 1.440/5.30 = 42.9\) fm against a radius of 7.0 fm. (b) \(58 \times 1.440/5.30 = 15.7\) fm against 4.8 fm. Neither reaches the nucleus, so both scatter by the Coulomb force alone. (Allowing the nucleus to recoil reduces the available energy to \(W_{cm}\) and makes \(d\) slightly larger.)

  1. A 2 MeV neutron collides head on, elastically, with a carbon-12 nucleus at rest. What fraction of its energy does it lose? If every collision were head on, how many would it take to slow it to 0.025 eV, the energy of a neutron at room temperature?
Answer

The fraction handed over is \(4m_1m_2/(m_1 + m_2)^2 = 4 \times 12/13^2 = 0.284\), so the neutron keeps 0.716 and leaves with 1.43 MeV. After \(n\) collisions it has \(2\ \text{MeV} \times 0.716^n\); setting this to 0.025 eV gives \(n = \ln(8 \times 10^7)/\ln(1/0.716) = 54.5\), so 55. Real collisions are mostly glancing and lose less, and a neutron in graphite takes about twice as many. In hydrogen a head-on collision would stop it at once.

  1. For the reaction \(^{4}\mathrm{He} + {}^{14}\mathrm{N} \to {}^{17}\mathrm{O} + p\), \(Q = -1.192\) MeV. The masses are 4.0026 u and 14.0031 u. (a) Find the threshold kinetic energy of the alpha. (b) A 7.69 MeV alpha strikes a nitrogen nucleus at rest. How much kinetic energy is there in the centre-of-mass frame, and how much is left in that frame after the reaction?
Answer
  1. \(W_{th} = 1.192 \times (4.0026 + 14.0031)/14.0031 = 1.53\) MeV. (b) \(W_{cm} = 7.69 \times 14.0031/18.0057 = 5.98\) MeV; after paying 1.19 MeV, 4.79 MeV is left as kinetic energy of the oxygen and the proton in the centre-of-mass frame. The other \(7.69 - 5.98 = 1.71\) MeV belongs to the motion of the centre of mass and stays with it.
  1. A small turbomolecular pump has a rotor of 0.5 kg that can be treated as a uniform disc of radius 3 cm, turning at 60 000 rpm. Find its moment of inertia, angular momentum, stored energy and tip speed, and how long a motor torque of 0.02 N m takes to spin it up.
Answer

\(I = \tfrac12 \times 0.5 \times 0.03^2 = 2.25 \times 10^{-4}\) kg m\(^2\). \(\omega = 2\pi \times 1000 = 6283\) rad/s. \(L = I\omega = 1.41\) kg m\(^2\)/s. \(W = \tfrac12I\omega^2 = 4.4\) kJ, enough to lift a 70 kg person 6.5 m. Tip speed \(\omega R = 188\) m/s. Spin-up \(t = L/\tau = 1.41/0.02 = 71\) s.

  1. A geostationary satellite goes round once per sidereal day, 86 164 s, keeping over one spot on the equator. With \(GM_E = 3.986 \times 10^{14}\) m\(^3\)/s\(^2\), find the radius of its orbit and its speed.
Answer

For a circle, \(mv^2/r = GM_Em/r^2\) and \(v = 2\pi r/t_p\), so \(r^3 = GM_Et_p^2/4\pi^2\): \(r = 4.216 \times 10^7\) m, or 42 164 km from the Earth’s centre (35 786 km above the equator). \(v = 2\pi r/t_p = 3.07\) km/s. This is Kepler’s third law with \(k = GM_Em\).

  1. A pendulum 0.5 m long is released from 20°. Find its period from the small-swing formula, then with the correction \(\theta_0^2/16\), and its speed at the bottom (exactly, from energy conservation, and from simple harmonic motion).
Answer

\(t_p = 2\pi\sqrt{0.5/9.807} = 1.419\) s. With \(\theta_0 = 0.349\) rad, the correction is \(0.349^2/16 = 0.0076\), giving 1.430 s (the exact value is 1.4296 s). Energy: \(v = \sqrt{2g\ell(1 - \cos20°)} = 0.769\) m/s. Simple harmonic motion: \(v_{max} = A\omega = \ell\theta_0\sqrt{g/\ell} = 0.773\) m/s, 0.5 per cent too high, because the true potential rises more slowly than the parabola.

  1. A copper cavity at 500 MHz has \(Q_0 = 40\,000\). Find the time constant of its stored energy when the drive is switched off, the time for the energy to fall to 1 per cent, and the width of its resonance. If the drive is set half a width off resonance, what fraction of the peak amplitude does it reach?
Answer

\(\tau_E = Q_0/\omega_0 = 40\,000/(2\pi \times 5 \times 10^8) = 12.7\) μs. To 1 per cent: \(\tau_E\ln100 = 58.6\) μs. Width \(\Delta f = f_0/Q_0 = 12.5\) kHz. Half a width off (\(6.25\) kHz) is the half-power point, where the energy is half its peak, so the amplitude is \(1/\sqrt2 = 0.71\) of the peak.
