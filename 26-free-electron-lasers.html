In one page

The wall. A laser needs an atomic transition, which fixes its wavelength, and mirrors, which do not exist for hard X-rays. A storage ring makes X-rays, but its electrons radiate with random phases, so the power grows only as \(N\).

The idea. Send a bright electron bunch through a long undulator together with a light wave. If the light slips ahead of the electrons by one wavelength per undulator period, each electron sees a steady force along its wiggle and gains or loses energy according to its phase. The energy modulation turns into density modulation at the light’s wavelength (microbunching), the bunched electrons radiate in phase, as \(N^2\), and the stronger light bunches them harder. John Madey worked out the stimulated process in 1971; his group measured gain in 1976 and ran an oscillator in 1977. In a long enough undulator the loop amplifies the electrons’ own noise to saturation in one pass (SASE), which needs no mirror.

The equations to remember.

\[ \lambda_r = \frac{\lambda_u}{2\gamma^2}\left(1+\frac{K^2}{2}\right), \qquad K = 0.934\,B_0\,[\mathrm{T}]\,\lambda_u\,[\mathrm{cm}] \]

\[ \rho_F = \left[\frac{1}{16}\,\frac{I}{I_A}\,\frac{K^2[JJ]^2}{\gamma^3\sigma_x^2k_u^2}\right]^{1/3}, \qquad L_G = \frac{\lambda_u}{4\pi\sqrt{3}\,\rho_F}, \qquad P_{sat} \approx \rho_F P_b \]

\[ \sigma_\delta \lesssim \rho_F, \qquad \varepsilon \lesssim \frac{\lambda_r}{4\pi}, \qquad C = \frac{1}{|1 - R_{56}\chi|} \]

with \(\lambda_u\) the undulator period (m), \(K\) its strength, \(\rho_F\) the Pierce parameter, \(I\) the peak current (A), \(I_A = 17.045\) kA, \(\sigma_x\) the rms beam size (m), \(k_u = 2\pi/\lambda_u\), \(L_G\) the power gain length (m), \(P_b\) the beam power (W), \(C\) the compression factor of a chicane (in this chapter only; elsewhere \(C\) is a circumference) with \(R_{56}\) (m) acting on a chirp \(\chi = d\delta/dz\) (m\(^{-1}\)). Saturation comes after about 20 gain lengths, about \(\lambda_u/\rho_F\). An energy recovery linac sends the spent beam back through its cavities half a period late, so that it returns its energy; the RF then supplies only what holds the field, about \(\hat{V}^2\Delta f/((R/Q)f_{RF})\) per cavity for microphonic detuning \(\Delta f\).

Numbers worth knowing. LCLS first lased on 10 April 2009 at 1.5 Å: 13.64 GeV, 3.0 kA, \(\lambda_u = 3\) cm, \(K = 3.5\), gain length 3.3 m, saturation in about 60 m, 1.1 mJ per pulse. \(\rho_F \approx 6\times10^{-4}\); \(\lambda/4\pi = 12\) pm at 1.5 Å. The European XFEL reaches 17.5 GeV and has made up to 2.0 mJ pulses. Jefferson Lab’s energy-recovered FEL gave 14.3 kW CW at 1.6 µm in 2006.

The wall

Maiman’s ruby laser, at 694 nm, began the age of light in which all the emitters act in phase. A laser works by stimulated emission: a photon passing an atom that sits in an upper energy level can make it drop to a lower one and emit a second photon, identical to the first in wavelength, direction and phase. One photon becomes two, two become four, and all are in step. The photon’s energy must equal the gap between the two levels (F7, energy levels), so every laser was tied to a transition in an atom, a molecule or a crystal, which fixed its wavelength. And it amplifies only while more atoms sit in the upper level than in the lower (a population inversion); otherwise absorption by the lower atoms wins. At short wavelengths two more things failed. Normal-incidence mirrors (light striking the surface square on, so that it comes straight back) reflect almost nothing in the X-ray range, so light cannot be stored and passed many times through a medium. And the upper levels of short-wavelength transitions empty fast, so inversion is hard to hold.

Accelerators already made X-rays. A storage ring’s electrons radiate in dipoles and undulators (chapter 20), and Hans Motz at Stanford had sent linac electrons through a periodic magnet in the early 1950s (Motz, Thon and Whitehurst, 1953). But a bunch of \(N\) electrons radiates with random relative phases at wavelengths much shorter than the bunch, so the powers add: \(N\) times one electron. Only at wavelengths longer than the bunch do the fields add, giving \(N^2\). A bunch of \(10^9\) electrons is a factor \(10^9\) short of what it could give.

Microwave engineers knew how to get coherence from free electrons. Travelling-wave tubes, klystrons and the cyclotron maser of Hirshfield, Bernstein and Wachtel (which the 1976 Stanford paper cites) all bunch a beam at the wavelength they amplify. (A klystron is met in chapter 13. A travelling-wave tube sends a beam along a wire helix, a slow-wave structure, which guides a microwave forward at about the electrons’ speed so that the two stay in step; a cyclotron maser bunches electrons gyrating in a magnetic field.) They work at centimetres, where a cavity or a slow-wave structure can be built to the size of the wavelength. Nothing of the kind can be built at a micrometre or an ångström.

John Madey, who finished his doctorate at Stanford in 1970, saw that an undulator itself is the structure. In “Stimulated emission of bremsstrahlung in a periodic magnetic field” (Journal of Applied Physics, 1971) he calculated, by the Weizsäcker-Williams method (which treats the magnet’s field, seen by the fast electron, as a flux of photons coming towards it, so that the process becomes a stimulated Compton scattering: an electron scattering a photon, here helped along by the light already present, as stimulated emission is helped in an atom), that electrons crossing a periodic transverse field together with a light wave emit at a slightly longer wavelength than they absorb, so that net gain appears on one side of the spontaneous line. The wavelength is set by the electron energy and the magnet period, so it can be tuned anywhere.

The first machines confirmed it, and also showed the next wall. The gain per pass was a few per cent in the infrared, and it falls at shorter wavelengths (Deacon and colleagues said so in 1977). An oscillator needs gain above its mirror losses. At wavelengths shorter than the ultraviolet there were neither good mirrors nor enough gain. The way out, found by theorists between 1980 and 1984, was to make the gain so large that one pass through a long undulator is enough. That demanded electron beams brighter than any that existed: kiloamperes of current, energy spreads of \(10^{-4}\), geometric emittances of about ten picometres. It took two decades of photoinjectors (chapter 7), bunch compressors and diagnostics to make them.

The physics

Pre-requisite (continued): Complex numbers and complex functions

Pre-requisite. Chapter 7 taught complex numbers in polar form, \(re^{i\theta}\), and their powers; chapter 9 the complex amplitude whose real part is the physical signal, and the complex conjugate; chapter 16 numbers on the unit circle and a complex frequency whose real or imaginary part is a rate of growth. This section adds three tools. First, the real and imaginary parts written with the conjugate, and the rule that an average over many electrons drops any term that turns with their phase. Second, a complex amplitude that changes slowly along the undulator and carries both the size and the phase of the light. Third, the \(n\) roots of a complex number, in particular the three cube roots of \(i\), whose real parts decide which of the high-gain FEL’s three modes grows. Section 1 uses sums of phasors; section 6 uses all three. Taught from Penrose, The Road to Reality, §5.1 (multiplication as a turn and a stretch) and §5.3 to §5.4 (the many values of a logarithm, roots and the \(n\)th roots of unity), and Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 5, section I.2 (the three roots of the high-gain FEL).

This chapter writes the imaginary unit as \(i\), as physics texts do; chapters 9 and 13 wrote \(j\), as engineers do. Nothing else changes.

A sum of phasors. A phasor here is a complex number of modulus 1, such as \(e^{-i\varphi}\): an arrow of unit length at angle \(-\varphi\). Section 1 adds one arrow per electron, \(\sum_je^{-ikz_j}\), and takes the square of the modulus. For any complex number \(X\), \(|X|^2 = XX^*\), where the conjugate \(X^*\) is \(X\) with \(i\) replaced by \(-i\). So

\[ \left|\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{-ikz_j}\right|^2 = \sum_{j}\sum_{l}e^{-ikz_j}\,e^{+ikz_l} = N + \sum_{j\ne l}e^{-ik(z_j - z_l)} . \]

The \(N\) terms with \(j = l\) are each 1. The other \(N(N - 1)\) depend only on the distances between electrons. This is the sum of chapter 20, written with exponentials instead of cosines; the exponential form is shorter because a product of exponentials is one exponential. (F5 drew oscillations as turning arrows; here each arrow is one electron’s contribution, and its angle is fixed by where that electron sits.)

Figure 26.1. Adding one unit arrow per electron, for \(N = 20\). Left: the angles \(kz_j\) are random (drawn from a fixed random sequence), the arrows wander, and the sum ends about \(\sqrt N = 4.5\) from the start; its squared length is about \(N\) on average. Right: the electrons sit a whole number of wavelengths apart, every arrow points the same way, and the sum is \(N = 20\) long, its square \(N^2 = 400\). The power radiated goes as the squared length of the sum.

Real and imaginary parts by the conjugate. Write \(X = x + iy\), with \(x\) and \(y\) real. Then \(X^* = x - iy\). Adding and subtracting the two,

\[ \mathrm{Re}\,X = \frac{X + X^*}{2}, \qquad \mathrm{Im}\,X = \frac{X - X^*}{2i} . \]

Check with \(X = 3 + 4i\): \(X - X^* = 8i\), and \(8i/2i = 4\). These forms help because, when \(X\) is an exponential, each piece is an exponential too, and exponentials are easy to average.

At work. Section 6 needs the average over the electrons of \(e^{-i\theta}\,\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_Le^{i\theta}]\), where \(\theta\) is each electron’s phase and \(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L\) is the same for all of them. With the formula,

\[ e^{-i\theta}\,\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_Le^{i\theta}] = e^{-i\theta}\,\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_Le^{i\theta} - \tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L^*e^{-i\theta}}{2i} = \frac{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L - \tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L^*e^{-2i\theta}}{2i} , \]

and averaging,

\[ \left\langle e^{-i\theta}\,\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_Le^{i\theta}]\right\rangle = \frac{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L - \tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L^*\,\langle e^{-2i\theta}\rangle}{2i} . \]

The first term survives. The second carries \(\langle e^{-2i\theta}\rangle\), which turns with the electrons’ phases. For electrons spread evenly over all phases it is exactly zero: \(N\) arrows equally spaced round the circle (twice round, for the factor 2) close into a polygon and sum to nothing. For a beam that is only slightly bunched it is small, and since it multiplies the field, which is also small at the start, the product is of second order and is dropped. Dividing by \(2i\) is the same as multiplying by \(-i/2\), so what is left is \(-\tfrac{i}{2}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L\): a quarter period of phase between the field and the energy modulation it makes.

A slowly varying complex amplitude. A light wave of fixed amplitude \(\mathcal{E}_L\) (V/m) and phase \(\phi_0\) is \(\mathcal{E}_x = \mathcal{E}_L\cos(ks - \omega t + \phi_0)\). Put both numbers into one complex amplitude, \(\tilde{\mathcal{E}} = \mathcal{E}_Le^{i\phi_0}\). Then

\[ \mathcal{E}_x = \mathrm{Re}\left[\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\,e^{i(ks - \omega t)}\right], \]

because \(\mathrm{Re}[\mathcal{E}_Le^{i(ks - \omega t + \phi_0)}] = \mathcal{E}_L\cos(ks - \omega t + \phi_0)\). The modulus of \(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\) is the amplitude and its argument is the phase. In an FEL the light grows and its phase shifts, but slowly: over metres, while the carrier \(e^{i(ks - \omega t)}\) repeats every 1.5 Å. So let \(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\) depend on \(s\), and keep the carrier separate. All the physics of growth sits in \(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(s)\).

At work. Section 6 finds that at LCLS the growing light has \(\tilde{\mathcal{E}} \propto e^{\mu_1s}\) with \(\mu_1 = 0.212 + 0.122i\) m\(^{-1}\). The real part makes the modulus grow by a factor \(e\) every \(1/0.212 = 4.72\) m. The imaginary part adds 0.122 rad per metre to the phase, so the total phase is \((k + 0.122\ \mathrm{m^{-1}})s - \omega t\): the amplified light’s crests travel slower than \(c\) by the fraction \(0.122/k = 2.9\times10^{-12}\), held back by the electrons that make it. Over one wavelength of 1.5 Å the modulus changes by \(0.212\times1.5\times10^{-10} = 3\times10^{-11}\) of itself. That is what “slowly varying” means here, and the continuation of Maxwell’s equations before section 3 builds on it.

The \(n\) roots of a complex number. Which numbers \(w\) satisfy \(w^n = Z\)? Write \(Z = Re^{i\Phi}\) and try \(w = re^{i\alpha}\). Then \(w^n = r^ne^{in\alpha}\), and this equals \(Z\) when the lengths agree, \(r^n = R\), and the angles agree up to whole turns, \(n\alpha = \Phi + 2\pi m\) for any integer \(m\) (Penrose’s point in §5.3: an angle, like a logarithm, has many values). So

\[ r = R^{1/n}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{\Phi + 2\pi m}{n}, \qquad m = 0, 1, \ldots, n - 1 . \]

Taking \(m = n\) gives the same number as \(m = 0\), so there are exactly \(n\) roots. They lie on a circle of radius \(R^{1/n}\), spaced by \(2\pi/n\): the corners of a regular polygon. By that symmetry they sum to zero for \(n \ge 2\).

The cube roots of \(i\). Here \(Z = i = e^{i\pi/2}\), so \(R = 1\), \(\Phi = \pi/2\) and \(n = 3\):

\[ \alpha = \frac{\pi}{6},\ \ \frac{\pi}{6} + \frac{2\pi}{3} = \frac{5\pi}{6},\ \ \frac{\pi}{6} + \frac{4\pi}{3} = \frac{3\pi}{2}\ (\text{the same as } -\tfrac{\pi}{2}) . \]

\[ e^{i\pi/6} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2} + \frac{i}{2}, \qquad e^{i5\pi/6} = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2} + \frac{i}{2}, \qquad e^{-i\pi/2} = -i . \]

Check the last: \((-i)^3 = -i^3 = -(-i) = i\). The real parts are \(\sqrt3/2 = 0.866\), \(-0.866\) and 0.

Figure 26.2. The three cube roots of \(i\) on the unit circle, at \(30°\), \(150°\) and \(270°\): the corners of an equilateral triangle. Cubing a root triples its angle, and \(3\times30° = 90°\), \(3\times150° = 450° = 90° + 360°\) and \(3\times270° = 810° = 90° + 2\times360°\), so all three land on \(i\). Only the root on the right half of the plane has a positive real part; a quantity \(e^{\mu s}\) with \(\mu\) along it grows. If a quantity goes as \(e^{\mu s}\) with \(\mu\) equal to a positive number \(a\) times one of these roots, the first grows as \(e^{0.866as}\), the second decays and the third only turns. That is the whole algebra of the FEL instability: three derivatives round a loop give \(\mu^3 = ia^3\), and one cube root of \(i\) has a positive real part.

At work. For LCLS, section 6 gives \(a = 2k_u\rho_F = 2\times209.4\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\times5.84\times10^{-4} = 0.2446\) m\(^{-1}\). The growing root is \(\mu_1 = 0.2446\,e^{i\pi/6} = 0.2118 + 0.1223i\) m\(^{-1}\). The field grows by \(e\) in \(1/0.2118 = 4.72\) m and the power, which goes as the square, in half that, 2.36 m: the one-dimensional gain length.

Exercise A1. Find the three cube roots of \(-i\) and their real parts. Which one grows?

Answer

\(-i = e^{-i\pi/2}\), so \(\alpha = -\pi/6\), \(-\pi/6 + 2\pi/3 = \pi/2\) and \(-\pi/6 + 4\pi/3 = 7\pi/6\). The roots are \(0.866 - 0.5i\), \(i\) and \(-0.866 - 0.5i\). The first grows, as fast as before, but its phase turns the other way. The roots of \(-i\) are the conjugates of the roots of \(i\).

Exercise A2. Show that the three cube roots of \(i\) sum to zero, and that their squares also sum to zero. (The ordinary-differential-equation continuation before section 6 uses both facts.)

Answer

Sum: real parts \(0.866 - 0.866 + 0 = 0\); imaginary parts \(0.5 + 0.5 - 1 = 0\). Squares: \(e^{i\pi/3} + e^{i5\pi/3} + e^{-i\pi} = (0.5 + 0.5 - 1) + i(0.866 - 0.866 + 0) = 0\). Both follow from the polynomial \(\mu^3 - i\), which has no \(\mu^2\) and no \(\mu\) term.

Pre-requisite (continued): Fourier series and transforms

Pre-requisite. Chapter 8 found the spectrum of an oscillation recorded for a finite time, a peak about one over the duration wide; chapter 9 the sinc function as the transform of a uniform profile; and chapter 20 the form factor of a bunch and the line of an undulator of \(N\) periods. This section does three things with them. It shows that section 1’s bunching factor is the form factor under another name. It writes the undulator’s line in the variable that FEL theory uses, the detuning \(\nu\), where its shape is \([\sin(\nu/2)/(\nu/2)]^2\). And it works out the slope of that line: zero at the centre, largest (0.270) at \(\nu = 2.61\), and with an integral of zero over all detunings. Section 1 uses the first; section 5 uses the slope for Madey’s theorem and the oscillator’s gain. Taught from Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 4 (the line width of undulator radiation) and chapter 5, section I.1 (the line-shape function \(S(\tau)\) and the gain function \(F(\tau)\)), and Chao and Tigner, Handbook, §3.1.10 (Madey’s theorem compared with the high-gain theory).

The bunching factor is a Fourier transform. Section 1 defines \(b(k) = (1/N)\sum_je^{-ikz_j}\). If the electrons are drawn independently from a line density \(\psi(z)\) (m\(^{-1}\), with unit area), the average of each term is \(\int\psi(z)e^{-ikz}dz\), so the expected bunching is

\[ \bar b(k) = \int\psi(z)\,e^{-ikz}\,dz , \]

the Fourier transform of the profile at wavenumber \(k\). Chapter 20’s form factor \(g\) is the same integral with \(e^{+ikz}\); for a real \(\psi\) the two are complex conjugates and \(|\bar b| = |g|\). For a Gaussian of rms length \(\sigma_z\), \(|\bar b| = e^{-k^2\sigma_z^2/2}\).

At work. A 250 pC LCLS bunch at 3 kA has \(\sigma_t = Q/(\sqrt{2\pi}I) = 33\) fs, \(\sigma_z = 10\) µm. At 1.5 Å, \(k\sigma_z = 4.2\times10^5\), and \(|\bar b| = e^{-8.7\times10^{10}}\): zero in every sense. The smooth shape of the bunch contributes nothing at X-ray wavelengths. All the bunching there must come from the randomness of the electrons’ positions (the next continuation) and from the FEL’s own microbunching.

The line of a finite wave train. An electron crossing \(N_u\) periods sends a wave train of \(N_u\) cycles at angular frequency \(\omega_1\), lasting \(\tau_w = 2\pi N_u/\omega_1\). Model its field as \(e^{i\omega_1t}\) for \(|t| < \tau_w/2\) and zero outside. Its transform at angular frequency \(\omega\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_{-\tau_w/2}^{\tau_w/2}e^{i(\omega_1 - \omega)t}\,dt &= \frac{2\sin[(\omega - \omega_1)\tau_w/2]}{\omega - \omega_1} = \tau_w\,\frac{\sin x}{x}, \\ x &= \frac{(\omega - \omega_1)\tau_w}{2} = \pi N_u\,\frac{\omega - \omega_1}{\omega_1} . \end{aligned} \]

The energy radiated per unit frequency goes as the square. Writing \(\nu = 2x\),

\[ S(\nu) = \left[\frac{\sin(\nu/2)}{\nu/2}\right]^2, \qquad \nu = 2\pi N_u\,\frac{\omega - \omega_1}{\omega_1} . \]

\(S\) is 1 at the centre, falls to zero at \(\nu = \pm2\pi\), which is \(\Delta\omega/\omega_1 = \pm1/N_u\), and has half its peak at \(\nu = \pm2.78\), a full width of 5.57 in \(\nu\) or \(0.886/N_u\) in relative frequency: chapter 20’s figure for the fundamental. Near the peak, chapter 20’s grating function \(\sin^2(N\varphi/2)/\sin^2(\varphi/2)\) becomes \(N^2S\), since \(\sin(\varphi/2) \approx \varphi/2\) there.

Detuning measured by the electron’s energy. An FEL holds the light’s frequency fixed and asks how electrons of slightly different energy respond. The resonant wavelength goes as \(1/\gamma^2\), so the resonant frequency goes as \(\gamma^2\): an electron with \(\gamma = \gamma_r(1 + \delta)\) has its line centred at \(\omega_1 = \omega_r(1 + 2\delta)\). Seen from that electron, the light is detuned by \(2\pi N_u(\omega_r - \omega_1)/\omega_1 \approx -4\pi N_u\delta\). FEL texts flip the sign so that the detuning is positive for electrons above resonance:

\[ \nu = 4\pi N_u\,\delta . \]

The line is symmetric, so \(S(\nu)\) is unchanged; only the sign of its slope depends on the choice. The line is zero at \(\delta = \pm1/(2N_u)\): an electron that far off resonance hardly radiates at the light’s frequency at all. For Stanford’s 162 periods this is \(3.1\times10^{-3}\).

The slope of the line. Section 5 needs \(-dS/d\nu\). With \(x = \nu/2\), \(S = \sin^2x/x^2\), and

\[ \frac{dS}{dx} = \frac{2\sin x\cos x}{x^2} - \frac{2\sin^2x}{x^3} = \frac{2\sin x}{x^3}\left(x\cos x - \sin x\right), \qquad \frac{dS}{d\nu} = \frac{1}{2}\frac{dS}{dx} . \]

Three facts follow.

  • Zero at the centre. Near \(\nu = 0\), \(\sin x/x \approx 1 - x^2/6\), so \(S \approx 1 - x^2/3 = 1 - \nu^2/12\) and \(-dS/d\nu \approx \nu/6\): zero on resonance, positive above, negative below.
  • A largest value. Setting the derivative of \(-dS/d\nu\) to zero has no closed form; a numerical search gives the maximum \(0.2701\) at \(\nu = 2.606\). Lee’s gain function \(F(\tau)\) is a quarter of this slope with the opposite sign, and his extreme value 0.0675 is \(0.270/4\).
  • Zero on balance. \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(-dS/d\nu)\,d\nu = S(-\infty) - S(\infty) = 0\). Whatever is gained above resonance is lost below.

The third fact has a practical meaning. A beam whose energy spread covers the line evenly samples positive and negative slopes equally and gives no net gain. The spread must be small compared with the line, about \(1/(2N_u)\) in \(\delta\), as section 5 and Lee both say.

Figure 26.3. The undulator’s line \(S(\nu) = [\sin(\nu/2)/(\nu/2)]^2\) (grey) and its negative slope \(-dS/d\nu\) (red), both computed from the formulas above. The slope is zero at the centre, largest, 0.270, at \(\nu = 2.61\), and an exact mirror image with the opposite sign below resonance, so its area over all \(\nu\) is zero. Section 5’s low gain follows the red curve: electrons above resonance (\(\nu > 0\)) give energy to the light, electrons below take it.

At work. For Stanford’s 162-period undulator the gain is largest at \(\delta_0 = 2.61/(4\pi\times162) = 1.28\times10^{-3}\), 31 keV above resonance in a beam of 24.5 MeV total energy. The light then sits on the low-frequency side of these electrons’ line, at a slightly longer wavelength than they would radiate on their own. This is the result of Madey’s 1971 paper described in “The wall”: net emission on the long-wavelength side of the line.

Pre-requisite (continued): Random signals and noise, spectral density

Pre-requisite. Chapter 8 described a random signal by its mean square and its spectral density and noted that random phases add in power; chapter 19 added the autocorrelation; chapter 20 showed that \(N\) random phasors give \(N\) on average, with a large scatter. This section turns to the randomness of the electrons’ own positions. A beam with no structure still has a bunching factor: \(|b|^2\) is \(1/N\) on average, with an exponential spread about that mean. The \(N\) that counts is the number of electrons in a coherence length. Light amplified from such noise has a spectrum of spikes, each about one over the pulse duration wide, and a pulse energy that fluctuates by \(1/\sqrt{M}\) when there are \(M\) spikes. Section 1 uses the first result; section 7 uses all of them. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, Statistical Physics, §110 and §111 (the Gaussian distribution of one and of several fluctuating quantities), Lee, Accelerator Physics, appendix A, section II.1 (the central limit theorem), and Chao and Tigner, Handbook, §3.1.10 (shot noise as the start of SASE, the coherence length, and the gamma distribution of pulse energies).

The bunching of random positions. Take \(N\) electrons placed independently at random along a length much longer than the wavelength. Their phases \(\theta_j = kz_j\) are then spread evenly over \(0\) to \(2\pi\), and

\[ b = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{-i\theta_j} . \]

Each term averages to zero, so \(\langle b\rangle = 0\). But \(|b|^2\) does not average to zero. From the sum of phasors in the complex-numbers continuation above,

\[ N^2|b|^2 = N + \sum_{j\ne l}e^{-i(\theta_j - \theta_l)} . \]

For independent phases each cross term averages to \(\langle e^{-i\theta_j}\rangle\langle e^{+i\theta_l}\rangle = 0\), so

\[ \langle|b|^2\rangle = \frac{1}{N} . \]

The typical size of \(|b|\) is \(1/\sqrt N\): \(N\) unit steps in random directions end about \(\sqrt N\) from the start. If instead the positions are drawn from a profile with expected bunching \(\bar b\) (the Fourier continuation above), each cross term averages to \(\bar b\,\bar b^* = |\bar b|^2\), and

\[ \langle N^2|b|^2\rangle = N + N(N - 1)|\bar b|^2 , \]

the formula quoted in section 1: shot noise plus the coherent part of the profile. At X-ray wavelengths \(\bar b\) is zero for any real bunch, so only the shot noise is left, and the radiated power is \(N\) times one electron’s.

How it scatters. Write \(b = u + iv\). The real part \(u = (1/N)\sum\cos\theta_j\) is a sum of \(N\) independent pieces, each of mean zero and mean square \(\langle\cos^2\theta\rangle/N^2 = 1/(2N^2)\); the same holds for \(v\), and \(\langle\cos\theta\sin\theta\rangle = 0\), so \(u\) and \(v\) are uncorrelated. A sum of many independent pieces has a Gaussian distribution, whatever the pieces look like: the central limit theorem, as Lee’s appendix states it. (Landau and Lifshitz reach the same Gaussian form by another route in §110, from the entropy near its maximum, and show in §111 that uncorrelated Gaussian variables are independent, so their joint density is a product.) So \(\sqrt N u\) and \(\sqrt N v\) are independent Gaussians of variance \(\tfrac12\), with joint density

\[ p(\sqrt Nu, \sqrt Nv) = \frac{1}{\pi}\,e^{-N(u^2 + v^2)} . \]

Let \(x = N|b|^2 = N(u^2 + v^2)\), the square of the distance from the origin in that plane. The ring between \(x\) and \(x + dx\) has area \(\pi\,dx\) (area \(\pi r^2 = \pi x\) inside radius \(r\)), so

\[ p(x) = e^{-x} , \]

an exponential distribution with mean 1 and an rms spread about that mean also of 1. The power of the noise at one wavelength, in one stretch of beam, is exponentially distributed: often much less than the mean, sometimes several times more. The chance of exceeding three times the mean is \(e^{-3} = 5\) per cent. A check by simulation: 4000 sets of 1000 random phases gave a mean of \(N|b|^2\) of 1.006, an rms spread of 1.003, and 5.2 per cent of the sets above 3.

Which \(N\). An FEL amplifies each stretch of the bunch on its own, because light slips ahead of the electrons by only about a cooperation length per gain length (section 7). Stretches further apart than a coherence length, about \(2\pi L_c = \lambda_r/(2\rho_F)\), grow independently, each from its own random \(b\). So the \(N\) that sets the starting bunching is \(N_c\), the number of electrons in one coherence length. (The Handbook’s coherence length at saturation, \(\lambda_r/(2\sqrt\pi\rho_F)\), is shorter by a factor \(\sqrt\pi = 1.8\); at this level of estimate the factor does not matter.)

At work. LCLS at 3 kA carries \(I/(ec) = 6.25\times10^{13}\) electrons per metre, 9400 in each 1.5 Å wavelength. With \(\rho_F = 5.84\times10^{-4}\) the coherence length is \(1.5\times10^{-10}/(2\times5.84\times10^{-4}) = 0.128\) µm, holding \(N_c = 8.0\times10^6\) electrons. The starting bunching is \(|b| \approx 1/\sqrt{N_c} = 3.5\times10^{-4}\), different in each coherence length along the bunch.

A spectrum of spikes. Now take a pulse of duration \(\tau\) built from many independent random contributions, as SASE light is. At each frequency its spectral amplitude is again a sum of many random phasors, so its spectral power is exponentially distributed, as above. How fast does it change with frequency? A contribution at time \(t\) enters the amplitude at angular frequency \(\omega\) with phase \(\omega t\). At \(\omega + \Delta\omega\) its phase is larger by \(\Delta\omega\,t\), and across the pulse that extra phase spans \(\Delta\omega\,\tau\). If \(\Delta\omega\,\tau \ll 2\pi\) the two sums are nearly the same; if \(\Delta\omega\,\tau \gtrsim 2\pi\) they are independent. The spectrum is therefore a row of spikes about \(\Delta f \approx 1/\tau\) wide, with random heights, under whatever envelope the amplifier allows. A simulation of complex noise with a flat envelope lasting \(\tau\) gives a spectrum whose autocorrelation (with the mean taken off) falls to half at \(0.44/\tau\), as the square of the flat envelope’s sinc transform predicts: spikes about \(1/\tau\) wide, as argued. Across an envelope of width \(B\) in frequency there are about \(M \approx B\tau\) spikes.

At work. A 75 fs LCLS pulse at 1.5 Å (\(f = c/\lambda = 2.0\times10^{18}\) Hz) has spikes \(1/\tau = 1.3\times10^{13}\) Hz wide: a relative width of \(6.7\times10^{-6}\), 0.055 eV at 8.27 keV. The envelope is about \(\rho_F\) wide, 4.8 eV, so the spectrum holds about \(\rho_Ff\tau \approx 90\) spikes: of order a hundred, as section 7 counts in time.

The pulse energy fluctuates. The pulse energy is a sum over \(M\) independent spikes, each exponentially distributed with relative rms 1. Independent contributions add in variance (counting statistics uses the same rule), so the sum of \(M\) has a relative rms of

\[ \sigma_u = \frac{1}{\sqrt M} . \]

The Handbook gives the full distribution of the normalised pulse energy \(u\) as a gamma distribution with exactly this variance. Simulated sums of exponential spikes gave relative spreads of 0.314 for \(M = 10\) and 0.099 for \(M = 100\), against \(1/\sqrt{10} = 0.316\) and 0.100. With about a hundred spikes, a SASE pulse in the exponential regime fluctuates by about 10 per cent from shot to shot. Near saturation every spike reaches about the same power and the fluctuations shrink.

1. Undulator radiation and coherence

Recall from chapter 20: a planar undulator has field \(B_y(s) = B_0\cos(k_u s)\) with period \(\lambda_u = 2\pi/k_u\). An electron of energy \(\gamma mc^2\) wiggles with angle \(x' = (K/\gamma)\sin(k_u s)\), where

\[ K = \frac{eB_0\lambda_u}{2\pi m_e c} = 0.934\,B_0\,[\mathrm{T}]\,\lambda_u\,[\mathrm{cm}] . \]

It radiates forward at the undulator wavelength derived there, with a line width of about \(1/N_u\) for \(N_u\) periods.

For \(N\) electrons at longitudinal positions \(z_j\), the field at wavenumber \(k\) is the sum of single-electron fields with phases \(kz_j\). Every electron makes the same wave train, only shifted in time: an electron a distance \(z_j\) further on emits it \(z_j/c\) earlier, which at wavenumber \(k\) is a phase of \(kz_j\). So the total field is one electron’s field times the sum of arrows \(\sum_je^{-ikz_j}\) (Figure 26.1), and the power, which goes as the square of the field, is

\[ P(k) = P_1(k)\left|\sum_{j} e^{-ikz_j}\right|^2 = N^2|b(k)|^2P_1(k), \]

with \(P_1\) the single-electron power and \(b(k) = \langle e^{-ikz}\rangle = (1/N)\sum_je^{-ikz_j}\) the bunching factor at that wavenumber, averaged over the electrons. (Averaged over many bunches of the same smooth profile, \(N^2|b|^2\) becomes \(N + N(N-1)|\bar b|^2\), with \(\bar b\) the Fourier transform of the profile; the term \(N\) is the shot noise.) For a smooth bunch much longer than the wavelength, \(|b|^2\) is of order \(1/N\) (shot noise) and the power is about \(NP_1\). If the bunch is modulated at the wavelength, \(|b|\) approaches 1 and the power approaches \(N^2P_1\). Making \(|b|\) large at the light’s own wavelength is the whole aim.

Pre-requisite (continued): Relativistic motion in electric and magnetic fields

Pre-requisite. Chapter 1 showed that only the electric field changes a particle’s energy, \(d(\gamma mc^2)/dt = q\mathbf{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\), so that a magnetic field turns a particle without changing its speed, and later continuations applied the Lorentz force to kicks, drifts and gyration. This section adds what an undulator needs. At fixed energy, any sideways velocity is taken from the forward velocity, so an electron that wiggles, or that is tilted by a small angle, moves forward more slowly. A wiggling electron’s forward speed also oscillates at twice the wiggle frequency, which is where the factor \([JJ]\) of section 3 comes from. Section 2 uses the mean forward speed for the resonance condition, section 3 the rate of work and the oscillation, and section 8 the tilted electron for its emittance rule. It uses \(1 - \beta \approx 1/(2\gamma^2)\) from chapter 20. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, The Classical Theory of Fields, §17 (the equation of motion of a charge, and the work done by the electric field alone), and Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 4 (the electron’s path and mean longitudinal speed in an undulator) and chapter 5, section I (its energy exchange with a light wave).

Energy changes only through \(\mathbf{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\). The rate of change of energy is \(q\mathbf{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\); the magnetic force, at right angles to \(\mathbf{v}\), does no work. In an FEL the light is a transverse wave with \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) along \(x\), so

\[ \frac{d(\gamma mc^2)}{dt} = q\,v_x\mathcal{E}_x . \]

An electron going straight down the axis has \(v_x = 0\) and cannot exchange any energy with the light, however strong the field. The undulator’s whole job is to give it a sideways velocity, \(v_x = cx' = (cK/\gamma)\sin(k_us)\), that keeps step with the light’s field (section 2 arranges the step).

At work. At LCLS the peak wiggle angle is \(K/\gamma = 3.5/26\,694 = 1.31\times10^{-4}\) rad. Dividing the rate of work by the forward speed \(v_s \approx c\) gives the energy change per metre, at most \(e(K/\gamma)\mathcal{E}_x\). In a light field of 10 GV/m that is 1.31 MeV per metre at the best moment of a period. Averaged over a period, two oscillations that keep in step give half the product of their peaks (the mean of \(\sin^2\) is \(\tfrac12\)), which is section 3’s first equation: 0.66 MeV per metre. The wobble described below takes a further factor \([JJ] = 0.744\), leaving 0.49 MeV per metre for an electron at the best phase.

Sideways speed is taken from forward speed. The energy fixes the speed: \(\beta^2 = 1 - 1/\gamma^2\). The magnetic field keeps \(\gamma\) constant, so \(\beta_x^2 + \beta_s^2 = \beta^2\) is fixed too, and

\[ \beta_s = \sqrt{\beta^2 - \beta_x^2} \approx \beta - \frac{\beta_x^2}{2} \]

for \(\beta_x \ll 1\) and \(\beta \approx 1\) (the first two terms of \(\sqrt{1 - \epsilon} \approx 1 - \epsilon/2\)). With \(1 - \beta \approx 1/(2\gamma^2)\),

\[ 1 - \beta_s \approx \frac{1}{2\gamma^2} + \frac{\beta_x^2}{2} . \]

Two small terms, one from the energy and one from the sideways motion. For an X-ray FEL both are of order \(10^{-9}\), and both matter, because the light gains on the electron by \(1 - \beta_s\) of the distance travelled and the resonance needs that gain to be one wavelength per period.

A tilted electron. Let an electron travel straight at a small angle \(\theta_e\) to the axis, so \(\beta_x \approx \theta_e\):

\[ 1 - \beta_s \approx \frac{1}{2\gamma^2}\left(1 + \gamma^2\theta_e^2\right) . \]

It falls behind the light faster than an electron on axis, which shifts its resonant wavelength. Section 8 compares this extra term, \(\gamma^2\theta_e^2\), with the undulator’s own term \(K^2/2\) and asks that the relative shift stay below \(\rho_F\).

At work. For LCLS (\(\gamma = 26\,694\), \(K = 3.5\)) the on-axis electron in the undulator has \(1 - \bar\beta_s = (1 + K^2/2)/(2\gamma^2) = 5.00\times10^{-9}\). An angle of 2.4 µrad adds \(\theta_e^2/2 = 2.9\times10^{-12}\), a fraction \(5.8\times10^{-4}\) of it, equal to \(\rho_F\): the alignment tolerance quoted at the bench.

A wiggling electron. In a planar undulator \(\beta_x \approx (K/\gamma)\sin(k_us)\). Since \(\sin^2a = \tfrac12(1 - \cos2a)\),

\[ \beta_x^2 = \frac{K^2}{2\gamma^2}\left(1 - \cos2k_us\right), \]

and the forward speed is

\[ \beta_s \approx 1 - \frac{1 + K^2/2}{2\gamma^2} + \frac{K^2}{4\gamma^2}\cos2k_us . \]

The mean, \(\bar\beta_s = 1 - (1 + K^2/2)/(2\gamma^2)\), is the speed section 2 uses. The electron’s forward speed also oscillates, twice per period, because it moves sideways fastest twice per period, at each crossing of the axis.

At work. For LCLS, \(1/(2\gamma^2) = 7.02\times10^{-10}\) and \(K^2/(4\gamma^2) = 4.30\times10^{-9}\). The wiggle slows the electron six times more than its energy alone does. A numerical average of the exact \(\sqrt{\beta^2 - \beta_x^2}\) over one period gives \(1 - \bar\beta_s = 4.99958\times10^{-9}\), the same as the formula to six figures; the terms dropped are of order \(10^{-17}\). Over one 3 cm period the light gains \(0.03\times5.00\times10^{-9} = 1.50\times10^{-10}\) m: one wavelength of 1.5 Å.

The wobble behind \([JJ]\). The oscillating part of \(\beta_s\) moves the electron back and forth about a point travelling at \(\bar\beta_sc\). Integrating \((K^2/4\gamma^2)\cos2k_us\) over \(s\) gives a displacement \((K^2/8\gamma^2k_u)\sin2k_us\). For LCLS its amplitude is \(4.30\times10^{-9}/(2\times209.4) = 1.03\times10^{-11}\) m, about a fifteenth of the wavelength. In units of the light’s phase it is \(k_rK^2/(8\gamma^2k_u)\); with \(k_r = 2\gamma^2k_u/(1 + K^2/2)\) from the resonance condition this is

\[ \xi = \frac{K^2}{4 + 2K^2} , \]

0.430 rad for LCLS: exactly the argument of the Bessel functions in section 3’s \([JJ] = J_0(\xi) - J_1(\xi) = 0.744\). The electron’s phase relative to the light wobbles by \(\xi\sin2k_us\) while it wiggles, and averaging the coupling over that wobble with the Jacobi-Anger expansion brings in the Bessel functions. In a helical undulator \(\beta_x^2 + \beta_y^2 = K^2/\gamma^2\) is constant, there is no wobble, \([JJ] = 1\), and the mean speed is \(1 - (1 + K^2)/(2\gamma^2)\).

2. The resonance condition: one wavelength per period

The light travels at \(c\); the electron’s mean longitudinal speed is \(\bar\beta_s c\), a little slower, both because \(\beta < 1\) and because part of its speed is sideways. Chapter 20 found

\[ \bar\beta_s \approx 1 - \frac{1 + K^2/2}{2\gamma^2} . \]

(The relativistic-motion continuation before this section derives it, from \(1 - \beta \approx 1/(2\gamma^2)\) for the energy and \(\langle\beta_x^2\rangle/2 = K^2/(4\gamma^2)\) for the wiggle.) The electron takes \(\lambda_u/(\bar\beta_s c)\) to cross one period. In that time the light goes \(\lambda_u/\bar\beta_s\) and gets ahead by

\[ \frac{\lambda_u}{\bar\beta_s} - \lambda_u = \lambda_u\left(\frac{1}{\bar\beta_s} - 1\right) \approx \lambda_u\,(1 - \bar\beta_s) = \lambda_u\,\frac{1+K^2/2}{2\gamma^2} . \]

The middle step is the first-order Taylor series \(1/(1 - \epsilon) \approx 1 + \epsilon\), with \(\epsilon = 1 - \bar\beta_s\) of order \(10^{-9}\).

Now look at the energy exchange. The electron’s transverse velocity \(v_x\) reverses every half period. The light’s field \(\mathcal{E}_x\) at the electron must reverse at the same moments if \(v_x\mathcal{E}_x\), the rate of work, is to keep one sign. That happens if the light slips half a wavelength per half period: one wavelength \(\lambda_r\) per period (Figure 26.4). So

\[ \lambda_r = \frac{\lambda_u}{2\gamma^2}\left(1+\frac{K^2}{2}\right), \]

the same as the on-axis undulator wavelength.

Figure 26.4. The resonance condition. Top, seen from above and not to scale (in LCLS the wiggle is 1.3 µm from side to side in a 3 cm period): the field \(B_y\) points out of the page at \(s = 0, \lambda_u, 2\lambda_u\) and into it half a period later, and the electron (red) curves towards \(+x\) where \(B_y > 0\); its sideways velocity \(v_x\) is largest where it crosses the axis. Because the light slips ahead by one wavelength per period, the field the electron meets goes through one cycle per period, in step with \(v_x\), although the light itself oscillates \(2\times10^8\) times faster. The work on the electron, \(-ev_x\mathcal{E}_x \propto -\sin^2(k_us)\), then never changes sign: here the electron gives energy to the light all the way.

The undulator’s own light has exactly the wavelength with which the electrons can keep exchanging energy. Odd harmonics also satisfy the condition (the light slips \(n\) wavelengths per period, and the work still adds over a period), which is why FELs also emit weakly at \(\lambda_r/3\) and \(\lambda_r/5\).

Worked number: LCLS. At 13.64 GeV, \(\gamma = 26\,693\); with \(\lambda_u = 3.0\) cm and \(K = 3.5\) (Emma, PAC09, table 4),

\[ \lambda_r = \frac{0.030}{2\times26\,693^2}\left(1+\frac{3.5^2}{2}\right) = 1.50\ \text{Å}, \]

8.27 keV photons, the 1.5 Å of the first lasing. \(K = 3.5\) at 3 cm means \(B_0 = 1.25\) T, as the table gives. Over the 60 m to saturation, the light slips 2000 wavelengths, 0.3 µm, ahead of any electron: much less than the bunch, so each part of the bunch lases on its own light with its own neighbours.

Pre-requisite (continued): Maxwell’s equations and the wave equation

Pre-requisite. Chapter 13 derived the wave equation in empty space from Maxwell’s equations, and chapter 20 added polarisation. In an FEL the space is not empty: the beam’s own wiggling current is a source of light. This section derives the one-dimensional wave equation with a current source, \(\partial_s^2\mathcal{E}_x - c^{-2}\partial_t^2\mathcal{E}_x = \mu_0\partial_tJ_x\). It then writes the field as a fast carrier times a slowly varying complex amplitude, and shows that dropping the second derivative of that amplitude turns the wave equation into a first-order one, \(d\tilde{\mathcal{E}}/ds = -\tfrac12\mu_0c\tilde J\): the field grows in proportion to the part of the current that runs against it. An energy check with the Poynting flux confirms the sign. Section 6 uses the result as the third link of its loop. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, §4.3 (light) and §7.1 (the wave equation with sources); Landau and Lifshitz, The Classical Theory of Fields, §46 (the wave equation) and §62 (the retarded potentials, the wave equation with charges and currents); and Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 5, section I.2 (Maxwell’s equation for the FEL with a slowly varying amplitude and phase).

The wave equation with a source. Take two of Maxwell’s equations in vacuum with a current density \(\mathbf{J}\) (A/m\(^2\)):

\[ \nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}, \qquad \nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{J} + \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}{\partial t} . \]

Take the curl of the first and use the second for \(\nabla\times\mathbf{B}\):

\[ \nabla\times(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}) = -\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\times\mathbf{B}) = -\mu_0\frac{\partial\mathbf{J}}{\partial t} - \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}{\partial t^2} . \]

The vector identity \(\nabla\times(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}) - \nabla^2\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) turns this into

\[ \nabla^2\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} - \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}{\partial t^2} = \mu_0\frac{\partial\mathbf{J}}{\partial t} + \nabla(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}) . \]

Now make the one-dimensional approximation of section 6: the beam and the light are wide enough that nothing depends on \(x\) or \(y\), and the light’s field points along \(x\) and depends only on \(s\) and \(t\). Then \(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = \partial \mathcal{E}_x/\partial x = 0\) for this component, \(\nabla^2\) becomes \(\partial^2/\partial s^2\), and the \(x\)-component reads

\[ \frac{\partial^2\mathcal{E}_x}{\partial s^2} - \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\mathcal{E}_x}{\partial t^2} = \mu_0\frac{\partial J_x}{\partial t} . \]

Both sides are in V/m\(^3\). Without the current, any shape \(f(s - ct)\) solves it: light moving forward at \(c\). The current’s time derivative is what radiates. The longitudinal fields of the bunch’s own charge (space charge) are a separate component and are left out of the one-dimensional FEL.

Carrier and envelope. Write the field and the current with the same fast carrier, \(\psi = ks - \omega t\) with \(\omega = ck\), and slowly varying complex amplitudes:

\[ \mathcal{E}_x = \mathrm{Re}\left[\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(s)\,e^{i\psi}\right], \qquad J_x = \mathrm{Re}\left[\tilde J(s)\,e^{i\psi}\right] . \]

(The amplitudes depend on \(s\) only: a long beam in steady state. A short pulse would also need a slow dependence on \(t\), which adds a term \(c^{-1}\partial\tilde{\mathcal{E}}/\partial t\), as in Lee’s equation.) The derivatives are, writing \('\) for \(d/ds\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial^2\mathcal{E}_x}{\partial s^2} &= \mathrm{Re}\left[\left(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}'' + 2ik\tilde{\mathcal{E}}' - k^2\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\right)e^{i\psi}\right], \\ \frac{\partial^2\mathcal{E}_x}{\partial t^2} &= \mathrm{Re}\left[-\omega^2\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\,e^{i\psi}\right], \\ \frac{\partial J_x}{\partial t} &= \mathrm{Re}\left[-i\omega\tilde J\,e^{i\psi}\right] . \end{aligned} \]

Substituting, the terms \(-k^2\tilde{\mathcal{E}} + (\omega^2/c^2)\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\) cancel, because the carrier alone is a free wave. Since the equation holds at all times, the coefficients of \(e^{i\psi}\) must match:

\[ \tilde{\mathcal{E}}'' + 2ik\tilde{\mathcal{E}}' = -i\omega\mu_0\tilde J . \]

The slowly varying envelope. If \(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\) changes over a length \(L\) much longer than a wavelength, then \(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}' \sim \tilde{\mathcal{E}}/L\) and \(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}'' \sim \tilde{\mathcal{E}}/L^2\), and the first term on the left is smaller than the second by about \(1/(2kL)\). Drop it:

\[ \frac{d\tilde{\mathcal{E}}}{ds} = -\frac{\omega\mu_0}{2k}\tilde J = -\frac{\mu_0c}{2}\tilde J . \]

The equation is now first order in \(s\). The field at any point depends only on the current upstream: dropping \(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}''\) has also dropped the wave that would travel backwards (exercise E2), which an FEL does not make.

At work. At LCLS \(k = 2\pi/1.5\ \text{Å} = 4.19\times10^{10}\) m\(^{-1}\) and the field changes over a gain length of 3.3 m, so \(1/(kL_G) = 7\times10^{-12}\). Few approximations in physics are this good.

An energy check. The time-averaged power per unit area of a plane wave of amplitude \(|\tilde{\mathcal{E}}|\) is \(I = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0c|\tilde{\mathcal{E}}|^2\) (W/m\(^2\); chapter 13’s Poynting flux). Its rate of change along \(s\) is

\[ \frac{dI}{ds} = \varepsilon_0c\,\mathrm{Re}\left[\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^*\tilde{\mathcal{E}}'\right] = -\frac{\varepsilon_0\mu_0c^2}{2}\,\mathrm{Re}\left[\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^*\tilde J\right] = -\frac12\mathrm{Re}\left[\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^*\tilde J\right] = -\langle \mathcal{E}_xJ_x\rangle , \]

using \(\varepsilon_0\mu_0c^2 = 1\) and the rule that the time average of a product of two such waves is \(\tfrac12\mathrm{Re}[\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^*\tilde J]\). The light gains power exactly where the current flows against the field, \(\langle \mathcal{E}_xJ_x\rangle < 0\): there the field does negative work on the electrons, which means the electrons do work on the field. This is the energy exchange of section 3 seen from the light’s side.

At work. Section 6 finds that a beam of density \(n_0\) with bunching \(b\) carries a resonant current of amplitude \(|\tilde J| = en_0cK[JJ]|b|/\gamma\). For LCLS, \(n_0 = I/(2\pi\sigma_x^2ec) = 2.2\times10^{22}\) m\(^{-3}\) (\(I = 3\) kA, \(\sigma_x = 21.2\) µm), \(K[JJ]/\gamma = 9.8\times10^{-5}\). With \(|b| = 0.01\), \(|\tilde J| = 1.0\times10^6\) A/m\(^2\) and the field grows at \(\tfrac12\mu_0c|\tilde J| = 0.20\) GV/m per metre. If the bunching were simply held at 0.01, one metre would give 0.20 GV/m, an intensity of \(5.1\times10^{13}\) W/m\(^2\), and 0.14 MW through the beam’s area \(2\pi\sigma_x^2\); the field would grow in proportion to \(s\) and the power as \(s^2\), the coherent emission of a fixed bunching. With \(|b| = 1\) the same metre gives 1.4 GW (in this one-dimensional estimate, which ignores diffraction). With the shot-noise bunching of \(3.5\times10^{-4}\) it gives 7 MV/m. The FEL’s trick is that \(b\) does not stay fixed: the light bunches the beam further, and section 6 closes the loop.

3. The energy exchange and the ponderomotive phase

Let the light be a plane wave polarised along \(x\), \(\mathcal{E}_x = \mathcal{E}_L\cos(k_r s - \omega_r t + \phi_0)\), with \(\mathcal{E}_L\) its amplitude (V/m), \(k_r = 2\pi/\lambda_r\) and \(\omega_r = ck_r\). (Here \(k_r\), \(k_u\) and later \(k_{RF}\) are wavenumbers, kept apart from the quadrupole \(k\) by their subscripts.) The rate of change of the electron’s energy is \(q\mathbf{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\), with \(q = -e\) and \(v_x = c x' = (cK/\gamma)\sin(k_us)\):

\[ \frac{d(\gamma mc^2)}{dt} = -e\,\frac{cK}{\gamma}\sin(k_us)\,\mathcal{E}_L\cos(k_rs - \omega_rt + \phi_0) . \]

Two steps turn this into an equation along the undulator. First, the electron moves forward at very nearly \(c\), so \(ds = c\,dt\) and \(d/dt = c\,d/ds\); dividing both sides by \(mc^2\cdot c\) gives \(d\gamma/ds\). Second, a product of a sine and a cosine is a sum of two sines, \(\sin a\cos b = \tfrac12[\sin(a+b) + \sin(a-b)]\) (F1, sine and cosine), with \(a = k_us\) and \(b = k_rs - \omega_rt + \phi_0\):

\[ \frac{d\gamma}{ds} = -\frac{eKE_L}{2\gamma mc^2}\left[\sin\theta + \sin\left((k_u - k_r)s + \omega_r t - \phi_0\right)\right], \]

with the ponderomotive phase

\[ \theta = (k_r + k_u)s - \omega_r t + \phi_0 . \]

(Ponderomotive, from the Latin for “weight-moving”, is the name for a slow push that comes out of the product of two fast oscillations, here the wiggle and the light. The phase \(\theta\) says where the electron sits within the light wave, measured in radians: \(\theta\) and \(\theta + 2\pi\) are the same place one wavelength further on.)

In these phases \(t\) is the time at which the electron reaches \(s\), so along its path \(dt/ds = 1/(\bar\beta_sc)\) on average. Near resonance the argument of the second sine then changes at the rate \(k_u - k_r + k_r/\bar\beta_s \approx 2k_u\) (the slip of section 2 makes \(k_r(1/\bar\beta_s - 1) = k_u\)): it goes through two full cycles per undulator period and averages to zero. The first varies slowly if \(\theta\) does. Its rate is

\[ \frac{d\theta}{ds} = k_r + k_u - \frac{\omega_r}{\bar\beta_s c} = k_u - k_r\left(\frac{1}{\bar\beta_s} - 1\right) = k_u - k_r\,\frac{1+K^2/2}{2\gamma^2}, \]

using \(\omega_r = ck_r\) and section 2’s \(1/\bar\beta_s - 1 \approx (1 + K^2/2)/(2\gamma^2)\). This vanishes at the resonant energy \(\gamma_r\) of section 2, where \(k_r(1 + K^2/2)/(2\gamma_r^2) = k_u\). For a slightly different energy, let \(\delta = (\gamma - \gamma_r)/\gamma_r\), the relative energy deviation (for these electrons the same as \(\Delta p/p\)). Differentiating with respect to \(\gamma\) and using the resonance condition,

\[ \frac{d}{d\gamma}\left(\frac{d\theta}{ds}\right) = k_r\,\frac{1 + K^2/2}{\gamma^3} = \frac{2k_u}{\gamma_r}\quad\text{at }\gamma = \gamma_r , \]

so a small change \(\Delta\gamma = \gamma_r\delta\) changes the rate by \((2k_u/\gamma_r)\gamma_r\delta = 2k_u\delta\) (F1, small changes). For the energy, \(d\delta/ds = (1/\gamma_r)\,d\gamma/ds\); keep the slow term, and set \(\gamma \approx \gamma_r\) in the factor in front, since \(\delta\) is of order \(10^{-3}\) at most. The pair is

\[ \frac{d\theta}{ds} = 2k_u\delta, \qquad \frac{d\delta}{ds} = -\frac{eK[JJ]\mathcal{E}_L}{2\gamma_r^2mc^2}\sin\theta . \]

Here \(e\mathcal{E}_L\) is a force (N) and \(mc^2\) an energy (J), so the right side is in m\(^{-1}\), as \(d\delta/ds\) must be. In electronvolts, \(e\mathcal{E}_L/mc^2\) is just \(\mathcal{E}_L\) in V/m divided by 511 000 V.

The factor \([JJ] = J_0(\xi) - J_1(\xi)\), with \(\xi = K^2/(4+2K^2)\) and \(J_{0,1}\) Bessel functions, is quoted, not derived: in a planar undulator the longitudinal speed oscillates at twice the undulator frequency, the phase \(\theta\) wobbles, and averaging the coupling over the wobble reduces it (Huang and Kim 2007, section II). For a helical undulator \([JJ] = 1\). For LCLS, \(\xi = 0.430\) and \([JJ] = 0.744\).

These two equations are what chapter 12 found for a particle in an RF bucket: the light wave and the undulator together act as an accelerating wave moving with the electrons at \(\bar\beta_s c\). The same motion of a free charge in a plane wave is worked out exactly in Landau and Lifshitz volume 2, section 47, problem 2.

4. The pendulum and microbunching

Combining the two equations,

\[ \frac{d^2\theta}{ds^2} = -\Omega^2\sin\theta, \qquad \Omega^2 = \frac{eK[JJ]\mathcal{E}_Lk_u}{\gamma_r^2mc^2}, \]

a pendulum with Hamiltonian \(H = k_u\delta^2 - \kappa\cos\theta\), \(\kappa = eK[JJ]\mathcal{E}_L/(2\gamma_r^2mc^2)\) (Tong’s Classical Mechanics, section 10.1, works the pendulum in phase space). The step: differentiate \(d\theta/ds = 2k_u\delta\) once more and substitute \(d\delta/ds = -\kappa\sin\theta\), giving \(\theta'' = -2k_u\kappa\sin\theta\), so \(\Omega^2 = 2k_u\kappa\). To check the Hamiltonian, Hamilton’s equations (F3, from forces to action; chapter 12), with \(\theta\) as the position, \(\delta\) as the momentum and \(s\) as the time, are

\[ \frac{d\theta}{ds} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial\delta} = 2k_u\delta, \qquad \frac{d\delta}{ds} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial\theta} = -\kappa\sin\theta , \]

the pair of section 3. \(H\) stays constant along each electron’s path, so each path is a curve in the \((\theta, \delta)\) plane along which \(k_u\delta^2 - \kappa\cos\theta\) keeps one value. The separatrix passes through \(\theta = \pm\pi\) at \(\delta = 0\), where \(H = -\kappa\cos\pi = \kappa\). Setting \(k_u\delta^2 - \kappa\cos\theta = \kappa\) and using \(1 + \cos\theta = 2\cos^2(\theta/2)\),

\[ \delta_{sep}(\theta) = \pm\sqrt{\frac{2\kappa}{k_u}}\,\cos\frac{\theta}{2} , \]

which at \(\theta = 0\) reaches \(\delta_{max} = \sqrt{2\kappa/k_u}\), the half-height of the FEL bucket. Buckets repeat every \(\lambda_r\) along the bunch, which is every \(2\pi\) in \(\theta\).

At work. For LCLS in a light field of \(\mathcal{E}_L = 10\) GV/m: \(\kappa = 3.5\times0.744\times10^{10}/(2\times26\,693^2\times511\,000\ \mathrm{V}) = 3.58\times10^{-5}\) m\(^{-1}\). The bucket’s half-height is \(\sqrt{2\times3.58\times10^{-5}/209.4} = 5.8\times10^{-4}\), about \(\rho_F\). The height grows as \(\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_L}\), so at the 85 GV/m of saturation (exercise E3) it is \(\sqrt{8.5} = 2.9\) times larger: a few \(\rho_F\), which is section 7’s picture of saturation. The small-swing wavenumber is \(\Omega = \sqrt{2\times209.4\times3.58\times10^{-5}} = 0.122\) m\(^{-1}\), so a quarter of a small-amplitude period takes \((\pi/2)/0.122 = 12.8\) m of undulator.

Electrons enter evenly spread in \(\theta\). Those at \(0 < \theta < \pi\) lose energy, those at \(-\pi < \theta < 0\) gain it; the gainers then move forward in phase and the losers back, and after about a quarter of a pendulum period they crowd together near \(\theta = 0\). That is a density modulation at \(\lambda_r\): microbunching. If the electrons start exactly on resonance, as many gain as lose and the light gets nothing, to first order. If they start slightly above resonance, \(\delta_0 > 0\), they drift towards the decelerating side as they bunch and the light gains. Gain therefore lies on the high-energy side of the spontaneous line, as the 1976 experiment saw.

5. Low gain: Madey’s theorem and the oscillator

When the field changes little in one pass, the energy given to the light can be found by perturbation theory in \(\mathcal{E}_L\) to second order. (Perturbation theory solves a problem with a small push by expanding in powers of the push. To zeroth order the electrons drift through untouched; to first order in \(\mathcal{E}_L\) their energies are modulated, by as much up as down; only at second order, when that modulation has moved them in phase, does the light gain on balance, which is why the gain is proportional to \(\mathcal{E}_L^2\), the light’s own power, and the fractional gain does not depend on how strong the light is. That is the small-signal regime.) The result, Madey’s theorem, is that the gain is proportional to minus the derivative of the spontaneous line shape. In terms of the dimensionless Pierce parameter \(\rho_F\) defined in section 6 (written \(\rho\) in the FEL literature; here with a subscript because \(\rho\) is the bending radius), the small-signal gain of an undulator of \(N_u\) periods is

\[ G(\nu) = (4\pi N_u\rho_F)^3\left(-\frac{d}{d\nu}\right)\left[\frac{\sin(\nu/2)}{\nu/2}\right]^2, \qquad \nu = 4\pi N_u\delta_0 , \]

where \(G\) is the fractional increase of light power per pass and \(\nu\) is the detuning. It is quoted here; integrating the linear equations of section 6 numerically over a short undulator reproduces it. The bracket is the spontaneous line. Its negative derivative (Figure 26.3) is zero on resonance, positive above, negative below, with a maximum of 0.270 at \(\nu = 2.61\). So \(G_{max} = 0.27\,(4\pi N_u\rho_F)^3\). Since \(\rho_F^3\) is proportional to current, the low-gain gain grows as \(IN_u^3\). At fixed \(\lambda_u\), \(\rho_F^3 \propto \gamma^{-3} \propto \lambda_r^{3/2}\), so gain falls at short wavelengths.

The Stanford numbers. Elias, Fairbank, Madey, Schwettman and Smith (1976) used a superconducting helical magnet of 3.2 cm period, 5.2 m long (162 periods), with 2.4 kG on axis, around a 10.2 mm copper tube, and 24 MeV bunches from the Stanford superconducting linac at 1300 MHz with 70 mA peak current. A CO\(_2\) laser sent 10.6 µm light along the axis. They measured 7 per cent gain per pass, zero at the peak of the spontaneous line, net gain on the high-energy side and absorption on the low, as the theorem says. For a helical undulator the resonance is \(\lambda_r = \lambda_u(1+K^2)/(2\gamma^2)\) with \(K = 0.934\times0.24\times3.2 = 0.717\); with \(\gamma = 1 + 24/0.511 = 47.97\) this gives 10.5 µm.

The oscillator. Put the undulator between two mirrors. Light from one bunch makes a round trip and meets the next bunch, so the round-trip time must equal the bunch spacing. Deacon, Elias, Madey, Ramian, Schwettman and Smith (1977) fired one bunch every 84.6 ns, so the mirrors were \(c\times84.6\ \text{ns}/2 = 12.7\) m apart. At 43.5 MeV the oscillator ran above threshold at 3.417 µm, with 0.36 W average output, about 7 kW peak, 1.5 per cent mirror transmission and 500 kW inside the cavity. (The resonance formula gives 3.27 µm at 43.5 MeV; the measured line needs 42.5 MeV, 2.2 per cent less, within the 5 per cent calibration error the authors quote for their spectrometer.) Gain must exceed the round-trip loss, so the power grows pass by pass until saturation. An electron can lose energy only until it falls through the line, which is about \(1/(2N_u)\) wide in \(\delta\), so the efficiency of a simple oscillator is of order \(1/(2N_u)\): 0.3 per cent at Stanford. Oscillators remain the right choice in the infrared, where good mirrors exist and the gain is enough.

Pre-requisite (continued): Ordinary differential equations (linear, constant and varying coefficients)

Pre-requisite. Chapter 2 and its continuations solved linear equations with constant coefficients by trying exponentials, including pairs of coupled first-order equations whose characteristic equation is a quadratic. This section goes one order higher. Three coupled first-order equations in a loop become one third-order equation; trying \(e^{\mu s}\) gives a cubic with three roots; and three starting values fix how much of each root’s solution is present. For the FEL’s equation \(b''' = ia^3b\), with a beam that enters with a seed field but no bunching, each of the three modes gets exactly a third of the field, and the power stays near its input value before the growing mode takes over. Section 6 uses all of it. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §23 (linear equations with constant coefficients solved by exponentials, the characteristic equation and its roots, the general solution as a sum over roots), and Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 5, section I.2 (the three roots of the high-gain FEL and the growing one).

From a loop to one equation. Section 6 ends with three linear first-order equations, each feeding the next and the last feeding the first:

\[ b' = -2ik_u\tilde P, \qquad \tilde P' = i\alpha\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L, \qquad \tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L' = i\beta_Fb , \]

with \(\alpha\) and \(\beta_F\) real positive constants (section 6 gives their values; the subscript keeps \(\beta_F\) apart from the particle’s speed). Differentiate the first and substitute the second, then differentiate again and substitute the third:

\[ b'' = -2ik_u\,(i\alpha\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L) = 2k_u\alpha\,\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L, \qquad b''' = 2k_u\alpha\,(i\beta_Fb) = ia^3\,b, \qquad a^3 = 2k_u\alpha\beta_F . \]

One third-order equation has replaced three first-order ones. Nothing is lost: \(b\), \(b'\) and \(b''\) carry the same information as \(b\), \(\tilde P\) and \(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L\), since \(b' \propto \tilde P\) and \(b'' \propto \tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L\).

Trying an exponential. The coefficients are constant, so try \(b = e^{\mu s}\). Each derivative multiplies by \(\mu\), and the equation becomes the characteristic equation

\[ \mu^3 = ia^3 . \]

Its three roots are \(a\) times the three cube roots of \(i\) (the complex-numbers continuation before section 1):

\[ \mu_1 = a\,e^{i\pi/6}, \qquad \mu_2 = a\,e^{i5\pi/6}, \qquad \mu_3 = -ia . \]

The equation is linear, so any combination of solutions is a solution, and the general solution has one constant per order:

\[ b(s) = c_1e^{\mu_1s} + c_2e^{\mu_2s} + c_3e^{\mu_3s} . \]

Physically these are three modes of the coupled beam and light: one grows, as \(e^{(\sqrt3/2)as}\); one decays; one keeps its size and only turns in phase.

Fixing the constants from the start. Three constants need three starting values, \(b(0)\), \(b'(0)\) and \(b''(0)\):

\[ c_1 + c_2 + c_3 = b(0), \qquad c_1\mu_1 + c_2\mu_2 + c_3\mu_3 = b'(0), \qquad c_1\mu_1^2 + c_2\mu_2^2 + c_3\mu_3^2 = b''(0) . \]

Three facts about the roots make this easy. Multiplying out \((\mu - \mu_1)(\mu - \mu_2)(\mu - \mu_3)\) and comparing with \(\mu^3 - ia^3\), which has no \(\mu^2\) and no \(\mu\) term:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mu_1 + \mu_2 + \mu_3 = 0, \qquad \mu_1\mu_2 + \mu_2\mu_3 + \mu_3\mu_1 = 0, \\ &\mu_1^2 + \mu_2^2 + \mu_3^2 = (\mu_1 + \mu_2 + \mu_3)^2 - 2(\mu_1\mu_2 + \mu_2\mu_3 + \mu_3\mu_1) = 0 . \end{aligned} \]

A seeded amplifier. The beam enters cold and unbunched, \(b(0) = 0\) and \(\tilde P(0) = 0\) so \(b'(0) = 0\), together with a seed field \(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_0\), so \(b''(0) = 2k_u\alpha\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_0\). Try \(c_j = b''(0)/(3\mu_j^2)\). Since \(\mu_j^3 = ia^3\), \(1/\mu_j^2 = \mu_j/(ia^3)\) and \(1/\mu_j = \mu_j^2/(ia^3)\), so

\[ \sum c_j = \frac{b''(0)}{3ia^3}\sum\mu_j = 0, \qquad \sum c_j\mu_j = \frac{b''(0)}{3ia^3}\sum\mu_j^2 = 0, \qquad \sum c_j\mu_j^2 = b''(0) . \]

All three conditions hold. The field is \(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L = b''/(2k_u\alpha) = \sum c_j\mu_j^2e^{\mu_js}/(2k_u\alpha)\), which is

\[ \tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L(s) = \frac{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_0}{3}\left(e^{\mu_1s} + e^{\mu_2s} + e^{\mu_3s}\right) . \]

Each mode starts with a third of the seed. Far down the undulator only the growing one matters, and the power is \(|\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L|^2 \approx (|\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_0|^2/9)\,e^{\sqrt3as}\): a ninth of the input power, growing by \(e\) every \(1/(\sqrt3a) = L_G\).

What happens at the start. Expand each exponential: \(\sum e^{\mu_js} = 3 + s\sum\mu_j + \tfrac{s^2}{2}\sum\mu_j^2 + \tfrac{s^3}{6}\sum\mu_j^3 + \ldots = 3 + \tfrac{s^3}{6}(3ia^3) + \ldots\). So

\[ \frac{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L}{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_0} = 1 + \frac{i(as)^3}{6} + \ldots \]

The first change is in the phase, not the size, and it is slow, as the cube of the distance. The three modes together hold the power near its input value at first. Numerically, \(P/P_0 = |\tfrac13\sum e^{\mu_js}|^2\) is 1.0004 at \(as = 0.5\), 1.025 at 1, 1.28 at 1.5, 2.60 at 2, 19.8 at 3, 121 at 4 and 647 at 5. The growing mode alone, \((1/9)e^{\sqrt3as}\), gives 0.63, 1.49, 3.55, 20.1, 113 and 641 at \(as\) = 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4 and 5: from about \(as = 3\) on it describes the whole. A direct numerical integration of the three first-order equations gives the same powers to ten figures. This slow start is the lethargy region of section 6. In this ideal model (cold beam, exactly on resonance, no diffraction) the power does not fall below its input.

6. High gain: the collective instability

When the light grows appreciably in one pass, the field and the bunching must be solved together. Take a long, cold beam on resonance, uniform with electron density \(n_0\) (m\(^{-3}\)), and a field written \(\mathcal{E}_x = \mathrm{Re}[\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L(s)e^{i(k_rs - \omega_rt)}]\) with a slowly varying complex amplitude \(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L\) (V/m). The phase \(\phi_0\) of section 3 now sits in \(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L\), so here \(\theta = (k_r + k_u)s - \omega_rt\), and the energy equation reads \(d\delta/ds = -\kappa_0\,\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_Le^{i\theta}]\) with \(\kappa_0 = eK[JJ]/(2\gamma_r^2mc^2)\). Define the bunching \(b = \langle e^{-i\theta}\rangle\) and the energy-weighted bunching \(\tilde P = \langle\delta e^{-i\theta}\rangle\), averages over electrons. To first order in the small quantities:

Both \(b\) and \(\tilde P\) are zero for electrons spread evenly in phase (the arrows of Figure 26.1 close up), and both grow as the beam bunches. The three links, each taken to first order in the small quantities \(b\), \(\tilde P\) and \(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L\):

  1. From \(d\theta/ds = 2k_u\delta\): \(\quad db/ds = -2ik_u\tilde P\). (For each electron, \(d(e^{-i\theta})/ds = -i\,(d\theta/ds)\,e^{-i\theta} = -2ik_u\,\delta e^{-i\theta}\) by the chain rule; averaging over the electrons gives the result.)
  2. From the energy equation, writing \(\mathrm{Im}[X] = (X - X^*)/2i\) and dropping the term in \(e^{-2i\theta}\), whose average is itself first order: \(\quad d\tilde P/ds = i\,\dfrac{eK[JJ]}{4\gamma_r^2mc^2}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L\). (The derivative of \(\delta e^{-i\theta}\) has two parts. One is \((d\delta/ds)e^{-i\theta} = -\kappa_0e^{-i\theta}\mathrm{Im}[\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_Le^{i\theta}]\), whose average is \(-\kappa_0\times(-\tfrac{i}{2}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L) = \tfrac{i}{2}\kappa_0\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L\) by the “At work” of the complex-numbers continuation. The other is \(\delta\,(-2ik_u\delta)e^{-i\theta}\), of second order in \(\delta\), which is zero for a cold beam and is dropped.)
  3. From the one-dimensional wave equation \(\partial_s^2\mathcal{E}_x - c^{-2}\partial_t^2\mathcal{E}_x = \mu_0\partial_tJ_x\), dropping \(d^2\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L/ds^2\) (slow envelope): \(\quad d\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L/ds = -\tfrac{1}{2}\mu_0c\tilde J\), where \(\tilde J\) is the complex amplitude of the transverse current density at the resonant frequency. The current is \(-en v_x\), with density \(n = n_0(1 + 2\,\mathrm{Re}[be^{i\theta}])\); its resonant part has complex amplitude \(\tilde J = -ie n_0 cK[JJ]b/\gamma_r\) (in size, the wiggle velocity \(cK/\gamma_r\), times the density wave \(2|b|n_0\), times \(\tfrac12\) from the product of two oscillations; the \(-i\) comes from the \(\sin(k_us)\) of the wiggle). So \(d\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L/ds = i\mu_0c^2en_0K[JJ]\,b/(2\gamma_r)\).

The current in link 3, step by step. Write \(\sin(k_us) = (e^{ik_us} - e^{-ik_us})/2i\) (F5, Euler’s formula) and \(2\,\mathrm{Re}[be^{i\theta}] = be^{i\theta} + b^*e^{-i\theta}\). Their product holds four terms, and the one that turns with the light’s own carrier \(e^{i\psi}\), \(\psi = k_rs - \omega_rt\), is \(be^{i\theta}\times(-e^{-ik_us})/2i = -be^{i\psi}/2i\), since \(\theta - k_us = \psi\). Its partner \(b^*e^{-i\theta}\times e^{ik_us}/2i\) is its complex conjugate, and the two together are \(2\,\mathrm{Re}[-be^{i\psi}/2i] = \mathrm{Re}[ibe^{i\psi}]\). The other two terms turn at \(\theta + k_us\), two undulator wavenumbers away from the carrier, and do not drive the light. So the resonant current is \(J_x = -en_0(cK/\gamma_r)\,\mathrm{Re}[ibe^{i\psi}] = \mathrm{Re}[\tilde Je^{i\psi}]\) with \(\tilde J = -ien_0cKb/\gamma_r\), and the wobble of section 3 adds the factor \([JJ]\).

Now close the loop. Write the three links as \(b' = -2ik_u\tilde P\), \(\tilde P' = i\alpha\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L\) and \(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L' = i\beta_Fb\), with

\[ \alpha = \frac{eK[JJ]}{4\gamma_r^2mc^2}, \qquad \beta_F = \frac{\mu_0c^2en_0K[JJ]}{2\gamma_r} . \]

Differentiating the first equation twice and substituting the other two (the ordinary-differential-equations continuation above does this step), \(b''' = -2ik_u\cdot i\alpha\cdot i\beta_F\,b = 2ik_u\alpha\beta_F\,b\). With \(\mu_0c^2 = 1/\varepsilon_0\),

\[ 2k_u\alpha\beta_F = \frac{2k_u\,e^2n_0K^2[JJ]^2}{8\,\varepsilon_0\gamma_r^3mc^2} = \frac{k_u\,e^2n_0K^2[JJ]^2}{4\,\varepsilon_0\gamma_r^3mc^2} . \]

Naming this \((2k_u\rho_F)^3 = 8k_u^3\rho_F^3\) defines the Pierce parameter:

\[ \frac{d^3b}{ds^3} = i\,(2k_u\rho_F)^3\,b, \qquad \rho_F^3 = \frac{e^2n_0K^2[JJ]^2}{32\,\varepsilon_0mc^2\gamma_r^3k_u^2} . \]

Check the units: \(e^2n_0/(\varepsilon_0mc^2)\) is in C\(^2\) m\(^{-3}\)/(F m\(^{-1}\) J) = m\(^{-2}\), and dividing by \(k_u^2\) (m\(^{-2}\)) leaves a pure number, as \(\rho_F\) must be. (The combination \(e^2n_0/(\varepsilon_0m)\) is the square of the plasma frequency of the beam’s electrons, chapter 7; \(\rho_F\) measures how strongly the beam’s own charge and current can act back on it within one undulator period.)

For a round Gaussian beam of peak current \(I\) and rms size \(\sigma_x\), the density on axis is \(n_0 = I/(2\pi\sigma_x^2ec)\): the current \(I\) carries \(I/e\) electrons a second, which at speed \(c\) fill \(I/(ec)\) per metre, spread over the area \(2\pi\sigma_x^2\) that a round Gaussian of peak density \(n_0\) effectively covers. Substituting it brings in the combination \(e/(4\pi\varepsilon_0mc^3)\), whose inverse is a current built from the electron’s constants alone, the Alfvén current \(I_A = 4\pi\varepsilon_0mc^3/e = 17.045\) kA. (Physically it is near the current at which a beam’s own magnetic field would bend its electrons round within the beam’s own size, for \(\gamma\beta = 1\).) So \(e^2n_0/\varepsilon_0 = e\,I/(2\pi\sigma_x^2c\,\varepsilon_0) = 2mc^2\,I/(I_A\sigma_x^2)\), and

\[ \rho_F = \left[\frac{1}{16}\,\frac{I}{I_A}\,\frac{K^2[JJ]^2}{\gamma_r^3\sigma_x^2k_u^2}\right]^{1/3}, \]

Huang and Kim’s equation (5), and the dimensionless Pierce parameter of Bonifacio, Pellegrini and Narducci (1984). (The name recalls J. R. Pierce’s theory of the travelling-wave tube, which leads to the same cubic.)

Solutions go as \(e^{\mu s}\) with \(\mu^3 = i(2k_u\rho_F)^3\). The three cube roots of \(i\) are \(e^{i\pi/6}\), \(e^{i5\pi/6}\) and \(e^{-i\pi/2}\). The first has real part \(\cos(\pi/6) = \sqrt3/2\), so the field amplitude grows as \(e^{(\sqrt3/2)(2k_u\rho_F)s} = e^{\sqrt3k_u\rho_Fs}\) and the power, the square of the amplitude, as \(e^{2\sqrt3k_u\rho_Fs} = e^{s/L_G}\) with

\[ L_G = \frac{1}{2\sqrt3\,k_u\rho_F} = \frac{\lambda_u}{4\pi\sqrt{3}\,\rho_F} . \]

This is the one-dimensional power gain length. A seed field entering with no bunching excites all three roots equally, so the growing mode carries a third of the amplitude, a ninth of the power: the output first lingers, then rises exponentially (the “lethargy” region, Figure 26.6). The physics is a loop with three links, field to energy modulation, energy modulation to bunching, bunching to field, each a derivative; three derivatives in a loop give the cube root of \(i\), and one root grows.

The one-dimensional model assumes a beam wide enough that diffraction can be ignored, no energy spread and no emittance. Real gain lengths are longer; Ming Xie’s fitted formula, used in the LCLS laser-heater paper, corrects \(L_G\) for diffraction, emittance and energy spread.

Worked number: LCLS. Take \(I = 3.0\) kA, \(\gamma = 26\,693\), \(K = 3.5\), \(\lambda_u = 3\) cm, a slice normalised emittance of 0.4 µm and the mean undulator \(\beta_x = 30\) m (Emma, PAC09). The geometric emittance is \(\varepsilon = \varepsilon_n/\gamma\) (for \(\beta \approx 1\)), and the rms size is \(\sqrt{\varepsilon\beta_x}\) (chapter 16), so \(\sigma_x = \sqrt{\varepsilon_n\beta_x/\gamma} = 21\) µm and

\[ \rho_F = \left[\frac{1}{16}\,\frac{3000}{17\,045}\,\frac{3.5^2\times0.744^2}{26\,693^3\times(21.2\times10^{-6})^2\times209.4^2}\right]^{1/3} = 5.8\times10^{-4}, \]

\(L_G = 0.03/(21.77\times5.84\times10^{-4}) = 2.36\) m. LCLS measured 3.3 m: the three-dimensional effects lengthen it by 40 per cent.

7. SASE: from noise to saturation

Without a seed, the input is the beam’s own shot noise: the random positions of electrons give \(|b| \sim 1/\sqrt{N_c}\), with \(N_c\) the number of electrons in a coherence length, and the spontaneous undulator light of roughly the first two gain lengths acts as the seed (Huang and Kim). This is self-amplified spontaneous emission, proposed by A. M. Kondratenko and E. L. Saldin (Particle Accelerators, 1980) and given its high-gain theory by Bonifacio, Pellegrini and Narducci (Optics Communications, 1984).

Saturation. The growth stops when the electrons, bunched and losing energy, fall out of resonance by about the bucket’s height, which at that point is of order \(\rho_F\). Each electron has then given up a fraction about \(\rho_F\) of its energy:

\[ P_{sat} \approx \rho_F P_b, \qquad P_b = \frac{E}{e}\,I . \]

(\(P_b\) is the beam power: \(I/e\) electrons arrive each second, each carrying the energy \(E\).)

Huang and Kim find the saturation distance to be \(L_G\ln\) of a number proportional to \(N_c\), typically 18 to 20 gain lengths. Since \(4\pi\sqrt3 = 21.8\), this is about \(\lambda_u/\rho_F\), the length of \(1/\rho_F\) periods. The relative bandwidth at saturation is about \(\rho_F\).

For LCLS, \(P_b = 13.64\ \text{GeV}\times3\ \text{kA} = 41\) TW and \(\rho_FP_b = 24\) GW. The measured pulse gave 1.1 mJ in an estimated 75 fs, about 15 GW. Saturation came at about 60 m, \(60/3.3 = 18\) gain lengths; \(\lambda_u/\rho_F = 51\) m. The electrons lost 4.6 MeV each, \(3.4\times10^{-4}\) of their energy, of order \(\rho_F\).

Spikes. Each part of the bunch amplifies its own noise, and information travels only by slippage: in one gain length the light moves ahead by about the cooperation length \(L_c = \lambda_r/(4\pi\rho_F)\) (Bonifacio and colleagues, 1994). Regions further apart than about \(2\pi L_c\) grow independently, so a SASE pulse is a train of spikes with random amplitudes, and its spectrum is a set of spikes of relative width about \(\lambda_r/(c\,\tau_L)\) for a pulse of duration \(\tau_L\), under an envelope of width about \(\rho_F\). For LCLS, \(L_c = 20\) nm; a 75 fs pulse (22 µm) holds of order a hundred spikes. The pulse energy fluctuates from shot to shot by about \(1/\sqrt{M}\), \(M\) the number of spikes; near saturation the fluctuations shrink.

8. What the beam must be

Energy spread. An electron off resonance by \(\delta\) drifts in phase by \(2k_u\delta\) per metre, so over one gain length \(L_G = 1/(2\sqrt3k_u\rho_F)\) by \(2k_u\delta L_G = \delta/(\sqrt3\rho_F)\) radians. If that is more than about a radian, electrons of different energies drift apart in phase faster than the light can bunch them. To keep in step over a gain length,

\[ \sigma_\delta \lesssim \rho_F . \]

For LCLS this is \(6\times10^{-4}\) of 13.64 GeV, 8 MeV. The slice energy spread at the undulator was about 1.8 MeV (section 10).

Emittance. Two conditions, each approximate. First, angle: an electron moving at angle \(\theta_e\) to the axis is slower along it, which shifts its resonant wavelength by \(\gamma^2\theta_e^2/(1+K^2/2)\) relative to the line. (From the relativistic-motion continuation: \(1 - \beta_s\) grows from \((1 + K^2/2)/(2\gamma^2)\) to \((1 + K^2/2 + \gamma^2\theta_e^2)/(2\gamma^2)\), and the slip per period, which is the resonant wavelength, grows in the same ratio.) A beam of emittance \(\varepsilon\) where the betatron function is \(\beta_x\) has rms angle \(\sqrt{\varepsilon/\beta_x}\) (chapter 16, at a waist), so take \(\theta_e^2 = \varepsilon/\beta_x\) and require

\[ \frac{\gamma^2}{1 + K^2/2}\,\frac{\varepsilon}{\beta_x} < \rho_F . \]

The resonance condition gives \(\gamma^2/(1 + K^2/2) = \lambda_u/(2\lambda_r)\), so \(\varepsilon < 2\rho_F\lambda_r\beta_x/\lambda_u\); then \(\rho_F = \lambda_u/(4\pi\sqrt3L_G)\) from section 6 gives

\[ \varepsilon < \frac{2\rho_F\lambda_r\beta_x}{\lambda_u} = \frac{\lambda_r}{4\pi}\cdot\frac{2}{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{\beta_x}{L_G} . \]

(Huang and Kim’s equation (73) has the same form, rounding the factor to 1.) Second, size: the electron beam must lie within the light it amplifies. A light beam of rms size \(\sigma_r\) cannot be parallel: diffraction (F5; chapter 20’s diffraction limit) gives it an rms angle of at least \(\lambda_r/(4\pi\sigma_r)\), so it doubles its area over a distance of about \(\sigma_r\) divided by that angle, \(4\pi\sigma_r^2/\lambda_r\), the Rayleigh length. For the light to stay as narrow as the electrons over a gain length, \(4\pi\sigma_r^2/\lambda_r \approx L_G\) with \(\sigma_r \approx \sigma_x\), so we want \(\varepsilon\beta_x \lesssim \lambda_rL_G/(4\pi)\). Multiplying the two, \(\beta_x\) and \(L_G\) cancel:

\[ \varepsilon \lesssim \frac{\lambda_r}{4\pi}, \qquad \varepsilon_n \lesssim \frac{\gamma\lambda_r}{4\pi}, \]

the diffraction limit of chapter 20. The rule is soft: the FEL still works a little above it, more slowly. For LCLS at 1.5 Å, \(\lambda_r/4\pi = 12\) pm and \(\gamma\lambda_r/4\pi = 0.32\) µm; the slice emittance of 0.4 µm sits at the rule. At the European XFEL’s 17.5 GeV, 1 Å needs about 0.27 µm.

Current. \(\rho_F \propto I^{1/3}\). At the LCLS beam size and energy, the 1D gain length would be 7.3 m at 100 A, 3.4 m at 1 kA and 2.4 m at 3 kA, and the saturation length \(\lambda_u/\rho_F\) 160 m, 74 m and 51 m. A photoinjector gives tens of amperes (33 A at LCLS), so the bunch must be shortened by about a hundred.

Pre-requisite (continued): Linear systems and transfer matrices

Pre-requisite. Chapter 8 built transfer matrices, including one that carries the momentum deviation \(\delta\); chapter 12 the matrix of one period; and chapter 18 normalised coordinates, in which every stretch of beam line is a rotation by its phase advance. This section adds three things. The longitudinal map of a bend, with its element \(R_{56}\) and the two sign conventions in use. What a linear map does to a bunch whose energy is correlated with position: it shortens the bunch and raises the uncorrelated (slice) energy spread by the same factor. And the general element \(M_{12} = \sqrt{\beta_1\beta_2}\sin\Delta\psi\) from one point of a line to another. Section 9 uses the first two for the chicanes; “At the bench” uses the third for the deflecting cavity. Taught from Chao and Tigner, Handbook, §4.4.1 (Emma’s bunch compression: the chirp, the compression factor and the \(R_{56}\) of a chicane), and Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, section II (the transfer matrix between two points written with the betatron functions).

The longitudinal map. Follow each electron by two numbers: its position \(z\) in the bunch (m, positive towards the head, as in section 9) and its relative energy deviation \(\delta\). A static magnet does no work, so \(\delta\) comes out unchanged. But the path length depends on energy: in a chicane a higher-energy electron is bent less, takes the shorter path, and moves towards the head. To first order the extra path is proportional to \(\delta\), and calling the constant \(R_{56}\) (m), the map is

\[ \begin{pmatrix} z_f \\ \delta_f \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -R_{56} \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} z_i \\ \delta_i \end{pmatrix} . \]

With \(z\) positive forwards, a longer path moves an electron backwards, hence the minus sign; for a chicane the path is shorter at higher energy, so \(R_{56} < 0\) and \(z_f = z_i + |R_{56}|\delta\). Emma in the Handbook, and chapter 25, write \(z_f = z_i + R_{56}\delta\) and give the chicane a positive \(R_{56}\). The physics is the same; only the sign of \(R_{56}\) changes. The safe check, whatever the book: does the higher-energy electron end up nearer the head? The matrix has determinant 1, so it keeps the area of the bunch in the \((z, \delta)\) plane, as Liouville’s theorem requires of motion in static magnetic fields.

A chirped bunch through it. Let the energy before the chicane have a part correlated with position, the chirp \(\chi\) (m\(^{-1}\)), and an uncorrelated part \(\delta_u\) independent of position: \(\delta_i = \chi z_i + \delta_u\). Then

\[ z_f = z_i - R_{56}(\chi z_i + \delta_u) = (1 - R_{56}\chi)\,z_i - R_{56}\,\delta_u . \]

The two terms are independent, so their variances add:

\[ \sigma_{z,f}^2 = (1 - R_{56}\chi)^2\sigma_{z,i}^2 + R_{56}^2\sigma_{\delta_u}^2 . \]

When the second term is small, the bunch is shorter by \(C = 1/|1 - R_{56}\chi|\). The second term sets the floor: at full compression, \(R_{56}\chi = 1\), the length is \(|R_{56}|\sigma_{\delta_u}\), set by the uncorrelated spread alone.

The slice spread grows by the same factor. After the chicane, what energy spread does a thin slice of the bunch at fixed \(z_f\) hold? Invert the map for \(z_i\), \(z_i = (z_f + R_{56}\delta_u)/(1 - R_{56}\chi)\), and substitute into \(\delta = \chi z_i + \delta_u\):

\[ \delta = \frac{\chi}{1 - R_{56}\chi}\,z_f + \delta_u\left(1 + \frac{R_{56}\chi}{1 - R_{56}\chi}\right) = \frac{\chi}{1 - R_{56}\chi}\,z_f + \frac{\delta_u}{1 - R_{56}\chi} . \]

The new chirp is larger by \(1/(1 - R_{56}\chi)\), and so is the uncorrelated part: the slice energy spread grows by \(C\). No electron’s energy changed. What changed is how many electrons share each slice: electrons that sat \(C\) times further apart, with slightly different energies, now sit in the same slice. Length down by \(C\), slice spread up by \(C\): the area \(\sigma_z\sigma_{\delta_u}\) is the same, Liouville again.

At work. LCLS BC1 (section 9): the bunch arrives with \(\sigma_{z,i} = 0.65\) mm (the injector deflector’s measurement at 250 pC), a chirp \(\chi = -18.2\) m\(^{-1}\), an uncorrelated spread of 3 keV at 250 MeV (\(\sigma_{\delta_u} = 1.2\times10^{-5}\)), and meets \(R_{56} = -45.5\) mm. Then \(R_{56}\chi = 0.829\), \(C = 5.86\), and \(\sigma_{z,f} = 111\) µm. The floor term \(|R_{56}|\sigma_{\delta_u} = 0.55\) µm is negligible here. The slice spread rises from 3 keV to \(3\times5.86 = 17.6\) keV. A simulation of 200 000 electrons through the same map gives \(\sigma_{z,f} = 111.8\) µm, a new chirp of \(-105.9\) m\(^{-1}\) (\(= \chi C\)), the slice spread up by 5.82 (the simulation used \(R_{56}\chi = 0.828\)), and the same \((z, \delta)\) area to eight figures.

From one point to another: \(M_{12}\). In chapter 18’s normalised coordinates, \(X = x/\sqrt{\beta}\) and \(P = (\alpha x + \beta x')/\sqrt{\beta}\), the stretch of line from point 1 to point 2, whatever it contains, is a rotation by the phase advance \(\Delta\psi\) between them:

\[ X_2 = X_1\cos\Delta\psi + P_1\sin\Delta\psi . \]

Give a particle at point 1 an angle \(x_1'\) but no offset. Then \(X_1 = 0\) and \(P_1 = \sqrt{\beta_1}\,x_1'\), so \(X_2 = \sqrt{\beta_1}\sin\Delta\psi\,x_1'\) and, multiplying by \(\sqrt{\beta_2}\),

\[ x_2 = \sqrt{\beta_1\beta_2}\,\sin\Delta\psi\;x_1', \qquad M_{12} = \sqrt{\beta_1\beta_2}\,\sin\Delta\psi . \]

Lee writes the whole matrix this way. An angle is turned into an offset most effectively when the phase advance is 90° and both beta functions are large. Check with a plain drift of length \(L\) starting at a waist \(\beta^*\): there \(\beta_2 = \beta^* + L^2/\beta^*\) and \(\tan\Delta\psi = L/\beta^*\), and \(\sqrt{\beta^*\beta_2}\sin\Delta\psi = \sqrt{\beta^{*2} + L^2}\cdot L/\sqrt{\beta^{*2} + L^2} = L\), the drift’s familiar \(M_{12}\).

At work. A deflecting cavity gives each electron an angle proportional to its arrival time; \(M_{12}\) carries that angle to a screen as an offset, while the beam’s own size there is \(\sqrt{\varepsilon\beta_2}\). Their ratio is the resolution formula of “At the bench”. A kick of 1 µrad where \(\beta_1 = 50\) m, seen 90° later where \(\beta_2 = 10\) m, moves the beam by \(\sqrt{500}\times10^{-6} = 22\) µm.

9. Bunch compression

The chicane. Four dipoles bend the beam out and back. Higher-energy electrons bend less and take a shorter path. Let \(z\) be an electron’s position in the bunch, positive towards the head, and let the extra path length be \(R_{56}\delta\) (the transport-matrix element, in m). Then after the chicane

\[ z_f = z_i - R_{56}\delta . \]

In the small-angle limit, with bend angle \(\theta_B\) per dipole, dipole length \(L_B\) and drift \(\Delta L\) between the first and second (and third and fourth) dipoles, the extra path is \(\theta_B^2\Delta L\) in the two slanted drifts and \(4\times L_B\theta_B^2/6\) in the arcs. Since \(\theta_B \propto 1/(1+\delta)\),

\[ R_{56} \approx -2\theta_B^2\left(\Delta L + \tfrac{2}{3}L_B\right) . \]

LCLS BC1 (Akre and colleagues 2008, table IV): \(\theta_B = 4.97°\), \(L_B = 0.204\) m, \(\Delta L = 2.43\) m give \(|R_{56}| = 38.6\) mm; the table says 39 mm. (Exercise 6 at the end derives the two path lengths: a straight line tilted at a small angle \(\theta_B\) is longer than its projection by the factor \(1/\cos\theta_B \approx 1 + \theta_B^2/2\), and an arc by \(\theta_B/\sin\theta_B \approx 1 + \theta_B^2/6\). The minus sign comes from differentiating: a higher energy means a smaller \(\theta_B\) and so a shorter path.)

Top, not to scale: the four dipoles of the chicane; an electron with less energy (blue) bends more and takes the longer path, so it falls back; one with more energy (red) takes the shorter path and moves forward. Bottom, from the map \(z_f = z_i - R_{56}\delta\) with the chapter’s numbers: the bunch before BC1 (grey; \(\pm2\sigma_z = \pm1.3\) mm, chirp \(\chi = -18.2\) m\(^{-1}\), so the head at \(z > 0\) has less energy) and after (\(R_{56} = -45.5\) mm, green): the tail has caught up and the bunch is \(1/(1 - 0.828) = 5.8\) times shorter. No electron’s energy changed; each moved only left or right in this picture. The band’s thickness, the uncorrelated spread, is exaggerated 100 times to be visible; at a fixed \(z\) the green band is thicker in \(\delta\) by the same factor 5.8, the growth of the slice spread, and the two bands have the same area.

The chirp. Write \(\delta = \delta_u + \chi z_i\): an uncorrelated part \(\delta_u\) and a linear correlation, the chirp \(\chi\) (m\(^{-1}\)). Then

\[ z_f = (1 - R_{56}\chi)z_i - R_{56}\delta_u, \qquad C = \frac{\sigma_{z,i}}{\sigma_{z,f}} \approx \frac{1}{|1 - R_{56}\chi|} \]

as long as \(|R_{56}|\sigma_{\delta_u}\) is small compared with the final length. With \(R_{56} < 0\), compression needs \(\chi < 0\): the head must have less energy than the tail, so the tail catches up. The bunch’s longitudinal phase-space area is conserved, so the uncorrelated energy spread grows by \(C\).

Making the chirp off crest. An RF section of voltage \(\hat V\) gives \(\Delta W = e\hat V\sin\phi\), with \(\phi\) larger for later arrival. A particle at \(z\) arrives earlier by \(z/c\), so \(\phi = \phi_s - k_{RF}z\) with \(k_{RF} = \omega_{RF}/c\). After the section, at energy \(E_f\),

\[ \chi = \frac{d\delta}{dz} = -\frac{e\hat Vk_{RF}\cos\phi_s}{E_f} . \]

\(\chi < 0\) needs \(\cos\phi_s > 0\): \(\phi_s < 90°\), before the crest, on the rising side. In the linac convention \(\Delta W = e\hat V\cos\phi\) this is \(-90° < \phi < 0\), which is how LCLS quotes its phases.

Worked number: LCLS BC1 at 250 pC. From Emma’s table 3: L1S at \(-20°\) and an X-band (11.424 GHz) section L1X of 20 MV at \(-160°\); the beam goes from 135 MeV to 250 MeV at BC1 (Bane and colleagues 2009, table II). L1X takes off \(20\cos20° = 18.8\) MeV, so L1S raises the beam from 135 to 269 MeV, and \(e\hat V = 134/\cos20° = 142.6\) MV at 2.856 GHz (\(k_{RF} = 59.86\) m\(^{-1}\)). In the sine convention their phases are \(70°\) and \(-70°\). Each adds a negative chirp:

\[ \chi = -\frac{142.6\times59.86\times\cos70° + 20\times239.4\times\cos70°}{250}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}} = -18.2\ \mathrm{m^{-1}} . \]

With \(R_{56} = -45.5\) mm, \(R_{56}\chi = 0.829\) and \(C = 5.9\). The machine went from 33 A to 250 A, a factor 7.6, which needs \(R_{56}\chi = 0.868\), a chirp 4.7 per cent stronger. The linear model is close, and it shows why compressors need feedback: near full compression (\(R_{56}\chi \to 1\)) a small change of phase changes \(C\) a lot. The X-band section’s other job is to cancel the curvature of the S-band sine, which would otherwise compress the bunch into a spike with a tail. BC2 at 4.3 GeV (\(R_{56} = -24.7\) mm) compresses another 12 times to 3 kA, and L3 runs on crest, where the bunch’s own longitudinal wakefield in the long linac takes out the remaining chirp (chapter 22).

10. Coherent synchrotron radiation, the microbunching instability and the laser heater

CSR. In a chicane’s last dipole the bunch is short, and it radiates coherently at wavelengths longer than \(\sigma_z\), as \(N^2\) (section 1). The radiation from the tail catches up with the head across the chord of the arc and changes its energy. For a Gaussian bunch in steady state on an arc of bending radius \(\rho\), the mean energy loss per unit length and the rms spread it causes are

\[ \left\langle\frac{dW}{ds}\right\rangle \approx -0.35\,\frac{Nr_emc^2}{\rho^{2/3}\sigma_z^{4/3}}, \qquad \sigma_{dW/ds} \approx 0.25\,\frac{Nr_emc^2}{\rho^{2/3}\sigma_z^{4/3}} . \]

The steady-state CSR wake is quoted without derivation (it is in the CSR papers of Saldin, Schneidmiller and Yurkov and their colleagues); the two coefficients come from averaging that wake over a Gaussian, done numerically for this chapter and matching the Handbook of Accelerator Physics and Engineering, whose table of Gaussian bunch wakes gives \(-0.758\) and \(0.532\) in units that convert to 0.350 and 0.246 here. (Bane and colleagues write the BC1 loss with a coefficient of 1.8 and a different convention; their figure is about five times these, and the difference was not traced.) For LCLS BC1 at 250 pC (\(N = 1.56\times10^9\), \(\rho = 2.1\) m, a last dipole of 0.2 m; Bane and colleagues 2009) and \(\sigma_z = 100\) µm, the mean loss is 21 keV and the spread 15 keV; at 10 µm, near full compression, 0.45 MeV and 0.31 MeV. Because it happens where the dispersion is not zero, an energy change becomes a horizontal kick and the emittance grows. Bane and colleagues measured this in both LCLS compressors and matched it with the codes ELEGANT, CSRTRACK and IMPACT-Z.

The microbunching instability. A small density ripple at, say, 20 µm drives an energy ripple through the beam’s own longitudinal space charge in the linac. The chicane turns the energy ripple into a larger density ripple, compressed in wavelength, and the next linac and chicane repeat it. Huang, Borland and colleagues (2004) found that for LCLS the gain could be large enough to amplify modulations made in the RF gun or even shot noise, breaking up the slice energy spread.

The cure is the uncorrelated energy spread. After a chicane a ripple \(e^{ikz}\) has been sheared by \(-R_{56}\delta_u\); averaging \(e^{-ikR_{56}\delta_u}\) over a Gaussian of width \(\sigma_{\delta}\) gives a factor

\[ e^{-(kR_{56}\sigma_\delta)^2/2} . \]

With \(R_{56} = 45.5\) mm at 250 MeV, the natural 3 keV spread leaves a 20 µm ripple at 0.99 of itself; 20 keV reduces it to 0.52, and a 5 µm ripple to \(3\times10^{-5}\). The laser heater adds that spread on purpose, early and cleanly: a 758 nm laser co-propagating with the beam in a short undulator in the middle of a small chicane at 135 MeV imposes an energy modulation at 758 nm, and the second half of the chicane smears it into a spread. LCLS runs it at about 6 µJ for 20 keV rms; after the total compression of 90 this is 1.8 MeV, \(1.3\times10^{-4}\) at 13.64 GeV, safely below \(\rho_F\). With the heater off, the FEL power in the exponential regime fell by an order of magnitude and the gain length grew by about 1 m (Huang and colleagues 2010).

11. Seeding and short pulses

SASE’s spikes and its wavelength jitter are a price; seeding removes them by starting the FEL from a clean field or a clean bunching.

High-gain harmonic generation (HGHG). A seed laser of wavenumber \(k_s\) in a short modulator undulator imposes an energy modulation of amplitude \(\mathcal{M}\sigma_E\), \(\mathcal{M}\) times the uncorrelated rms energy spread \(\sigma_E = \sigma_\delta E\). A chicane with \(R_{56}\) follows. Write \(\mathcal{D} = k_s|R_{56}|\sigma_\delta\). After the chicane, the bunching at harmonic \(n_h\) is \(b_{n_h} = \langle e^{-in_hk_sz_f}\rangle\); with \(z_f = z_i - R_{56}(\delta_u + \mathcal{M}\sigma_\delta\sin k_sz_i)\), the Jacobi-Anger expansion \(e^{ia\sin x} = \sum_mJ_m(a)e^{imx}\) picks out one Bessel term, and the Gaussian average over \(\delta_u\) gives

\[ |b_{n_h}| = |J_{n_h}(n_h\mathcal{M}\mathcal{D})|\,e^{-n_h^2\mathcal{D}^2/2} . \]

The exponential kills high harmonics unless \(\mathcal{D} \lesssim 1/n_h\), and then the Bessel function needs \(\mathcal{M} \gtrsim n_h\). Optimising \(\mathcal{D}\): for \(n_h = 5\), \(\mathcal{M} = 5\) gives \(|b| = 0.18\); for \(n_h = 10\), \(\mathcal{M} = 5\) gives 0.03 and \(\mathcal{M} = 10\) gives 0.16. But in the radiator the modulation acts as energy spread: a sinusoid of amplitude \(\mathcal{M}\sigma_\delta\) adds an rms of \(\mathcal{M}\sigma_\delta/\sqrt2\), so the spread becomes \(\sqrt{1+\mathcal{M}^2/2}\,\sigma_\delta\), about \(0.7\mathcal{M}\) times the original, and it must stay below \(\rho_F\). So one HGHG step reaches a modest harmonic, about the tenth (Handbook, section 3.1.11). FERMI at Trieste saw its first seeded light at 65 nm on 13 December 2010, and at 43 nm soon after (Penco, FEL 2011) and was tuned over 65 to 20 nm (Allaria and colleagues 2012). Echo-enabled harmonic generation (EEHG), proposed by G. Stupakov in 2009, uses two modulators and two chicanes: the first chicane, very strong, shreds phase space into thin energy bands, and the second turns them into bunching at a high harmonic, with much less loss of bunching as the harmonic grows.

Self-seeding. For hard X-rays there is no seed laser. Colleagues at DESY proposed letting the first half of the undulator lase by SASE, passing the light through a diamond crystal whose Bragg reflection leaves a narrow line in the forward beam, delaying the electrons in a chicane to wash out their bunching, and amplifying that line in the second half. LCLS demonstrated it in 2012: 0.4 to 0.5 eV bandwidth at 8 to 9 keV, 40 to 50 times narrower than SASE (Amann and colleagues).

Attosecond pulses. A pulse shorter than a cooperation length holds a single spike. In 2019 a team at LCLS reported isolated soft X-ray attosecond pulses with peak power above 100 GW (Duris and colleagues), by making only a very short slice of the bunch able to lase.

Pre-requisite (continued): Driven oscillator, resonance and quality factor

Pre-requisite. Chapter 13 showed that losses add as \(1/Q\), that holding a fixed voltage off resonance costs \(1 + y^2\) times the forward power with \(y = 2Q_L\Delta f/f\), and, in its section 7, how a beam on crest loads a cavity and sets the matched coupling. This section joins those pieces into one formula for the forward power with both beam loading and detuning, derived from the cavity’s equivalent circuit. Then it asks the question an energy recovery linac poses: with no net beam current, which loaded \(Q\) needs the least power against a given microphonic detuning? The answer is \(Q_L = f/(2\Delta f)\), where the power is \(\hat V^2\Delta f/((R/Q)f)\). Section 12 uses both results. Taught from Padamsee, Knobloch and Hays, RF Superconductivity for Accelerators, §17.2 to §17.4 (the beam-loaded equivalent circuit, optimal coupling, and the generator power when the current or the frequency changes) and §19.3 (microphonics); the adding of phasors is from chapter 4.

The circuit. Near one mode a cavity behaves as a parallel resonant circuit (chapter 13, section 6). Describe each way out for energy by a conductance (siemens), in parallel: \(G_c\) for the walls, \(G_{ext}\) for the coupler and the line behind it. Their sum \(G_L = G_c + G_{ext}\) goes with the loaded \(Q_L\). Near resonance the circuit’s admittance is

\[ Y = G_L(1 + iy), \qquad y = 2Q_L\frac{\Delta f}{f} , \]

with \(\Delta f\) the difference between the cavity’s frequency and the drive’s: chapter 13’s detuning parameter, written as an admittance.

Conductance and \(R/Q\). The course’s (linac) convention defines \(R/Q\) by the stored energy, \(U = \hat V^2/((R/Q)\omega)\), with \(\omega = 2\pi f\). A conductance \(G\) at voltage amplitude \(\hat V\) takes \(\tfrac12G\hat V^2\), so the coupler’s quality factor is

\[ Q_{ext} = \frac{\omega U}{\tfrac12G_{ext}\hat V^2} = \frac{2}{(R/Q)\,G_{ext}}, \qquad G_{ext} = \frac{2}{(R/Q)\,Q_{ext}} , \]

and in the same way \(G_c = 2/((R/Q)Q_0)\) and \(G_L = 2/((R/Q)Q_L)\). In a superconducting cavity \(Q_0 \approx 10^{10}\) while \(Q_{ext}\) is \(10^6\) to \(10^8\), so \(G_c \ll G_{ext}\) and \(G_L \approx G_{ext}\), \(Q_L \approx Q_{ext}\). (The factor 2 here is the factor between the linac and circuit conventions, as in chapter 13.)

The currents. Model the generator, behind its circulator, as a current source \(\tilde i_g\) feeding the cavity in parallel with \(G_{ext}\). The beam is another current: bunches short compared with an RF period, of average current \(I\), carry an RF current of amplitude \(2I\) (chapter 13). A beam on crest takes energy from the field, so its RF current is in phase with the voltage, like a resistor’s. At the cavity, the generator’s current must supply what the cavity’s admittance draws and what the beam draws:

\[ \tilde i_g = \tilde VG_L(1 + iy) + 2I\,\frac{\tilde V}{\hat V} . \]

The first part splits into an in-phase part, \(\hat VG_L\), and a reactive part, \(\hat VG_Ly\); the beam adds to the in-phase part. So \(|\tilde i_g|^2 = (\hat VG_L + 2I)^2 + (\hat VG_Ly)^2\).

The forward power. A current source \(\tilde i_g\) with internal conductance \(G_{ext}\), feeding a matched load \(G_{ext}\), makes a voltage \(\tilde i_g/(2G_{ext})\) and delivers \(\tfrac12G_{ext}|\tilde i_g/(2G_{ext})|^2 = |\tilde i_g|^2/(8G_{ext})\). That is the power of the forward wave, whatever the load does with it; what the cavity cannot accept is reflected into the circulator’s load. With \(G_L \approx G_{ext}\),

\[ P_g = \frac{(\hat VG_L + 2I)^2 + (\hat VG_Ly)^2}{8G_L} = \frac{\hat V^2G_L}{8}\left[\left(1 + \frac{2I}{\hat VG_L}\right)^2 + y^2\right] . \]

Put \(G_L = 2/((R/Q)Q_L)\): then \(\hat V^2G_L/8 = \hat V^2/(4(R/Q)Q_L)\) and \(2I/(\hat VG_L) = (R/Q)Q_LI/\hat V\), so

\[ P_g = \frac{\hat V^2}{4(R/Q)Q_L}\left[\left(1 + \frac{(R/Q)Q_LI}{\hat V}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{2Q_L\Delta f}{f}\right)^2\right] , \]

the formula of section 12. Three checks, with a TESLA cavity at \(\hat V = 16.6\) MV, \(R/Q = 1036\ \Omega\), \(f = 1.3\) GHz:

  • Matched beam. With \(I = 10\) mA and \(Q_L = \hat V/((R/Q)I) = 1.60\times10^6\), the first bracket is \(2^2 = 4\) and \(P_g = \hat VI = 166\) kW: everything goes to the beam. A detuning of 10 Hz gives \(y = 0.025\) and adds only 25 W, because the bandwidth is wide.
  • Same coupler, no beam. \(P_g = \hat V^2/(4(R/Q)Q_L) = 41.5\) kW, a quarter of the beam power, as Padamsee, Knobloch and Hays find. The cavity takes only its wall loss (27 W at \(Q_0 = 10^{10}\)); the rest is reflected.
  • No beam, walls kept. Without the approximation \(G_L \approx G_{ext}\), the forward power at resonance is \(\hat V^2(G_c + G_{ext})^2/(8G_{ext})\) and the walls take \(\tfrac12G_c\hat V^2\). Their ratio is \(4G_cG_{ext}/(G_c + G_{ext})^2 = 4\beta_c/(1 + \beta_c)^2\) with \(\beta_c = G_{ext}/G_c = Q_0/Q_{ext}\): chapter 13’s coupling formula, all the power accepted at \(\beta_c = 1\).

Two beams that cancel. In an energy recovery linac each cavity carries the accelerated beam and, half an RF period later, the decelerated one. Half a period is a phase of \(\pi\), so the second beam’s RF current is \(2Ie^{i\pi} = -2I\). Phasors add: the net RF beam current is zero, and so is the beam’s term in \(P_g\). If the decelerated beam is off by a small phase \(\Delta\phi\), the net current is \(2I(1 - e^{i\Delta\phi}) \approx -2iI\Delta\phi\): reactive, a quarter period out of phase, and it acts like extra detuning. If the two currents differ (a beam lost on the way round), an in-phase net current remains.

The best loaded \(Q\) against microphonics. With no net current,

\[ P_g = \frac{\hat V^2}{4(R/Q)}\left[\frac{1}{Q_L} + 4Q_L\left(\frac{\Delta f}{f}\right)^2\right] . \]

A small \(Q_L\) (strong coupling) wastes power because the field leaks out through the coupler; a large one makes the bandwidth narrower than the detuning. Setting the derivative with respect to \(Q_L\) to zero, \(-1/Q_L^2 + 4(\Delta f/f)^2 = 0\), so

\[ Q_L = \frac{f}{2\Delta f}, \qquad y = 1, \qquad P_g = \frac{\hat V^2}{4(R/Q)Q_L}\times2 = \frac{\hat V^2\,\Delta f}{(R/Q)\,f} . \]

At the optimum the cavity’s half bandwidth equals the detuning. Microphonics (Padamsee, Knobloch and Hays, §19.3) are mechanical vibrations of the thin niobium cells, driven by pumps, compressors and the helium bath, that shift the frequency by a few to tens of hertz. \(\Delta f\) here must be the largest detuning to be held, not the rms.

At work. For the TESLA cavity with \(\Delta f = 10\) Hz: \(Q_L = 1.3\times10^9/20 = 6.5\times10^7\) and \(P_g = (16.6\times10^6)^2\times10/(1036\times1.3\times10^9) = 2.05\) kW. A numerical minimisation of \(P_g(Q_L)\) gives the same \(Q_L\) and power. Against the 166 kW needed to accelerate 10 mA without recovery, the saving is 98.8 per cent. The approximation \(G_c \ll G_{ext}\) is safe: \(Q_L/Q_0 = 0.0065\). If the detuning reaches 20 Hz with \(Q_L\) still at \(6.5\times10^7\), \(y = 2\) and the power is 5.1 kW; the RF system must have that headroom, or \(Q_L\) must be chosen for 20 Hz (4.1 kW).

Exercise H3. Show that the matched loaded \(Q\) for a beam on crest, \(Q_L = \hat V/((R/Q)I)\), also minimises \(P_g\) at zero detuning.

Answer

At \(y = 0\), \(P_g = (\hat V^2/(4(R/Q)))\,(1 + q)^2/Q_L\) with \(q = (R/Q)Q_LI/\hat V\), which is proportional to \(Q_L\). So \(P_g \propto (1 + q)^2/q\), and \(d/dq[(1 + q)^2/q] = (1 + q)(q - 1)/q^2 = 0\) at \(q = 1\), which is \(Q_L = \hat V/((R/Q)I)\). There \(P_g = \hat VI\), with nothing reflected.

12. Energy recovery

A linac that drives an FEL throws away almost all its beam power: the FEL takes \(\rho_F\) or \(1/(2N_u)\) of it. An energy recovery linac (ERL), proposed by Maury Tigner in 1965 for colliding beams, bends the spent beam round and sends it back through the same cavities, timed half an RF period late, so that it is decelerated and gives its energy back to the field, which accelerates the next bunches. The spent beam reaches the dump at about the injection energy.

Figure 26.8. An energy recovery linac. Left, not to scale: bunches from the injector (6 MeV in the CBETA-like example below) are accelerated in the linac (red), pass the FEL in the return loop, and come back through the same cavities (blue), decelerated, to the dump. Right: the cavity voltage over one RF period. The fresh bunch crosses at the crest and takes energy \(e\hat V\) from the field; the returning bunch, timed half a period later, crosses at the trough and gives the same energy back. Their RF currents are equal and opposite and add to zero, so the RF need only hold the field.

The power. Each cavity now carries two beams of the same current \(I\) at phases \(\phi\) and \(\phi + \pi\); their components at the RF frequency cancel, and the net beam loading is zero. From chapter 13, the forward power needed to hold a voltage \(\hat V\) in a cavity of \(R/Q\) (linac convention; circuit texts use half) and loaded quality factor \(Q_L\), with net beam current \(I_{net}\) on crest and detuning \(\Delta f\), is

\[ P_g = \frac{\hat V^2}{4(R/Q)Q_L}\left[\left(1 + \frac{(R/Q)Q_LI_{net}}{\hat V}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{2Q_L\Delta f}{f_{RF}}\right)^2\right] . \]

(Check: with \(I_{net} = I\), no detuning and the matched \(Q_L = \hat V/((R/Q)I)\), it gives \(P_g = \hat VI\), all to the beam.) With energy recovery, \(I_{net} = 0\). Only the detuning by microphonics (mechanical vibration of the thin niobium walls) remains, and choosing \(Q_L = f_{RF}/(2\Delta f)\) minimises the bracket:

\[ P_g = \frac{\hat V^2\,\Delta f}{(R/Q)\,f_{RF}} . \]

The saving per cavity is \(\hat VI - P_g\). The same holds for the whole linac: without recovery the RF must supply \(I(E_f - E_{inj})/e\) and the dump receives \(IE_f/e\); with it, the RF supplies the light, the losses and the overhead above, and the dump receives about \(IE_{inj}/e\).

Worked number. A TESLA nine-cell cavity at 1.3 GHz (\(R/Q = 1036\ \Omega\), the Handbook’s 518 Ω in the circuit convention, length 1.038 m; chapter 13) run CW at 16 MV/m holds \(\hat V = 16.6\) MV. To give 10 mA it needs 166 kW. With energy recovery and 10 Hz of microphonics, \(Q_L = 6.5\times10^7\) and \(P_g = 2.0\) kW; the wall loss at \(Q_0 = 10^{10}\) is 27 W, at 2 K. For a CBETA-like machine (6 MeV injector, 150 MeV top energy) at 10 mA, the dump would take 1.5 MW without recovery and 60 kW with it.

The ERL’s own limits are beam loss (the beam is large in current and every lost microampere heats something), the transverse beam break-up instability (a bunch excites a dipole mode, returns on the next pass and drives it in phase; chapter 22), the large energy spread of the spent beam after the FEL, which the return optics must accept, and the photocathode gun’s charge lifetime.

How it was researched and built

Stanford, 1971 to 1977. Madey’s 1971 paper was a theory; the experiments needed a magnet and a beam. The group built a superconducting double-helix winding, 5.2 m long with a 3.2 cm period, and used the superconducting linac of the High Energy Physics Laboratory at 1.3 GHz. The 1976 amplifier experiment (received 15 December 1975) sent a pulsed CO\(_2\) laser beam along the axis and recovered the gain by mixing the detector signal at the 1300 MHz bunch frequency, since the bunches filled only 0.56 per cent of the time. The 1977 oscillator (received 17 February 1977) removed the laser and added mirrors. It ran with 2.6 A peak current, 0.05 per cent energy width and an emittance of 0.06 mm mrad at 43.5 MeV. Only 0.01 per cent of the beam energy came out as light, and the authors noted that recirculating the beam in a storage ring could raise the efficiency.

Infrared oscillators and energy recovery, 1998 to 2010. Jefferson Lab built a kW-class infrared FEL on a superconducting linac with same-cell energy recovery. Running “straight ahead”, with the beam dumped, it lased near 5 µm with up to 311 W at 38 MeV and 1.1 mA. With the recirculation loop it recovered CW currents up to 5 mA and lased with up to 1720 W at 3.1 µm (Neil and colleagues, received September 1999). The IR Upgrade first lased at high power at 5.75 µm in 2004, delivering 8 mA of recirculated beam at 165 MeV, 1.33 MW of beam power, and reached 10.5 kW in 1 s pulses. In 2006, at 115 MeV with a new permanent-magnet wiggler, heating distorted the mirrors and clamped the power; a cryogenically cooled sapphire output coupler removed the distortion and the FEL gave 14.3 kW CW at 1.6 µm (Benson and colleagues, PAC 2007). At Daresbury, ALICE, an ERL test bed, first lased on 23 October 2010 at 27.5 MeV and 8 µm, the first FEL in the UK.

Proving SASE, 1998 to 2007. Hogan, Pellegrini and colleagues measured a SASE gain above \(10^5\) at 12 µm (1998). The TESLA Test Facility at DESY, built to develop superconducting cavities, saw SASE at 109 nm with a gain of about 3000 in 2000 (a paper with 145 authors), and by 2002 GW pulses of 30 to 100 fs between 95 and 105 nm, with full transverse coherence. Extended, it became FLASH, open to users since 2005. In 2007 it lased at 13.7 nm with up to 170 µJ, about 10 fs and 10 GW, with 0.6 per cent in the third harmonic (4.6 nm) and 0.03 per cent in the fifth, at 2.75 nm in the water window. In 2010, with a seventh accelerating module, 1.25 GeV gave 4.1 nm. FLASH uses TESLA modules of eight 1 m cavities at 1.3 GHz and 2 K, and compresses 50 to 80 A to 1 to 2 kA between 150 and 450 MeV.

LCLS, 2002 to 2009. SLAC used part of its 3 km linac (chapter 14). The design report (Arthur and colleagues, SLAC-R-593) appeared in 2002. The injector was commissioned in 2007, the compressors in 2008. First electrons went through the empty undulator line on 13 December 2008; twenty-one 3.4 m undulator segments were installed in late March 2009. On 10 April 2009 at 20:00 the first 12 segments were slowly inserted, the trajectory corrected for each, and by 21:00, with the peak current raised back to 3 kA, 1.5 Å light was seen on a YAG screen. Emma’s account calls it the very first trial, after two years of commissioning the injector and linac. The full undulator has 33 NdFeB permanent-magnet segments with a fixed 6.8 mm gap, 112 m of magnet in 132 m. User operation was scheduled from August 2009; the facility paper of 2010 (39 authors) reports operation from 22 to 1.2 Å.

SACLA, 2006 to 2011. The Japanese XFEL at SPring-8 chose a compact design: a thermionic CeB\(_6\) cathode in a 500 kV gun instead of a photocathode, C-band accelerating structures at 35 MV/m (8 GeV in 400 m), and short-period in-vacuum undulators, 5 m each, for a facility of about 700 m. Construction started in 2006 and was completed in March 2011. On 7 June 2011, at 7.1 GeV, the team closed the undulator gaps one by one from upstream, and at the eighth saw a bright spot on a YAG screen: 1.2 Å (Shintake, FEL 2011). The numbers fit an 18 mm period: with \(K = 1.8\), 7.1 GeV and 1.2 Å, the resonance condition gives \(\lambda_u = 17.7\) mm. By 2012 it had lased at 0.634 Å with over 10 GW (Ishikawa and colleagues, 74 authors).

The European XFEL, 2009 to 2017. Eleven countries built a 17.5 GeV superconducting linac of TESLA cavities (over 800 tested at DESY; Reschke and colleagues 2017) in a tunnel 6 to 25 m below Hamburg, 3.3 km from gun to experimental hall. It runs 10 Hz trains of up to 2700 bunches, up to 27 000 pulses a second, through three-stage compression to a design 5 kA. Construction began in early 2009. First lasing came on 2 May 2017, at 6.4 GeV and about 9 Å with all 35 SASE1 segments at \(K = 3.5\); 2 Å on 24 May; 1 mJ three days later; first user experiments scheduled for September 2017 (Weise and Decking, FEL 2017). Those numbers fit a 40 mm period: \(\lambda_r = 9.1\) Å at 6.4 GeV and 1.2 Å at 17.5 GeV. The 2020 paper (483 authors) reports lasing from 7 to 14 keV with up to 2.0 mJ, 5000 bunches a second, and 6 W of average X-ray power.

CBETA, 2020. Cornell and Brookhaven built a test ERL with four accelerating and four decelerating passes through one superconducting cryomodule of six 1.3 GHz cavities, with a single return arc of fixed-field alternating-gradient (FFA) optics made of Halbach permanent magnets, which carries 42, 78, 114 and 150 MeV in one vacuum chamber (6 MeV injection plus 36 MeV per pass). Energy recovery over all four passes was reported in 2020 (Bartnik and colleagues). The motivation was the electron-ion collider: its designs need an ERL to feed an electron cooler for the hadron beams (chapter 24), and one eRHIC concept accelerated the colliding electrons themselves in a multi-pass ERL with FFA return arcs (CBETA design report).

Costs are not quoted here: none could be confirmed from a primary source.

How an X-ray FEL is designed today. Start from the wavelength and the electron energy available. Choose \(\lambda_u\) and \(K\) (short periods with in-vacuum or small-gap permanent magnets lower the energy needed, as at SACLA). Compute \(\rho_F\) and the 3D gain length with Xie’s formula, and make the undulator longer than the saturation length, with room for tapering (reducing \(K\) along the undulator to keep the slowing electrons resonant). The beam: a photocathode RF gun (120 MV/m at the cathode in the LCLS design), a laser heater, two or three chicanes, a harmonic cavity (X-band at LCLS, 3.9 GHz at the European XFEL) to linearise the chirp, and a long main linac. The design is checked by start-to-end simulation: space-charge tracking through the gun, ELEGANT or a similar code with CSR through the linac and compressors, and GENESIS or GINGER through the undulator. Undulator segments are a few metres long, with a quadrupole, a cavity BPM and a phase shifter in each break.

At the bench

The gain curve. The basic measurement of a SASE FEL is intensity against undulator length. At LCLS in 2009 the FEL light fell on a YAG screen 50 m after the undulator, imaged by a camera; the pixel sum was the signal. To shorten the undulator, the operators either extracted segments one at a time (each can be moved 80 mm out of the beam) or kicked the electron beam off axis with a steering coil after a given segment, which stops the gain from that point on. They averaged 20 to 30 shots at each point. The curve on a log scale is a straight line, the exponential regime, then bends over at saturation. The slope gave 3.3 m at 1.5 Å with 25 segments installed; a GENESIS simulation overlaid it. The European XFEL measured the same curve at 9.3 keV. Below the straight line lies the spontaneous emission, rising linearly with length, and the start of the curve rises more slowly than the exponential (the lethargy region). The pulse energy is found separately: varying the taper over 70 m to switch the FEL on and off, LCLS saw the electron energy at the dump change by 4.6 MeV, which at 0.25 nC is 1.1 mJ.

Spiky spectra. A single-shot spectrometer (at the European XFEL, HiREX) shows a SASE spectrum as tens to hundreds of narrow spikes under an envelope about \(\rho_F\) wide, different every shot. The average of many shots is smooth. The pulse energy fluctuates shot by shot: in its first months SACLA reported about 18 per cent (Shintake’s slides). With self-seeding, one line stands above a pedestal of SASE.

Bunch length with a transverse deflecting cavity. An RF structure in a dipole mode gives each electron a sideways kick proportional to its arrival time, like a streak camera. With deflecting voltage \(V_\perp\), an electron at \(z\) gets \(y' = (eV_\perp/E)\sin(k_{RF}z) \approx (eV_\perp k_{RF}/E)z\). After a phase advance \(\Delta\psi\) to a screen, \(y = \sqrt{\beta_{y,d}\beta_{y,s}}\sin\Delta\psi\,y'\), while the beam’s own size there is \(\sqrt{\varepsilon\beta_{y,s}}\). Dividing, the length resolution is

\[ \sigma_{z,res} = \frac{E}{eV_\perp k_{RF}}\,\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\beta_{y,d}}}\,\frac{1}{|\sin\Delta\psi|}, \]

with \(\beta_{y,d}\) the beta function at the deflector. The LCLS XTCAV, after the undulator, uses two 1 m X-band (11.424 GHz) structures fed by one 50 MW klystron for a 45 MV kick (Behrens and colleagues 2014). At 13.6 GeV, with 0.5 µm normalised emittance, an assumed \(\beta_{y,d} = 50\) m and \(|\sin\Delta\psi| = 1\), the formula gives 0.77 µm, 2.6 fs; the paper reports about 4 fs rms for hard X-rays and below 1 fs for soft X-rays at lower energy, against about 10 fs for the S-band deflector at 5 GeV. A dipole after the deflector disperses energy on the other axis, so the screen shows the longitudinal phase space. Comparing it with lasing on and off shows where the FEL took energy: the lasing footprint, which gives the X-ray pulse’s length and shape shot by shot. LCLS also has a deflector in the injector (TCAV0) to measure the bunch at 135 MeV (0.65 mm rms at 250 pC; Emma, table 1) and to see the laser heater’s effect.

Beam-based alignment of the undulator. The beam must be straight to a few micrometres over 100 m: an angle of \(\theta_e\) shifts the resonance by \(\gamma^2\theta_e^2/(1+K^2/2)\), which for LCLS must stay below \(\rho_F\), so \(\theta_e < 2.4\) µrad. At LCLS each segment sits on a girder with its quadrupole, an X-band cavity BPM of below 1 µm resolution, and an insertable beam-finder wire (two 40 µm carbon wires referenced to the magnetic centre). Five motorised cams move each girder in \(x\), \(y\), pitch, yaw and roll; a 0.5 µm step of a quadrupole gives a 30 nrad kick, seen on the downstream BPMs with 5 nrad resolution. The procedure: with the undulators out, record the BPM readings at several beam energies (4.3, 7.0, 9.25 and 13.6 GeV), scaling the magnets upstream but changing nothing in the undulator line. A trajectory that depends on energy reveals quadrupole and BPM offsets, which are solved for and corrected by moving the girders, until the trajectory is the same at every energy: dispersion-free and straight. Then the segments go in one by one, the field integral of each is checked by its effect on the orbit, and the \(K\) of each is measured from its spontaneous emission (at first, on a YAG screen behind a nickel foil, whose absorption edge marks a photon energy). A 5 mrad cant of the jaws lets \(K\) be fine-tuned by \(\pm0.6\) per cent by moving the segment sideways.

RF power with and without energy recovery. In an ERL the forward power of each cavity is the evidence. Without beam, it is the small \(\hat V^2/(4(R/Q)Q_L)[1 + (2Q_L\Delta f/f_{RF})^2]\), fluctuating with microphonics. With the beam accelerated and dumped straight ahead, it rises by \(\hat VI\) per cavity (166 kW at 10 mA in the example above). With recovery working, it falls back to near the no-beam value, and the dump’s calorimeter or current monitor confirms the beam arrived at low energy. The decelerating phase is set so that the spent beam’s energy spread fits the return line’s acceptance (Benson and colleagues). At Jefferson Lab the injector cavities, which get no energy back, were limited to about 40 kW each by window heating, which capped the current near 8 mA.

How it fails, and what it looks like.

  • No lasing. The YAG shows only the faint spontaneous glow. Check the energy against \(K\) (a 0.1 per cent energy error is several \(\rho_F\) of wavelength mismatch at 1.5 Å), the trajectory (redo the alignment), the peak current (the bunch-length monitor after each chicane, calibrated with the deflector), and the emittance.
  • Compression drifts. Coherent edge radiation from the last chicane dipole changes; the FEL power falls. LCLS’s “6×6” feedback uses six RF phases and amplitudes to hold energies and bunch lengths, keeping the 3 kA peak current to about 10 per cent rms.
  • CSR at full compression. A BPM after BC1 sees the beam steered by its CSR energy loss; the effect is used to find the full-compression phase quickly. The emittance after the chicane grows.
  • Microbunching. Coherent optical transition radiation on OTR screens downstream of the compressors, which makes them useless for beam size; a slice energy spread larger than expected; less FEL power. Turn up the laser heater.
  • Undulator damage. Beam loss or radiation demagnetises permanent magnets; collimators before the undulator and a fast abort kicker protect them.
  • Mirror heating (oscillators). The power clamps as the mirror distorts; Jefferson Lab found its efficiency fell as the beam power rose until it cooled the output coupler cryogenically.
  • ERL losses. A microampere lost in an uncooled chamber raises the vacuum; Jefferson Lab kept losses below 100 nA in most of the machine and 10 nA at the wiggler, matched the beam on its halo, and raised the beam break-up threshold above 10 mA with an optical rotator. Its cathode gave about 500 C before it needed attention.

Where it stops

An X-ray FEL is limited by its driver. The wavelength goes as \(\lambda_u/\gamma^2\) and the emittance rule as \(\varepsilon_n \lesssim \gamma\lambda_r/4\pi\), so shorter wavelengths need higher energy or brighter beams. Shorter periods need smaller gaps and give lower \(K\); the European XFEL’s 1 Å needs 17.5 GeV and a superconducting linac of about 1.7 to 1.9 km (sources differ; the Handbook gives 1.7 km and 768 cavities) in a 3.3 km facility. Brightness is set by the photoinjector and by how well compression preserves it; CSR and the microbunching instability tax every kiloampere. Repetition rate is set by the RF: LCLS’s copper linac fires at up to 120 Hz, while superconducting linacs give long bunch trains (the European XFEL) or continuous operation (LCLS-II, Galayda 2018).

What would move the wall is a much shorter accelerator: fields of tens to hundreds of GV/m in a plasma wave, which could put an FEL driver in a room if its beams can be made as bright and as stable as a linac’s. That is chapter 27. For energy recovery the limits are current (beam break-up, halo, cathodes) and the spread of the spent beam; the ideas for higher-energy ERLs and colliders return in chapter 25.

Exercises

1. Find the resonant wavelength and photon energy of LCLS at 13.64 GeV, \(\lambda_u = 3\) cm, \(K = 3.5\), and the peak undulator field.

Answer

\(\gamma = 13.64\times10^9/510\,999 = 26\,693\). \(\lambda_r = 0.03\times(1 + 6.125)/(2\times26\,693^2) = 1.50\) Å, and \(hc/\lambda_r = 12\,398.4/1.50\) eV \(= 8.27\) keV. \(B_0 = K/(0.934\times3.0) = 1.25\) T.

2. The 1977 Stanford oscillator: helical magnet, \(\lambda_u = 3.2\) cm, 2.4 kG, beam 43.5 MeV kinetic, one bunch every 84.6 ns. Find \(K\), the predicted wavelength, the mirror spacing, and the energy that would give the measured 3.417 µm.

Answer

\(K = 0.934\times0.24\times3.2 = 0.717\). \(\gamma = 1 + 43.5/0.511 = 86.13\); for a helix \(\lambda_r = \lambda_u(1+K^2)/(2\gamma^2) = 0.032\times1.514/(2\times7418) = 3.27\) µm. Mirrors: \(c\times84.6\ \mathrm{ns}/2 = 12.7\) m. For 3.417 µm, \(\gamma = \sqrt{\lambda_u(1+K^2)/(2\lambda)} = 84.21\), 42.5 MeV, 2.2 per cent lower: inside the stated 5 per cent calibration error.

3. On a gain curve the FEL signal rises by a factor 400 between 20 m and 40 m of undulator, in the straight part of the log plot. Find \(L_G\). If saturation is 18 gain lengths, how long must the undulator be?

Answer

\(P \propto e^{s/L_G}\), so \(L_G = 20\ \mathrm{m}/\ln400 = 3.34\) m. Saturation at about \(18\times3.34 = 60\) m; an undulator of 70 to 80 m leaves room for a taper.

4. Compute \(\rho_F\) and the 1D gain length for LCLS: 3.0 kA, 13.64 GeV, \(K = 3.5\), \(\lambda_u = 3\) cm, \(\varepsilon_n = 0.4\) µm, \(\beta_x = 30\) m. Estimate the saturated power and compare with 15 GW.

Answer

\(\xi = 12.25/(4 + 24.5) = 0.430\), \([JJ] = J_0 - J_1 = 0.744\). \(\sigma_x = \sqrt{0.4\times10^{-6}\times30/26\,693} = 21.2\) µm. \(\rho_F = 5.84\times10^{-4}\); \(L_G = 0.03/(4\pi\sqrt3\times5.84\times10^{-4}) = 2.36\) m (measured 3.3 m). \(P_b = 13.64\ \mathrm{GV}\times3000\ \mathrm{A} = 40.9\) TW; \(\rho_FP_b = 24\) GW, the same order as the 15 GW estimated from 1.1 mJ in about 75 fs.

5. Show that the three-equation linear model gives \(d^3b/ds^3 = i(2k_u\rho_F)^3b\), find the growing root, and show that a seed field with no initial bunching gives one ninth of its power to the growing mode.

Answer

\(b' = -2ik_u\tilde P\), \(\tilde P' = i\kappa_1\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L\), \(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L' = i\kappa_2b\) with \(\kappa_1 = eK[JJ]/(4\gamma^2mc^2)\), \(\kappa_2 = \mu_0c^2en_0K[JJ]/(2\gamma)\). Then \(b''' = -2ik_u\tilde P'' = -2ik_u(i\kappa_1)(i\kappa_2)b = 2ik_u\kappa_1\kappa_2b\), and \(2k_u\kappa_1\kappa_2 = (2k_u\rho_F)^3\) defines \(\rho_F\). Roots \(\mu_j = 2k_u\rho_F\,\lbrace e^{i\pi/6}, e^{i5\pi/6}, e^{-i\pi/2}\rbrace\); the first grows with \(\mathrm{Re}\,\mu = \sqrt3k_u\rho_F\). The field obeys the same cubic; with \(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L(0) = \mathcal{E}_0\), \(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L'(0) = i\kappa_2b(0) = 0\) and \(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L''(0) = i\kappa_2b'(0) = 0\), the solution is \(\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_L = (\mathcal{E}_0/3)\sum_je^{\mu_js}\), since \(\sum_j\mu_j = \sum_j\mu_j^2 = 0\). The growing term has amplitude \(\mathcal{E}_0/3\), power \(\mathcal{E}_0^2/9\) relative.

6. Derive \(R_{56} \approx -2\theta_B^2(\Delta L + 2L_B/3)\) for a symmetric four-dipole chicane and evaluate it for LCLS BC1 (\(\theta_B = 4.97°\), \(L_B = 0.204\) m, \(\Delta L = 2.43\) m). With a chirp of \(-18.2\) m\(^{-1}\), what compression does \(R_{56} = -45.5\) mm give, and what chirp gives the measured factor 7.6?

Answer

A drift of projected length \(\Delta L\) at angle \(\theta_B\) is longer by \(\Delta L\theta_B^2/2\); there are two. A dipole of projected length \(L_B\) has arc \(L_B\theta_B/\sin\theta_B \approx L_B(1 + \theta_B^2/6)\); there are four. The extra path is \(\theta_B^2(\Delta L + 2L_B/3)\); with \(\theta_B \propto 1/(1+\delta)\), \(d/d\delta\) gives \(R_{56} = -2\theta_B^2(\Delta L + 2L_B/3) = -2\times0.0867^2\times2.566 = -38.6\) mm. \(C = 1/(1 - 0.0455\times18.2) = 1/(1 - 0.829) = 5.9\). For \(C = 7.6\), \(R_{56}\chi = 1 - 1/7.6 = 0.868\), \(\chi = -19.1\) m\(^{-1}\), 4.7 per cent more.

7. The LCLS laser heater adds 20 keV rms at 135 MeV, and the total compression is 90. Find the slice energy spread at 13.64 GeV and compare with \(\rho_F\). Why add it at 135 MeV rather than at the undulator?

Answer

Absolute spread is unchanged by acceleration and multiplied by the compression: \(20\ \mathrm{keV}\times90 = 1.8\) MeV, \(1.3\times10^{-4}\) of 13.64 GeV, below \(\rho_F = 5.8\times10^{-4}\). It must be added before the compressors because its job is to damp the instability that grows in them: in BC1 (\(R_{56} = 45.5\) mm, 250 MeV) it reduces a 20 µm ripple by \(e^{-(kR_{56}\sigma_\delta)^2/2} = 0.52\) per pass and a 5 µm one by \(3\times10^{-5}\).

8. For HGHG show that \(|b_{n_h}| = |J_{n_h}(n_h\mathcal{M}\mathcal{D})|e^{-n_h^2\mathcal{D}^2/2}\), and find the best bunching at the 10th harmonic for \(\mathcal{M} = 5\) and \(\mathcal{M} = 10\).

Answer

\(z_f = z_i - R_{56}\delta_u - R_{56}\mathcal{M}\sigma_\delta\sin(k_sz_i)\). Then \(e^{-in_hk_sz_f} = e^{-in_hk_sz_i}e^{in_hk_sR_{56}\delta_u}\sum_mJ_m(n_hk_sR_{56}\mathcal{M}\sigma_\delta)e^{imk_sz_i}\). Averaging over \(z_i\) keeps \(m = n_h\); averaging over Gaussian \(\delta_u\) gives \(e^{-(n_hk_sR_{56}\sigma_\delta)^2/2}\). So \(|b| = |J_{n_h}(n_h\mathcal{M}\mathcal{D})|e^{-n_h^2\mathcal{D}^2/2}\). Maximising over \(\mathcal{D}\) numerically: 0.028 for \(\mathcal{M} = 5\) and 0.155 for \(\mathcal{M} = 10\) (at \(\mathcal{D} = 0.113\)). The price of \(\mathcal{M} = 10\) is an energy spread \(\sqrt{1 + 50} = 7.1\) times larger in the radiator.

9. A TESLA cavity (\(R/Q = 1036\ \Omega\), 1.038 m) runs CW at 16 MV/m with 10 mA. Find the RF power without recovery, and with recovery for 10 Hz of microphonics at the optimum \(Q_L\). What fraction is saved?

Answer

\(\hat V = 16.6\) MV; without recovery \(P = \hat VI = 166\) kW. With recovery, \(Q_L = f_{RF}/(2\Delta f) = 6.5\times10^7\) and \(P_g = \hat V^2\Delta f/((R/Q)f_{RF}) = (16.6\times10^6)^2\times10/(1036\times1.3\times10^9) = 2.0\) kW. Saving 164 kW, 98.8 per cent; the wall loss (27 W at \(Q_0 = 10^{10}\)) is extra, paid at 2 K.

10. Combine the angle condition \(\varepsilon < (2/\sqrt3)(\lambda_r/4\pi)(\beta_x/L_G)\) and the size condition \(\varepsilon\beta_x < \lambda_rL_G/4\pi\) to get the emittance rule, and evaluate the normalised emittance needed for 1 Å at 17.5 GeV.

Answer
