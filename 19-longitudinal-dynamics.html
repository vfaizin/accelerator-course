19 Longitudinal dynamics in depth

1945 to today: from McMillan’s phase equation to the CERN PS, the LHC and MAX IV; past the wall of transition, where the restoring force of phase stability vanishes, and past the limit of what one sine wave can do to a bunch, by treating longitudinal phase space as a Hamiltonian system that can be steered, rotated, split and merged.

In one page

The wall. Phase stability (chapter 12) holds a particle near the synchronous phase. But in a strong-focusing proton ring the slip factor \(\eta\) passes through zero at transition, where the bunch has no longitudinal focusing, is at its shortest and has its largest energy spread. In 1959 the CERN PS lost its beam there until the RF phase was switched. Later, colliders needed bunches of a given length, spacing and number that the injectors’ RF could not make directly.

The idea. A particle’s arrival phase \(\phi\) and energy offset \(\Delta E\) obey a two-line map, turn by turn, which in the continuous limit comes from a Hamiltonian. Longitudinal phase space then behaves like the phase space of a pendulum: it has a separatrix, a bucket and an area (the longitudinal emittance) that slow changes preserve. Everything else in the chapter is a way of moving that area around.

Equations to remember. With \(h\) the harmonic number, \(\hat{V}\) the voltage (V), \(E\) the total energy (eV), \(\omega_0\) the revolution angular frequency (rad/s) and \(\eta = \alpha_c - 1/\gamma^2\):

\[ \Delta E_{n+1} = \Delta E_n + q\hat{V}(\sin\phi_n - \sin\phi_s), \qquad \phi_{n+1} = \phi_n + \frac{2\pi h\eta}{\beta^2 E}\,\Delta E_{n+1} \]

\[ Q_s = \frac{\Omega_s}{\omega_0} = \sqrt{\frac{-h\eta\,q\hat{V}\cos\phi_s}{2\pi\beta^2 E}} \]

\[ \Delta E_{bucket} = \sqrt{\frac{2q\hat{V}\beta^2E}{\pi h|\eta|}}, \qquad A_{bucket} = \frac{8\,\Delta E_{bucket}}{h\omega_0}\ \ \text{(eV s, stationary)} \]

\[ \sigma_\delta = \frac{\Omega_s\,\sigma_t}{|\eta|}, \qquad \sigma_z = \frac{\beta c\,|\eta|\,\sigma_\delta}{\Omega_s} \]

and the non-adiabatic time at transition, \(T_c = \left(\pi\beta^2mc^2\gamma_t^4/(h\omega_0^2q\hat{V}|\cos\phi_s|\dot\gamma)\right)^{1/3}\).

Numbers worth knowing. LHC: 400.79 MHz, \(h\) = 35640, synchrotron frequency 63.7 Hz at 450 GeV (8 MV) and 23.0 Hz at 7 TeV (16 MV), buckets of 1.43 and 7.91 eV s, bunches of 1.0 and 2.5 eV s. Synchrotron tunes are of order \(10^{-3}\) in the LHC, the PS and a light source alike. The PS crosses transition at \(\gamma_t\) = 6.08; its non-adiabatic time is about 1.4 ms, and since 1973 a \(\gamma_t\) jump (2.3 in 0.8 ms then, about 1.2 in 0.5 ms today) has crossed it some fifty times faster. The PS makes the LHC’s 72-bunch, 25 ns train from 6 bunches by splitting each in three, then in two, then in two again, and rotates them to under 4 ns. Liouville’s theorem forbids all this RF work to shrink the emittance; only cooling and radiation can.

The wall

What 1945 left open. McMillan’s letter of September 1945 and Veksler’s notes of 1944 gave the small-oscillation picture (chapter 15). Bohm and Foldy’s “The theory of the synchrotron” (Physical Review 70, 249, 1946) was among the first full theoretical papers on the machine. The weak-focusing synchrotrons of 1946 to 1960 had \(\gamma_t < 1\) and so never met transition (chapter 15). For them the longitudinal theory of 1945 was enough.

Transition. Strong focusing (1952) changed that. A ring with tunes of several units has \(\alpha_c \approx 1/Q_x^2\) small and \(\gamma_t \approx Q_x\) of a few units, inside the proton energy range (chapter 16). At transition \(\eta = 0\): all momenta take the same time round the ring, the restoring force of phase stability vanishes, and on the far side the stable phase is on the other slope of the wave. The designers of the PS and the AGS knew this. By E. J. N. Wilson’s account (1996), Kjell Johnsen showed in Bergen in 1953 that there was little to fear; another CERN history (of John Adams) says Johnsen had to be persuaded. At the CERN Symposium of 1956 Johnsen presented “Effects of non-linearities on the phase transition” and G. K. Green “Phase transition in the alternating-gradient accelerator”. The cautious, among them the British laboratory’s Nimrod, kept weak focusing.

The PS, 1959. The PS took its first turn in September 1959. That autumn its beam was lost at transition. Wolfgang Schnell built a circuit, by Wilson’s account in a Nescafé tin, to change the RF phase at the moment of transition, and on 24 November 1959 the beam was accelerated through transition to 24 GeV; the machine, rated at 28 GeV, was brought to energy that November (Livingston and Blewett 1962), with pulses of about \(10^{10}\) protons at first (Gilardoni and Manglunki 2013). The AGS reached its design energy of 33 GeV in July 1960. By about 1961 both machines gave \(3\times10^{11}\) protons per pulse (Livingston and Blewett). At 1959 intensities the phase jump was enough.

The second wall: intensity at transition. As the intensity rose, the bunch’s passage through transition became the main longitudinal limit. Near \(\eta = 0\) the bunch is shortest and its charge density highest, and the negative-mass instability (chapter 22) blew it up. In that instability the bunch’s own charge, which pushes particles apart along the beam, makes them gather instead: above transition a particle pushed forward gains energy and so goes round more slowly, falling back into the crowd behind it, and a small lump of charge grows. Crossing faster was the only cure that worked at high intensity, and in 1973 the PS installed a \(\gamma_t\) jump (Hardt 1974).

The third wall: shaping bunches. A collider asks for a particular bunch length, spacing and number. The CERN PS of 1959 accelerated on \(h = 20\); the LHC needs bunches every 25 ns, under 4 ns long. The obvious method, to debunch the beam and rebunch it at the new harmonic, failed in the PS in 1999: a microwave instability (a fast growth of short ripples of density along the beam, driven by the fields the beam itself leaves in the vacuum chamber around it; chapter 22) blew up the momentum spread of the coasting beam, and the bunches could not be made shorter than 5 ns (Evans and Bryant 2008). A coasting beam is one spread evenly round the ring with no RF holding it in bunches. The way past was to treat the longitudinal phase space as a Hamiltonian system and move its area around with several harmonics at once, without ever letting the beam coast.

The physics

Symbols. \(\phi\) is the RF phase at which a particle crosses the cavity, increasing for later arrival; \(\phi_s\) the synchronous phase; \(\Delta E = E - E_s\) the energy offset (eV), and \(\delta = \Delta p/p = \Delta E/(\beta^2E)\). (The last step is chapter 1’s \(dE = v\,dp\): dividing by \(E = pc^2/v\) gives \(dE/E = (v^2/c^2)\,dp/p = \beta^2\,dp/p\).) \(n\) counts turns. \(T_0 = 2\pi/\omega_0\) is the revolution period. \(\hat{V}\) is the total voltage amplitude per turn. Some texts (Tecker, for example) define the slip factor with the opposite sign, \(1/\gamma^2 - 1/\gamma_t^2\); in this course \(\eta = \alpha_c - 1/\gamma^2\) is negative below transition and positive above.

Pre-requisite (continued): Matrices, determinants, eigenvalues

Pre-requisite. Chapter 8 showed that the determinant of a \(2\times2\) matrix is the factor by which it multiplies areas, and that \(\det(AB) = \det A\,\det B\). This section carries that over to maps that are not linear, through the Jacobian matrix, and shows that a kick followed by a drift keeps area exactly, while the same two steps done carelessly do not. Section 1 uses it to show that the turn-by-turn map of longitudinal motion is area preserving. Taught from Tong, Classical Mechanics, section 10.2 (the Jacobian in the proof of Liouville’s theorem) and section 10.4 (canonical transformations and the symplectic Jacobian); Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §45 and §46; and S. Y. Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 3, sections I.2 and I.3 (the synchrotron mapping equation and why it must keep area).

The idea. One turn of a ring takes each particle from \((\phi_n, \Delta E_n)\) to \((\phi_{n+1}, \Delta E_{n+1})\). The rule is not linear, because the kick contains \(\sin\phi\), so it is not a matrix, and chapter 8’s rule about determinants does not apply as it stands. But any smooth map looks linear if you look closely enough. Take a tiny patch of the plane. The map moves it, turns it and stretches it, and over a patch small enough the stretching is the same everywhere in it. That local linear map is a matrix, the Jacobian, and its determinant is the local factor by which areas change.

The Jacobian. Write a map of the plane as \(x_1 = F(x, y)\), \(y_1 = G(x, y)\), where the subscript 1 marks the values after the map. Take a point and a neighbour displaced from it by small \(dx\) and \(dy\). To first order in the displacement, each new coordinate changes by the sum of its slopes times the small changes (F1, section 9: \(\Delta f \approx (\partial f/\partial x)\Delta x + (\partial f/\partial y)\Delta y\)), so the two images differ by

\[ \begin{pmatrix} dx_1 \\ dy_1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \partial F/\partial x & \partial F/\partial y \\ \partial G/\partial x & \partial G/\partial y \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} dx \\ dy \end{pmatrix} = \mathcal{J}\begin{pmatrix} dx \\ dy \end{pmatrix}. \]

The matrix of partial derivatives \(\mathcal{J}\), evaluated at the point, is the Jacobian matrix of the map. It sends a small rectangle of sides \(dx\) and \(dy\) to a small parallelogram of area \(|\det\mathcal{J}|\,dx\,dy\), by chapter 8’s rule. The determinant depends on the point: a nonlinear map may stretch areas in one place and shrink them in another. If \(\det\mathcal{J} = 1\) at every point, every small rectangle keeps its area, and so does any region, since a region is a union of small rectangles and its area is their sum (F2, section 8). This is the step at the heart of the proof of Liouville’s theorem in chapter 7. There the map was “let the flow run for a time \(dt\)”, and its Jacobian had determinant \(1 + (\partial\dot x/\partial x + \partial\dot p/\partial p)\,dt\), which Hamilton’s equations make exactly 1.

Doing two maps in turn multiplies their Jacobians. Apply map \(A\), then map \(B\). A small displacement is first multiplied by \(\mathcal{J}_A\), evaluated at the starting point, then by \(\mathcal{J}_B\), evaluated where \(A\) landed. So the Jacobian of the combined map is the product \(\mathcal{J}_B\mathcal{J}_A\) (this is the chain rule, rate of change times rate of change, written as matrices), and its determinant is \(\det\mathcal{J}_B\,\det\mathcal{J}_A\). If each step keeps area, so does the whole: a ring of a thousand elements, each area preserving, is area preserving.

Shears. A shear moves one coordinate by an amount that depends only on the other. There are two kinds:

  • a drift, \(x_1 = x + g(y)\), \(y_1 = y\), with Jacobian \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & g'(y) \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\);
  • a kick, \(x_1 = x\), \(y_1 = y + f(x)\), with Jacobian \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ f'(x) & 1 \end{pmatrix}\).

Both determinants are 1 at every point, however nonlinear \(f\) and \(g\) are. Picture a deck of cards pushed sideways, each card by its own amount: the side of the deck changes shape, but every card keeps its length and thickness, so the side keeps its area.

The longitudinal map. Section 1’s turn is a kick in energy followed by a drift in phase. Write \(c_\phi = 2\pi h\eta/(\beta^2E)\) (rad/eV) for the phase slip per unit energy offset. With the variables in the order \((\phi, \Delta E)\):

\[ \mathcal{J}_{kick} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ q\hat V\cos\phi & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathcal{J}_{drift} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & c_\phi \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \]

\[ \mathcal{J}_{turn} = \mathcal{J}_{drift}\,\mathcal{J}_{kick} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + c_\phi q\hat V\cos\phi & c_\phi \\ q\hat V\cos\phi & 1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

The kick’s entry \(q\hat V\cos\phi\) is the derivative of \(q\hat V(\sin\phi - \sin\phi_s)\) with respect to \(\phi\). The determinant of the turn is \((1 + c_\phi q\hat V\cos\phi) - c_\phi q\hat V\cos\phi = 1\), at every phase. That is why the phase is advanced with the new energy \(\Delta E_{n+1}\): each half of the turn is then a pure shear.

At the synchronous phase the trace of \(\mathcal{J}_{turn}\) is \(2 + c_\phi q\hat V\cos\phi_s\). Chapter 11 showed that a one-turn matrix of determinant 1 gives a stable oscillation when its trace lies between −2 and 2, with the trace equal to \(2\cos2\pi Q\); chapter 12 found the same tune by solving this map directly.

At work (LHC, 7 TeV). \(c_\phi = 2\pi\times35640\times3.225\times10^{-4}/(7\times10^{12}\ \text{eV}) = 1.032\times10^{-11}\) rad/eV. At \(\phi_s = \pi\) with 16 MV, \(c_\phi q\hat V\cos\phi_s = -1.651\times10^{-4}\). The trace is \(2 - 1.651\times10^{-4}\), so \(\cos2\pi Q = 1 - 0.825\times10^{-4}\) and \(Q = 2.045\times10^{-3}\), the synchrotron tune of section 3. I put 400 particles on a small ellipse of phase amplitude 0.5 rad (energy amplitude 623 MeV) and tracked them through the map: after 10,000 turns the polygon they form encloses the same area to one part in \(10^7\), the residue being the coarseness of a 400-sided polygon.

The same steps done carelessly. Suppose a program updates both variables at once from their old values:

\[ \phi_{n+1} = \phi_n + c_\phi\,\Delta E_n, \qquad \Delta E_{n+1} = \Delta E_n + q\hat V(\sin\phi_n - \sin\phi_s). \]

This is Euler’s method (F1, section 11). Its Jacobian is \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & c_\phi \\ q\hat V\cos\phi & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), whose determinant is \(1 - c_\phi q\hat V\cos\phi\). A stable bucket needs \(c_\phi q\hat V\cos\phi_s < 0\), so near the centre the determinant exceeds 1 and every turn inflates areas. In the LHC at 7 TeV the factor is \(1 + 1.65\times10^{-4}\) per turn, which looks harmless; but after \(10^4\) turns, 0.89 s, it is \((1 + 1.65\times10^{-4})^{10^4} = 5.2\). (For small \(x\), \(\ln(1 + x) \approx x\), so \((1 + x)^N = e^{N\ln(1 + x)} \approx e^{Nx}\), here \(e^{1.65}\); F1, section 4.) Tracked with Euler’s update, the same 0.5 rad ellipse had grown in area by a factor 1.17 after 1,000 turns and 4.6 after 10,000 (a little less than 5.2, because as the particles spiral out they reach phases where \(\cos\phi\), and so the excess of the determinant, is smaller). The physics has no such growth: the program invented it. This is why Lee writes the mapping equation in its symplectic form, kick first and then drift with the new energy, and notes that its Jacobian is 1; for one pair of variables, symplectic means exactly \(\det\mathcal{J} = 1\). Changing variables. The Jacobian also converts areas between coordinate systems. The change from \((\phi, \Delta E)\) to \((\phi, \delta)\), with \(\delta = \Delta E/(\beta^2E)\), has the Jacobian \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/(\beta^2E) \end{pmatrix}\) and so divides every area by \(\beta^2E\). A quantity conserved in one set of variables need not be conserved in another; exercise J3 shows what this means for a ramp.

Exercise J1. Reverse the order of the two steps: first drift the phase with the old energy, \(\phi_1 = \phi + c_\phi\Delta E\), then kick the energy with the new phase, \(\Delta E_1 = \Delta E + q\hat V(\sin\phi_1 - \sin\phi_s)\). Is this map area preserving?

Answer

Yes. The drift’s Jacobian is \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & c_\phi \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) and the kick’s, evaluated at \(\phi_1\), is \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ q\hat V\cos\phi_1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Their product is \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & c_\phi \\ q\hat V\cos\phi_1 & 1 + c_\phi q\hat V\cos\phi_1 \end{pmatrix}\), of determinant 1. Any sequence of shears keeps area. What breaks it is updating both variables from the old values at once, which is not a sequence of shears.

Exercise J2. At LHC injection the synchrotron tune is \(5.66\times10^{-3}\) and the revolution frequency 11 245.5 Hz. By what factor would Euler’s update inflate a small bunch’s area in one second?

Answer

\(c_\phi q\hat V\cos\phi_s = -(2\pi Q_s)^2 = -1.265\times10^{-3}\), so the determinant near the centre is \(1.001265\). In one second, \(11\,245.5\) turns, the factor is \(\exp(11\,245.5\times\ln1.001265) = e^{14.2} = 1.5\times10^6\). A tracking code written this way would show the beam exploding out of the bucket within a second, with no physical cause.

Exercise J3. In the LHC, \(\beta \approx 1\) and the revolution frequency is fixed, so the area in \((\phi, \Delta E)\) is conserved during a slow ramp (section 8 explains why). A bunch occupies 1.0 eV s at 450 GeV and is ramped to 7 TeV without blow-up. By what factor does its area in the \((\phi, \delta)\) plane change?

Answer

The change of variables divides areas by \(\beta^2E\). The area in \((\phi, \Delta E)\) is the same at both energies, so the area in \((\phi, \delta)\) falls in the ratio \(450/7000 = 0.064\): about sixteen times smaller. Measured as a fraction of momentum, the bunch’s phase-space area shrinks during acceleration; measured in eV s, it does not. This is the longitudinal form of adiabatic damping.

Pre-requisite (continued): Hamiltonian perturbation theory, resonances, KAM and chaos

Pre-requisite. Chapter 12 showed how resonances break an oscillator’s invariant curves into chains of islands and, when neighbouring chains overlap, into chaos. This section gives the simplest system in which all of that can be watched: the standard map, a kick followed by a drift. It shows that section 1’s turn map is the standard map once its constants are scaled away, that the whole of stationary-bucket motion then depends on one number \(K \approx (2\pi Q_s)^2\), and how large that number must be before the bucket’s edge dissolves. Section 1 uses it when it calls its map “the standard map with a constant torque added”. Taught from Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 3, section I.2 and exercise 10 of section I (the synchrotron map rewritten as the standard map); the Handbook of Accelerator Physics and Engineering (Chao, Tigner and others, 3rd edition), section 2.3.3 (Taylor map, Hénon map, standard map); and Tong, Classical Mechanics, section 10.5 (integrable systems and invariant tori).

The map. Take an angle \(\theta\) (rad) and a variable \(p\) (rad per turn) that sets how far the angle advances in one turn. Once per turn:

\[ p_{n+1} = p_n - K\sin\theta_n, \qquad \theta_{n+1} = \theta_n + p_{n+1}. \]

The first line is a kick that depends on the angle; the second is a drift by the new \(p\). Both are shears, so the map keeps area exactly (previous section). There is one parameter, the kick strength \(K\), a pure number. A mechanical picture is a rod free to turn about a pivot, struck sharply once per period by a blow of fixed direction: the kicked rotor. Boris Chirikov made it the standard model of chaos in Hamiltonian systems (his review of 1979 is cited in chapter 18).

From the longitudinal map. Take a stationary bucket above transition, \(\phi_s = \pi\) and \(\eta > 0\), as in the LHC. Put \(\theta = \phi - \pi\), so that \(\sin\phi = -\sin\theta\), and measure energy by the phase slip it causes per turn, \(p = c_\phi\Delta E\) with \(c_\phi = 2\pi h\eta/(\beta^2E)\). Section 1’s two lines become

\[ p_{n+1} = p_n - c_\phi q\hat V\sin\theta_n, \qquad \theta_{n+1} = \theta_n + p_{n+1}, \]

the standard map with \(K = c_\phi q\hat V\). Below transition the same thing follows with the signs of \(\theta\) and \(p\) reversed. With \(\phi_s \ne 0\) or \(\pi\) the kick gains a constant term proportional to \(\sin\phi_s\), the torque of the tilted pendulum; the map is then no longer exactly the standard map, and the number that plays the part of \(K\) for small oscillations is \(c_\phi q\hat V|\cos\phi_s|\). So every ring’s stationary bucket, whatever its energy, voltage and harmonic, is the same map up to a change of scale: only \(K\) distinguishes them.

What \(K\) is. Linearise about \(\theta = 0\): the turn matrix has trace \(2 - K\) and determinant 1, so the motion is stable for \(0 < K < 4\) with \(\cos2\pi Q = 1 - K/2\) (chapter 12 derived this). For small \(K\), \(2\pi Q \approx \sqrt{K}\): \(K\) is the square of the synchrotron phase advance per turn. The other fixed point, \(\theta = \pi\), has trace \(2 + K > 2\) and is unstable for every \(K > 0\).

Small \(K\): the pendulum. When \(K \ll 1\), \(p\) changes little in a turn and \(\theta\) moves slowly, so the once-per-turn kicks blur into a steady force. The map then follows the pendulum Hamiltonian \(H = p^2/2 + K(1 - \cos\theta)\), with time counted in turns, and its separatrix reaches \(p = \pm2\sqrt{K}\): section 2’s continuous limit. The approximation fails in two places. Near the unstable point the particle crawls, the kicks no longer average out, and they act like a drive at the revolution frequency; the separatrix is replaced by a thin layer in which motion is chaotic. Its width falls off exponentially as \(K\) falls (Chirikov), so for proton rings it is far too thin to matter. And the map is periodic in \(p\) with period \(2\pi\): a particle that slips a whole RF period per turn is in the same state as one that does not slip at all. So the map has rows of buckets stacked in \(p\) as well as side by side in \(\theta\). In a real ring that row is far beyond any energy aperture: for the LHC at 7 TeV, \(p = 2\pi\) means \(\Delta E = 2\pi/c_\phi = 6\times10^{11}\) eV.

Large \(K\): chaos. As \(K\) grows, the kick resolves into its harmonics, resonances between the synchrotron oscillation and the turn appear as chains of islands inside and around the bucket, and the chaotic layer at the separatrix widens. The last invariant curve that separates one row of buckets from the next is destroyed at \(K = 0.9716\) (Greene’s value, cited in chapter 18). Beyond it a particle near the edge can wander from row to row, though just above the threshold it does so very slowly (chapter 18’s run at \(K = 1.0\) saw no orbit cross in \(10^5\) turns). I started 4,000 particles at \(\theta = 0\), between 95 and 99.95 per cent of the separatrix height, and tracked them for 2,000 turns. For \(K\) = 0.5 or less, none rose more than 10 per cent above the separatrix height; for \(K = 1.0\), 89 per cent did, although none reached the next row. Lee’s exercise quotes a larger critical tune, 0.393 (\(K \approx 6.1\)), for the point at which the whole phase space, centre included, becomes chaotic (chapter 12 cites it); Greene’s value marks the earlier loss of the last barrier at the bucket’s edge.

1. The two difference equations

Energy. At each passage the synchronous particle gains \(q\hat{V}\sin\phi_s\), which the rising field demands (chapter 15). Another particle, crossing at \(\phi_n\), gains \(q\hat{V}\sin\phi_n\). Its energy offset therefore changes by the difference:

\[ \Delta E_{n+1} = \Delta E_n + q\hat{V}\left(\sin\phi_n - \sin\phi_s\right). \]

Phase. Between passages the particle’s period differs from the synchronous one by \(\Delta T_0/T_0 = \eta\,\delta\) (derived in chapter 15, section 7). The RF goes through \(h\) periods per revolution, so a delay \(\Delta T_0\) moves the phase by \(\omega_{RF}\Delta T_0 = 2\pi h\,\eta\,\delta\):

\[ \phi_{n+1} = \phi_n + \frac{2\pi h\,\eta}{\beta^2 E}\,\Delta E_{n+1}. \]

These two lines, a kick in energy then a drift in phase, contain all of single-particle longitudinal motion in a ring. Three remarks.

  • The map is area preserving: its Jacobian is a product of two shears of determinant 1, so Liouville’s theorem holds exactly, turn by turn. It is the “standard map” of nonlinear dynamics with a constant torque added.
  • When \(\phi_s\), \(\hat{V}\), \(\eta\) or \(E\) change, they are updated each turn. Codes such as ESME (Fermilab) and BLonD (CERN, Timko et al. 2023) iterate these equations with several harmonics and the beam’s own induced voltage added.
  • \(\Delta T_0/T_0 = \eta\delta\) is first order in \(\delta\). Near transition the next term, \(\alpha_1\) in \(\Delta C/C = \alpha_c\delta + \alpha_1\delta^2\), matters (section 10).

Worked number (LHC, 7 TeV). The revolution period is \(35640/400.789\ \text{MHz} = 88.92\ \mu\)s. Take \(\eta = 3.225\times10^{-4}\) (see section 3) and a particle at \(\delta = 1.13\times10^{-4}\), about one standard deviation of the design bunch. It arrives 3.24 ps later each turn, against a bucket 2.495 ns long. A particle 0.1 rad from the synchronous phase (the zero crossing of the wave, since \(\phi_s = \pi\) here) at 16 MV is kicked by 1.60 MeV a turn, against \(E\) = 7 TeV. Both changes per turn are tiny: the continuous limit is excellent.

Pre-requisite (continued): Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics, the charged-particle Hamiltonian

Pre-requisite. Chapter 1 defined the Hamiltonian as the energy written in terms of the momentum, and chapter 7 used Hamilton’s equations as a flow through phase space. This section derives those equations, shows how to tell whether two given first-order equations come from a Hamiltonian, how to rescale a canonical pair without spoiling it, and what a negative mass means. Section 2 uses all four to write longitudinal motion as \(H(\phi, W_\phi)\), a kinetic term plus a potential, with a “mass” that is negative below transition. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §40 (Hamilton’s equations) and §45 (canonical transformations); Tong, Classical Mechanics, section 10.1; Schwichtenberg, No-Nonsense Classical Mechanics, chapter 5 (Hamiltonian mechanics); and Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 3, sections I and I.1 (the synchrotron Hamiltonian and the “negative mass” of a particle above transition).

Hamilton’s equations from the energy. Chapter 1 defined, for one coordinate \(q\) with Lagrangian \(L(q, \dot q, t)\), the momentum \(p = \partial L/\partial\dot q\) and the Hamiltonian \(H = p\dot q - L\), written as a function of \(q\), \(p\) and \(t\). Make a small change of everything and collect the changes:

\[ dH = \dot q\,dp + p\,d\dot q - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q}dq - \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}d\dot q - \frac{\partial L}{\partial t}dt. \]

The second and fourth terms cancel because \(p = \partial L/\partial\dot q\), and the Euler–Lagrange equation says \(\partial L/\partial q = \dot p\). So \(dH = \dot q\,dp - \dot p\,dq - (\partial L/\partial t)\,dt\). Comparing with \(dH = (\partial H/\partial p)dp + (\partial H/\partial q)dq + (\partial H/\partial t)dt\) term by term,

\[ \dot q = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p}, \qquad \dot p = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q}. \]

One second-order equation has become two first-order ones. A pair \((q, p)\) that obeys them is called a canonical, or conjugate, pair. The product of the units of \(q\) and \(p\) is always an energy times a time, the unit of action (J s, or eV s), because \(p\dot q\) has the units of \(H\).

Recognising a Hamiltonian. Often the two first-order equations come straight from the physics, \(\dot q = f(q, p)\) and \(\dot p = g(q, p)\), and the question is whether some \(H\) produces them. It would need \(\partial H/\partial p = f\) and \(\partial H/\partial q = -g\). Mixed partial derivatives do not depend on the order of differentiation (F1, section 9), so \(\partial^2H/\partial q\,\partial p\) computed both ways must agree: \(\partial f/\partial q = \partial^2H/\partial q\,\partial p = -\partial g/\partial p\). The two can come from one \(H\) only if

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial q} + \frac{\partial g}{\partial p} = 0, \]

which is the zero-divergence condition of Liouville’s theorem. When it holds, \(H\) is found by integrating \(f\) with respect to \(p\) and fixing the leftover function of \(q\) from \(g\). Section 2’s equations have \(\dot\phi\) depending only on \(\Delta E\) and \(\dot{\Delta E}\) only on \(\phi\), so both partial derivatives vanish separately and a Hamiltonian exists. Integrating, in the variables \((\phi, \Delta E)\),

\[ H_1(\phi, \Delta E) = \frac{h\omega_0\eta}{2\beta^2E}\Delta E^2 + \frac{q\hat V\omega_0}{2\pi}\Big[\cos\phi - \cos\phi_s + (\phi - \phi_s)\sin\phi_s\Big], \]

as you can check by differentiating. But \(\phi\,\Delta E\) has units of eV, not eV s, and \(H_1\) has units of eV/s. The equations are right; the scale of the variables is awkward.

Rescaling a pair. Suppose \((q, p)\) obey Hamilton’s equations with \(H\). Let \(P = \lambda p\) with \(\lambda\) a constant. Then \(\partial H/\partial p = \lambda\,\partial H/\partial P\), so

\[ \dot q = \frac{\partial(\lambda H)}{\partial P}, \qquad \dot P = \lambda\dot p = -\frac{\partial(\lambda H)}{\partial q}: \]

\((q, P)\) is a canonical pair with Hamiltonian \(\lambda H\). The same holds for scaling \(q\) instead. Areas in the new plane are \(\lambda\) times areas in the old. Section 2 takes \(\lambda = 1/(h\omega_0)\), \(W_\phi = \Delta E/(h\omega_0)\) in eV s: the Hamiltonian becomes \(H = H_1/(h\omega_0)\), in eV, and areas in \((\phi, W_\phi)\) are in eV s. Scaling the other variable instead, the arrival time \(\tau = (\phi - \phi_s)/(h\omega_0)\) (s) with \(\Delta E\) also has Hamiltonian \(H_1/(h\omega_0)\), so \((\tau, \Delta E)\) is canonical and \(d\tau\,d\Delta E = d\phi\,dW_\phi\), as section 2 states. Changing the time variable works the same way: counting time in turns \(n = t/T_0\) multiplies the Hamiltonian by \(T_0\).

Kinetic plus potential, and a negative mass. Many Hamiltonians have the form

\[ H = \frac{p^2}{2M} + U(q). \]

Then \(\dot q = p/M\) and \(\dot p = -U'(q)\), so \(M\ddot q = -U'(q)\): Newton’s law with mass \(M\) and potential \(U\), and \(H\) is the kinetic plus the potential energy (F3, section 5). Equilibria are where \(U' = 0\). Near one, at \(q_0\), \(M\,\Delta\ddot q = -U''(q_0)\,\Delta q\), an oscillation with

\[ \Omega^2 = \frac{U''(q_0)}{M} \]

if \(U''/M > 0\), and exponential growth if \(U''/M < 0\) (the test of chapter 7’s oscillator). With \(M > 0\) a valley of \(U\) is stable, as for a ball. With \(M < 0\) the acceleration points against the force, so a ball pushed downhill climbs back up: a hilltop is stable and a valley is not.

Section 2’s Hamiltonian has this form with

\[ M = \frac{\beta^2E}{h^2\omega_0^2\eta}\ \ (\text{eV s}^2), \qquad U(\phi) = \frac{q\hat V}{2\pi h}\Big[\cos\phi - \cos\phi_s + (\phi - \phi_s)\sin\phi_s\Big]\ \ (\text{eV}), \]

\[ U''(\phi) = -\frac{q\hat V}{2\pi h}\cos\phi. \]

The sign of \(M\) is the sign of \(\eta\). Below transition \(\eta < 0\): a particle with more energy goes round faster, arrives earlier, and so its phase, which counts lateness, decreases. A positive “momentum” moves the coordinate backwards: a negative mass. Lee uses the same phrase for the opposite case, above transition, because he thinks of the particle’s position round the ring, which increases as the phase decreases. The sign of \(M\) on its own depends only on which way the coordinate is counted. The physics is in the sign of \(U''/M\), and that does not depend on the convention.

At work (LHC, 7 TeV). \(\omega_0 = 70\,657\) rad/s, so \(M = 7\times10^{12}/(35640^2\times70\,657^2\times3.225\times10^{-4}) = 3.42\times10^{-3}\) eV s², positive. At \(\phi_s = \pi\), \(U'' = +q\hat V/(2\pi h) = 16\times10^6/(2\pi\times35640) = 71.45\) eV: a valley. \(\Omega^2 = 71.45/3.42\times10^{-3} = 2.09\times10^4\) s⁻², and \(\Omega/2\pi = 23.0\) Hz, the synchrotron frequency of section 3.

At work (PS, 30 ms before transition). At \(\gamma = \gamma_t - 1.32 = 4.76\), \(\eta = -0.0171\), \(f_0 = 466.5\) kHz, \(h = 21\), 200 kV and \(\phi_s = 26.1°\) (section 6). Then \(M = -6.60\times10^{-5}\) eV s², negative, and \(U''(\phi_s) = -(2\times10^5/2\pi\times21)\cos26.1° = -1361\) eV: the synchronous phase sits on a hilltop of \(U\). The ratio is positive, \(\Omega^2 = 2.06\times10^7\) s⁻², and the bunch oscillates at 723 Hz. At transition \(M \to \infty\): the particle is infinitely heavy and the restoring force can no longer move it, which is section 10’s freezing of the motion.

2. The continuous limit and the Hamiltonian

When the changes per turn are small, replace \(n\) by \(t/T_0\). A change per turn, divided by the time of a turn, is a rate: \((\phi_{n+1} - \phi_n)/T_0 \approx d\phi/dt\), and the same for \(\Delta E\). Dividing section 1’s two lines by \(T_0 = 2\pi/\omega_0\):

\[ \frac{d\phi}{dt} = \frac{h\omega_0\eta}{\beta^2E}\,\Delta E, \qquad \frac{d\,\Delta E}{dt} = \frac{q\hat{V}\omega_0}{2\pi}\left(\sin\phi - \sin\phi_s\right). \]

This holds while \(Q_s \ll 1\), true in every ring of this chapter. Introduce \(W_\phi = \Delta E/(h\omega_0)\), in eV s (subscripted because \(W\) alone is kinetic energy in this course). Then \((\phi, W_\phi)\) is a canonical pair, with time \(t\) as the independent variable and Hamiltonian

\[ H(\phi, W_\phi) = \frac{h^2\omega_0^2\eta}{2\beta^2E}\,W_\phi^2 + \frac{q\hat{V}}{2\pi h}\Big[\cos\phi - \cos\phi_s + (\phi - \phi_s)\sin\phi_s\Big]. \]

Check: \(\dot\phi = \partial H/\partial W_\phi\) gives the first equation, and \(\dot W_\phi = -\partial H/\partial\phi = (q\hat{V}/2\pi h)(\sin\phi - \sin\phi_s)\) the second. The phase measures the arrival time \(\tau\) of the particle at the cavity relative to the synchronous one: \(\phi - \phi_s = h\omega_0\tau\). So \(d\phi\,dW_\phi = d\tau\,d(\Delta E)\): area in the \((\phi, W_\phi)\) plane is area in the plane of arrival time and energy, measured in eV s. That is why longitudinal emittance is quoted in eV s.

The first term is a kinetic energy whose “mass” \(\beta^2E/(h^2\omega_0^2\eta)\) is negative below transition; the second is a potential, a cosine well tilted by the accelerating term. It is a pendulum with a constant torque (Tong, Classical Mechanics, section 10.1, draws the pendulum’s phase portrait and its separatrix; Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, section 40 for Hamilton’s equations, section 46 for Liouville’s theorem).

Pre-requisite (continued): Special functions and integrals (Bessel, Gaussian, error function)

Pre-requisite. Chapter 2 introduced the special functions the course uses, and chapter 12’s pendulum met one more: the complete elliptic integral of the first kind, \(K(k)\), which gives a pendulum’s period. This section shows how to evaluate it by hand: its power series for small \(k\), and its logarithm as \(k\) approaches 1. Section 3 uses the series to get \(\Omega(\hat\phi)/\Omega_s \approx 1 - \hat\phi^2/16\) and the logarithm to see why particles near the separatrix almost stop. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §11, problem 1 (the pendulum’s period as an elliptic integral, and its first correction); Tong, Classical Mechanics, section 2.1 (the pendulum’s period as an elliptic integral, and its small-amplitude limit); and Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 3, section II.5 (the period and action of large-amplitude synchrotron motion).

The function. For \(0 \le k < 1\),

\[ K(k) = \int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\xi}{\sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2\xi}} . \]

The number \(k\) is the modulus. Chapter 12 showed that a pendulum of small-amplitude angular frequency \(\Omega_s\) swinging to an amplitude \(\hat\phi\) has period \(T = (4/\Omega_s)K(\sin(\hat\phi/2))\), so its angular frequency is

\[ \frac{\Omega(\hat\phi)}{\Omega_s} = \frac{\pi}{2K(\sin(\hat\phi/2))} . \]

\(K(0) = \pi/2\) gives back \(\Omega = \Omega_s\). The integral has no expression in elementary functions, but both its ends are easy.

Small \(k\): a power series. The binomial series (F1, section 8), \((1 + x)^n = 1 + nx + \tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}x^2 + \dots\), with \(n = -\tfrac12\) and \(x\) replaced by \(-x\), gives \((1 - x)^{-1/2} = 1 + \tfrac12x + \tfrac38x^2 + \tfrac5{16}x^3 + \dots\), which converges for \(|x| < 1\). Its coefficients are \(c_n = \frac{1\cdot3\cdots(2n-1)}{2\cdot4\cdots2n}\). Put \(x = k^2\sin^2\xi\), which is below 1 throughout, and integrate term by term. Each term needs

\[ \int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^{2n}\xi\,d\xi = \frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{1\cdot3\cdots(2n-1)}{2\cdot4\cdots2n} = \frac{\pi}{2}\,c_n . \]

(For \(n = 1\): the average of \(\sin^2\) over a quarter period is \(\tfrac12\), so the integral is \(\pi/4\). The general case follows by integrating by parts, which lowers the power by two and brings out the factor \((2n - 1)/2n\).) So the same number appears twice and

\[ K(k) = \frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n^2k^{2n} = \frac{\pi}{2}\left(1 + \frac{k^2}{4} + \frac{9k^4}{64} + \frac{25k^6}{256} + \dots\right). \]

Then, for small \(k\), \(\pi/(2K) \approx 1 - k^2/4\). With \(k^2 = \sin^2(\hat\phi/2) \approx \hat\phi^2/4\),

\[ \frac{\Omega(\hat\phi)}{\Omega_s} \approx 1 - \frac{\hat\phi^2}{16}, \]

section 3’s result: the frequency falls with the square of the amplitude, by 1.7 per cent at 30° and 15 per cent at 90°.

\(k\) near 1: a logarithm. At \(k = 1\) the integrand becomes \(1/\cos\xi\), which is infinite at \(\xi = \pi/2\) and whose integral diverges. For \(k\) just below 1 write \(k' = \sqrt{1 - k^2}\), the complementary modulus; for a pendulum \(k' = \cos(\hat\phi/2)\). Put \(u = \pi/2 - \xi\), so that \(1 - k^2\sin^2\xi = 1 - k^2\cos^2u\). Split the range at some small \(u = a\), with \(k' \ll a \ll 1\):

  • For \(u < a\), \(1 - k^2\cos^2u \approx 1 - k^2(1 - u^2) \approx k'^2 + u^2\) (using \(\cos u \approx 1 - u^2/2\), so \(\cos^2u \approx 1 - u^2\), and \(k^2u^2 \approx u^2\)). The integral \(\int_0^a du/\sqrt{k'^2 + u^2}\) is done with the hyperbolic functions \(\sinh w = (e^w - e^{-w})/2\) and \(\cosh w = (e^w + e^{-w})/2\), which obey \(\cosh^2w - \sinh^2w = 1\) and \(d(\sinh w)/dw = \cosh w\). Put \(u = k'\sinh w\): then \(du = k'\cosh w\,dw\) and \(\sqrt{k'^2 + u^2} = k'\cosh w\), so the integrand is just \(dw\) and the integral is \(w = \sinh^{-1}(a/k')\). Solving \(x = (e^w - e^{-w})/2\) for \(w\) gives \(\sinh^{-1}x = \ln(x + \sqrt{x^2 + 1})\), which for large \(x\) is \(\ln 2x\). Here \(a/k'\) is large, so the integral is \(\approx \ln(2a/k')\).
  • For \(u > a\) the small \(k'\) hardly matters; with \(k = 1\) the integrand is \(1/\sin u\). Its integral is \(\ln\tan(u/2)\), as differentiating shows: \(\tfrac{d}{du}\ln\tan(u/2) = \tfrac{1}{2\cos^2(u/2)\tan(u/2)} = \tfrac{1}{2\sin(u/2)\cos(u/2)} = \tfrac{1}{\sin u}\). So \(\int_a^{\pi/2}du/\sin u = \ln\tan(\pi/4) - \ln\tan(a/2) = \ln\cot(a/2) \approx \ln(2/a)\), since \(\tan(\pi/4) = 1\) and \(\tan(a/2) \approx a/2\) for small \(a\).

The arbitrary \(a\) cancels in the sum:

\[ K(k) \approx \ln\frac{4}{k'}, \qquad \frac{\Omega(\hat\phi)}{\Omega_s} \approx \frac{\pi}{2\ln\left(4/\cos(\hat\phi/2)\right)} . \]

The frequency goes to zero at the separatrix, but only logarithmically: each tenfold step closer to it (in \(k'\)) adds \(\ln10 = 2.30\) to \(K\).

3. Small oscillations and the synchrotron tune

Differentiate the first equation and use the second, with \(\eta\), \(E\), \(\hat{V}\) constant over an oscillation:

\[ \frac{d^2\phi}{dt^2} = \frac{h\omega_0^2\eta\,q\hat{V}}{2\pi\beta^2E}\left(\sin\phi - \sin\phi_s\right). \]

For \(\phi = \phi_s + \Delta\phi\) with \(\Delta\phi\) small, \(\sin\phi - \sin\phi_s \approx \cos\phi_s\,\Delta\phi\) (the small-change rule: the change of \(\sin\) is its slope \(\cos\phi_s\) times the step), and the equation is that of simple harmonic motion, \(\ddot x = -\Omega^2x\):

\[ \ddot{\Delta\phi} + \Omega_s^2\,\Delta\phi = 0, \qquad \Omega_s^2 = -\frac{h\omega_0^2\,\eta\,q\hat{V}\cos\phi_s}{2\pi\beta^2E}, \qquad Q_s = \frac{\Omega_s}{\omega_0}. \]

The motion is an oscillation when \(\eta\cos\phi_s < 0\): below transition (\(\eta < 0\)) the stable phase is on the rising side, \(0 < \phi_s < \pi/2\); above it (\(\eta > 0\)), on the falling side, \(\pi/2 < \phi_s < \pi\). \(\Omega_s\) grows as \(\sqrt{\hat{V}}\), as \(\sqrt{h}\) and as \(\sqrt{|\eta|}\), and falls as \(1/\sqrt{E}\).

4. Fixed points and the separatrix

The fixed points of \(H\) are where \(\dot\phi = \dot W_\phi = 0\): \(W_\phi = 0\) and \(\sin\phi = \sin\phi_s\), so \(\phi = \phi_s\) and \(\phi = \pi - \phi_s\). The first is the stable centre (an elliptic point: nearby orbits are ellipses round it). The second is unstable (a hyperbolic point: nearby orbits are hyperbolas, which carry particles away). By the test of the Hamiltonian section, what decides is the sign of \(U''/M\): it is negative at \(\pi - \phi_s\), so \(U/M\) has a maximum there. With a positive mass (above transition) \(U\) itself has a hilltop at the unstable point; with the negative mass below transition \(U\) has a valley there and a hilltop at \(\phi_s\), as the PS example of that section found. The trajectory through the unstable point is the separatrix. Inside it, the bucket, particles oscillate; outside, they slip in phase without limit and are not accelerated. The word “bucket” was already in use in 1956, in Symon and Sessler’s paper on RF acceleration in fixed-field machines.

Take below transition, \(0 \le \phi_s < \pi/2\). Since \(H\) is conserved, every point of the separatrix has the value of \(H\) at the unstable point, \((\pi - \phi_s, 0)\), which is just the potential term there. Below transition \(\eta = -|\eta|\), so the kinetic term is \(-(h^2\omega_0^2|\eta|/2\beta^2E)W_\phi^2\); moving it to the other side, the kinetic term at \(\phi\) equals the potential at \(\phi\) minus the potential at \(\pi - \phi_s\). In the bracket, \(\cos(\pi - \phi_s) = -\cos\phi_s\), and the linear term \((\phi - \phi_s) - (\pi - 2\phi_s) = -(\pi - \phi - \phi_s)\). This gives

\[ \frac{h^2\omega_0^2|\eta|}{2\beta^2E}W_\phi^2 = \frac{q\hat{V}}{2\pi h}\Big[\cos\phi + \cos\phi_s - (\pi - \phi - \phi_s)\sin\phi_s\Big], \]

or, in energy, multiplying by \(h^2\omega_0^2\) (since \(\Delta E = h\omega_0W_\phi\)) and by \(2\beta^2E/|\eta|\),

\[ \Delta E_{sep}(\phi)^2 = \frac{q\hat{V}\beta^2E}{\pi h|\eta|}\Big[\cos\phi + \cos\phi_s - (\pi - \phi - \phi_s)\sin\phi_s\Big]. \]

Above transition, replace \(\phi_s\) by \(\pi - \phi_s\) and reflect the picture in phase; the results for height and area are the same.

5. The stationary bucket: height and area

With \(\phi_s = 0\) the bracket is \(1 + \cos\phi = 2\cos^2(\phi/2)\), and

\[ \Delta E_{sep}(\phi) = \Delta E_{bucket}\cos\frac{\phi}{2}, \qquad \Delta E_{bucket} = \sqrt{\frac{2q\hat{V}\beta^2E}{\pi h|\eta|}}. \]

The bucket runs from \(\phi = -\pi\) to \(\pi\), one whole RF period. Its area in the \((\phi, \Delta E)\) plane is twice the area under the upper half (the lower half is its mirror image): \(2\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\Delta E_{bucket}\cos(\phi/2)\,d\phi = 2\Delta E_{bucket}\big[2\sin(\phi/2)\big]_{-\pi}^{\pi} = 2\Delta E_{bucket}(2 + 2) = 8\,\Delta E_{bucket}\). Divide by \(h\omega_0\) to convert phase to time:

\[ A_{bucket} = \frac{8\,\Delta E_{bucket}}{h\omega_0} = \frac{8}{h\omega_0}\sqrt{\frac{2q\hat{V}\beta^2E}{\pi h|\eta|}}\quad\text{(eV s, with } \Delta E \text{ in eV)}. \]

The height grows only as \(\sqrt{\hat{V}}\); a higher harmonic gives a shorter and lower bucket, its area falling as \(h^{-3/2}\).

Worked numbers (LHC). At 450 GeV and 8 MV: \(\Delta E_{bucket}\) = 450 MeV, \(\delta\) = \(1.0\times10^{-3}\), area 1.43 eV s. At 7 TeV and 16 MV: 2.49 GeV, \(\delta = 3.56\times10^{-4}\), 7.91 eV s. Both areas are the design table’s (Evans and Bryant 2008). A 1.0 eV s bunch fills 70 per cent of the injection bucket; a 2.5 eV s bunch 32 per cent of the top-energy bucket (Figure 19.3).

6. The moving bucket

With \(\phi_s \neq 0\) the potential is tilted. The separatrix still passes through \(\pi - \phi_s\), and closes on the other side at the phase \(\phi_2\) where the bracket vanishes again. The height, at \(\phi = \phi_s\), follows by putting \(\phi = \phi_s\) in section 4’s \(\Delta E_{sep}^2\): the bracket becomes \(2\cos\phi_s - (\pi - 2\phi_s)\sin\phi_s = 2[\cos\phi_s - (\pi/2 - \phi_s)\sin\phi_s]\), and the factor 2 joins the one in \(\Delta E_{bucket}^2\):

\[ \Delta E_{max} = \Delta E_{bucket}\,Y(\phi_s), \qquad Y(\phi_s) = \sqrt{\cos\phi_s - \left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \phi_s\right)\sin\phi_s}. \]

Pre-requisite (continued): Distribution functions, Vlasov equation, beam temperature

Pre-requisite. Chapter 7 described a beam by a density in phase space, carried along by the Vlasov equation, and showed that nonlinear forces keep its true area while its rms area grows. This section adds three things for a beam held in an RF bucket: which distributions stay still (any function of the Hamiltonian); how much a mismatched bunch’s emittance grows once it has smeared out, \((r + 1/r)/2\); and the equilibrium an electron bunch settles into, of the Boltzmann form \(e^{-H/H_0}\), from which its length follows. Section 7 uses the first, section 9 the second, and section 11 the third, for MAX IV’s bunches lengthened by a harmonic cavity. Taught from Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 3, section VIII.4 (an equilibrium distribution must be a function of the Hamiltonian) and chapter 5, section I (the longitudinal Vlasov equation and its steady solutions \(f(H)\), in the setting of the free-electron laser); Tong, Classical Mechanics, section 10.2 (Liouville’s equation and its stationary solutions); and the Handbook of Accelerator Physics and Engineering, section 2.4.9 (the potential-well effect and the Haissinski equilibrium of an electron bunch).

Which distributions stay still. Let \(f(q, p, t)\) be the density of particles in a plane of a canonical pair. Chapter 7’s Vlasov equation says \(f\) is constant along each particle’s path:

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \dot q\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial q} + \dot p\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial p} = 0 . \]

A distribution that does not change, \(\partial f/\partial t = 0\), must therefore be constant along every trajectory. Try \(f = F(H)\) for any function \(F\). Then \(\partial f/\partial q = F'(H)\,\partial H/\partial q = -F'\dot p\) and \(\partial f/\partial p = F'\,\partial H/\partial p = F'\dot q\), and the two remaining terms are \(\dot q(-F'\dot p) + \dot p(F'\dot q) = 0\). Any function of the Hamiltonian is stationary. Inside a bucket the trajectories are the closed curves of constant \(H\), so the converse holds too: a stationary distribution must be constant on each curve, which is to say a function of \(H\). Such a distribution is called matched: its contours of equal density are the bucket’s own trajectories, and the motion carries each contour into itself.

For a small bunch, \(H\) is quadratic. In the normalised coordinates \(u = \Delta\phi\) and \(v = \Delta\dot\phi/\Omega_s\), in which small oscillations are circles, a matched distribution is round. A Gaussian of rms width \(\sigma_u\) in \(u\) must have the same width in \(v\); written in time and energy, that is section 7’s relation \(\sigma_\delta = \Omega_s\sigma_t/|\eta|\). In a large bunch \(H\) is not quadratic, and a Gaussian in \((\phi, \Delta E)\) is no longer a function of \(H\): even with the right ratio of widths it reshapes a little before it settles.

A mismatched bunch, smeared out. Now inject a bunch that is not matched: in \((u, v)\) an upright ellipse with rms widths \(\sigma_u^2 = \varepsilon r\) and \(\sigma_v^2 = \varepsilon/r\), so that its rms emittance is \(\sigma_u\sigma_v = \varepsilon\) and \(r\) measures the mismatch (\(r = 1\) is matched). In a perfectly linear bucket every particle turns at \(\Omega_s\), the ellipse rotates rigidly, and the bunch length swings between \(\sqrt{\varepsilon r}\) and \(\sqrt{\varepsilon/r}\) twice per synchrotron period: a quadrupole oscillation. In a real bucket the frequency falls with amplitude (section 3), so outer particles fall behind inner ones and the ellipse winds into a spiral. After many periods the particles’ angles round the centre are spread evenly (Figure 19.5). Each particle has kept its own radius, so the mean of \(u^2 + v^2\) is still \(\varepsilon r + \varepsilon/r\). But now it is shared equally between \(u\) and \(v\), with no correlation:

\[ \sigma_u^2 = \sigma_v^2 = \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(r + \frac1r\right), \qquad \frac{\varepsilon_{final}}{\varepsilon} = \frac12\left(r + \frac1r\right). \]

(Each of \(\sigma_u^2\) and \(\sigma_v^2\) is half the mean of \(u^2 + v^2\), because for a particle at a random angle \(\theta\) on a circle of radius \(a\), the averages of \(a^2\cos^2\theta\) and \(a^2\sin^2\theta\) are both \(a^2/2\). The new rms emittance \(\sigma_u\sigma_v\) is then \(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}(r + 1/r)\).) Sampling a million particles with \(r = \sqrt2\) and random angles gives 1.0609, against \(1.0607\) from the formula.

This is the difference between fine-grained and coarse-grained density. Liouville’s theorem still holds exactly: each particle’s density is carried unchanged, and the area the particles truly occupy is the same. But it is now drawn out into a spiral of thin filaments wound inside a larger ellipse. Once the filaments are finer than any measurement, or than the next machine’s acceptance can resolve, the bunch behaves as if it filled the larger ellipse. The growth cannot be undone in practice.

At work. The ratio a bunch needs, \(\sigma_E/\sigma_t\), grows as \(\Omega_s \propto \sqrt{\hat V}\) (section 7). A bunch made for one voltage and captured at twice that voltage has \(r = \sqrt2\): its emittance grows by 6.1 per cent. At four times the voltage, \(r = 2\) and the growth is 25 per cent, which section 9’s tracking confirmed for a short bunch (1.245).

The equilibrium of an electron bunch. A proton bunch has no equilibrium of its own: any \(F(H)\) stays still, and which one it has depends on its history. An electron bunch forgets its history. Radiation damps every energy offset towards zero, and the random emission of photons kicks it; the balance makes the energy distribution a Gaussian whose width \(\sigma_E\) is set by the ring (chapter 20), the same at every phase in the bunch. Put that together with stationarity. Write the Hamiltonian in arrival time \(\tau\) (s) and energy offset \(\Delta E\) (eV), the canonical pair of section 2:

\[ H = \frac{A}{2}\Delta E^2 + U(\tau), \qquad A = \frac{\eta}{\beta^2E}, \qquad U(\tau) = \frac{1}{T_0}\int_0^\tau\Big[U_0 - qV(\tau')\Big]d\tau' , \]

with \(U_0\) the energy radiated per turn (eV) and \(V(\tau)\) the total RF voltage met at arrival time \(\tau\). (Hamilton’s equations give \(\dot\tau = \eta\Delta E/(\beta^2E)\), section 2’s phase equation, and \(\dot{\Delta E} = [qV(\tau) - U_0]/T_0\), the net energy gained per turn spread over the turn.) The distribution must be some \(F(H)\), and at every \(\tau\) it must be a Gaussian in \(\Delta E\) of the same width. A function of a sum that splits into a product of a function of each part is an exponential. (Suppose \(F(x + y) = g(x)\,k(y)\) for all \(x\) and \(y\). Differentiate with respect to \(x\) and with respect to \(y\): both give \(F'(x + y)\), so \(g'(x)k(y) = g(x)k'(y)\), and \(g'/g = k'/k\). A function of \(x\) alone equal to a function of \(y\) alone is a constant, \(-1/H_0\) say; then \(g' = -g/H_0\), whose solution is \(g \propto e^{-x/H_0}\) (F1, section 10), and so \(F(x + y) \propto e^{-(x + y)/H_0}\).) So

\[ f(\tau, \Delta E) \propto e^{-H/H_0} = e^{-A\Delta E^2/2H_0}\,e^{-U(\tau)/H_0}, \qquad H_0 = A\,\sigma_E^2 . \]

(This needs \(A > 0\), which holds because electron rings run far above transition, \(\eta > 0\).) This is Boltzmann’s factor (F6, section 13; chapter 2) with \(H_0\) in the place of \(k_BT\). The width follows by matching the energy factor to a Gaussian, \(e^{-\Delta E^2/2\sigma_E^2}\): \(A/2H_0 = 1/2\sigma_E^2\), so \(H_0 = A\sigma_E^2\). Integrating over \(\Delta E\) leaves the line density

\[ \rho(\tau) \propto \exp\left(-\frac{U(\tau)}{H_0}\right). \]

The shape of the bunch is the shape of the well, seen through an exponential. (When the bunch’s own induced voltage is added to \(V\), \(U\) depends on \(\rho\) and the equation must be solved for itself; that is Haissinski’s equation of 1973, in the Handbook’s section 2.4.9.)

Check with a linear well. Near the centre, \(qV(\tau) - U_0 \approx qV'\tau\) with \(V' < 0\) on the falling slope used above transition, so \(U = (-qV'/2T_0)\tau^2\) and \(\rho\) is a Gaussian with \(\sigma_t^2 = H_0T_0/(-qV')\). Since \(\Omega_s^2 = A(-qV')/T_0\) for this Hamiltonian, \(\sigma_t = A\sigma_E/\Omega_s = \eta\sigma_\delta/\Omega_s\): section 7’s relation again. For MAX IV with the main RF alone this gives \(\sigma_z\) = 10.06 mm.

A flat well. A third-harmonic cavity can be set so that the total voltage meets three conditions at the bunch centre: \(V(0) = U_0/q\), \(V'(0) = 0\) and \(V''(0) = 0\). Then \(qV - U_0 \approx qV'''\tau^3/6\), and

\[ U(\tau) = a\,\tau^4, \qquad a = -\frac{qV'''}{24\,T_0} . \]

The bunch is no longer Gaussian but flat-topped, \(\rho \propto e^{-a\tau^4/H_0}\). Its rms length needs one more special function.

The gamma function. For \(x > 0\),

\[ \Gamma(x) = \int_0^\infty w^{x-1}e^{-w}\,dw . \]

Integrating by parts gives \(\Gamma(x + 1) = x\,\Gamma(x)\), and \(\Gamma(1) = 1\), so for whole numbers \(\Gamma(n + 1) = n!\): it extends the factorial to fractions. Two values needed here, from tables or a computer, are \(\Gamma(1/4) = 3.6256\) and \(\Gamma(3/4) = 1.2254\); and \(\Gamma(1/2) = \sqrt\pi\), which is the Gaussian integral of chapter 2 in disguise. Integrals of \(e^{-u^4}\) reduce to it: put \(w = u^4\), so \(u = w^{1/4}\) and \(du = \tfrac14w^{-3/4}dw\), and

\[ \int_0^\infty u^{s-1}e^{-u^4}du = \frac14\int_0^\infty w^{(s-1)/4}\,w^{-3/4}e^{-w}dw = \frac14\int_0^\infty w^{s/4 - 1}e^{-w}dw = \frac{\Gamma(s/4)}{4} . \]

The rms length is the square root of the mean of \(\tau^2\), the integral of \(\tau^2\rho\) divided by the integral of \(\rho\). With \(u = (a/H_0)^{1/4}\tau\) the top is the case \(s = 3\) and the bottom \(s = 1\), so \(\langle u^2\rangle = \Gamma(3/4)/\Gamma(1/4)\) and

\[ \sigma_t = \sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(3/4)}{\Gamma(1/4)}}\left(\frac{H_0}{a}\right)^{1/4}, \qquad \frac{\Gamma(3/4)}{\Gamma(1/4)} = 0.3380 . \]

Now the length grows as \(\sigma_E^{1/2}\) instead of \(\sigma_E\), and falls as the inverse fourth root of the third derivative \(V'''\) rather than the inverse square root of the slope \(V'\).

At work (MAX IV). Write \(V = V_1\sin(\omega\tau + \phi_1) + V_3\sin(3\omega\tau + \phi_3)\) with \(V_1\) = 1.6 MV, \(\omega = 2\pi\times99.931\) MHz and \(U_0\) = 856 keV. The three conditions are \(V_1\sin\phi_1 + V_3\sin\phi_3 = 856\) kV, \(V_1\cos\phi_1 + 3V_3\cos\phi_3 = 0\) and \(V_1\sin\phi_1 + 9V_3\sin\phi_3 = 0\). The third gives \(V_3\sin\phi_3 = -V_1\sin\phi_1/9\), and putting that into the first, \(\tfrac89V_1\sin\phi_1 = 856\) kV; the second gives \(V_3\cos\phi_3 = -V_1\cos\phi_1/3\); and \(V_3 = \sqrt{(V_3\sin\phi_3)^2 + (V_3\cos\phi_3)^2}\). They give \(\sin\phi_1 = 9U_0/(8qV_1) = 0.602\), \(\phi_1 = 143.0°\) (the falling side, above transition), \(V_3\) = 439 kV, and \(V''' = -\omega^3(V_1\cos\phi_1 + 27V_3\cos\phi_3) = 8\omega^3V_1\cos\phi_1 = -2.53\times10^{33}\) V/s³. With \(T_0\) = 1.761 µs, \(a = 5.99\times10^{37}\) eV/s⁴. The energy spread \(\sigma_\delta = 0.769\times10^{-3}\) at 3 GeV is \(\sigma_E\) = 2.307 MeV, and \(\eta = 3.06\times10^{-4}\), so \(H_0 = \eta\sigma_E^2/E = 0.543\) eV. Then \((H_0/a)^{1/4} = 309\) ps, \(\sigma_t = 179\) ps and \(\sigma_z = c\sigma_t\) = 53.8 mm. Using the full potential, not just its \(\tau^4\) term, gives 54.0 mm, section 11’s number: the bunch is 5.4 times longer than with the main RF alone.

7. Bunch length, energy spread and longitudinal emittance

A small bunch matched to the bucket fills an ellipse in the plane of arrival time \(t\) and \(\Delta E\). From \(\dot\phi = (h\omega_0\eta/\beta^2E)\Delta E\) and harmonic motion of amplitude \(\hat\phi\) at \(\Omega_s\): if \(\Delta\phi = \hat\phi\cos\Omega_st\), its rate \(\dot\phi\) swings between \(\pm\hat\phi\,\Omega_s\), and it is largest where \(\Delta E\) is largest, so \(\hat\phi\,\Omega_s = h\omega_0|\eta|\,\Delta\hat{E}/(\beta^2E)\). With \(\hat t = \hat\phi/(h\omega_0)\) and \(\hat\delta = \Delta\hat{E}/(\beta^2E)\):

\[ \hat\delta = \frac{\Omega_s}{|\eta|}\,\hat t, \qquad \sigma_\delta = \frac{\Omega_s\,\sigma_t}{|\eta|}, \qquad \sigma_z = \beta c\,\sigma_t = \frac{\beta c\,|\eta|\,\sigma_\delta}{\Omega_s}. \]

This one relation is used in two directions. In a proton ring the emittance is given (it was made at the source and in the injectors) and the voltage sets the ratio of length to spread. In an electron ring \(\sigma_\delta\) is fixed by synchrotron radiation (chapter 20) and the voltage sets the length.

Longitudinal emittance. The area of the ellipse is \(\pi\,\Delta\hat{E}\,\hat t\), in eV s. Conventions differ in what \(\hat{}\) means. CERN’s hadron machines quote the area of the ellipse at two standard deviations, \(\varepsilon_l = \pi(2\sigma_E)(2\sigma_t) = 4\pi\sigma_E\sigma_t\), and call it the “bunch area”; the bunch length is quoted as \(4\sigma_t\). Electron machines quote \(\sigma_z\) and \(\sigma_\delta\) instead.

Worked numbers (LHC). At 7 TeV, 16 MV and 2.5 eV s, the small-amplitude formulas give \(4\sigma_t\) = 1.007 ns and \(2\sigma_E/E = 2.26\times10^{-4}\); the design table gives 1.06 ns and \(2.2\times10^{-4}\). At 450 GeV, 8 MV and 1.0 eV s they give 1.50 ns, against 1.71 ns in the table: the bunch fills 70 per cent of the bucket and the nonlinear part of the well (section 3) lengthens it.

Worked numbers (MAX IV). With the bare-lattice \(\sigma_\delta = 0.769\times10^{-3}\) (the value for the ring’s own magnets alone, before the undulators, the rows of alternating magnets that make the users’ light, add radiation of their own; chapter 20), \(\alpha_c = 3.06\times10^{-4}\) and \(\Omega_s/2\pi\) = 1.12 kHz, \(\sigma_z\) = 10.06 mm, \(\sigma_t\) = 33.6 ps. Tavares et al. give a natural bunch length of 10.1 mm.

Pre-requisite (continued): Action-angle variables

Pre-requisite. The action of an oscillation is the phase-space area its orbit encloses divided by \(2\pi\), and the angle is the phase that advances steadily round the orbit (chapter 16; chapter 12 used them for resonances). For a linear oscillation the frequency does not depend on the action. This section gives the relation that holds for a nonlinear one, such as motion in a bucket: the frequency is the derivative of the energy with respect to the action, \(\Omega = dH/dJ\), or equivalently the period is the derivative of the enclosed area with respect to the energy. Section 8 labels particles by their action, which a slow change keeps; the next section uses each particle’s frequency to choose which particles a band of noise will heat. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §49 (equations 49.8 and 49.9) and §50; Tong, Classical Mechanics, section 10.5; and Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 3, section II.5 (equations 3.57 and 3.58, the period and the action of a synchrotron orbit).

The relation. Take one degree of freedom with a Hamiltonian \(H(q, p)\) that does not depend on time. An orbit of energy \(E\) is a closed curve in the \((q, p)\) plane, and encloses the area

\[ A(E) = \oint p\,dq , \]

taken once round the orbit. Raise the energy by \(dE\). At each \(q\) the curve moves outwards in \(p\) by \((\partial p/\partial E)\,dE\), at fixed \(q\). Since \(E = H(q, p)\), \(\partial E/\partial p = \partial H/\partial p = \dot q\), so \(\partial p/\partial E = 1/\dot q\). Hence

\[ \frac{dA}{dE} = \oint\frac{\partial p}{\partial E}\,dq = \oint\frac{dq}{\dot q} = \oint dt = T , \]

the period of the orbit. (At the turning points \(\dot q = 0\) and the integrand is infinite, but integrably so; the result is exact.) With the action \(J = A/2\pi\) and \(\Omega = 2\pi/T\), this is

\[ \frac{dJ}{dE} = \frac{1}{\Omega}, \qquad \Omega(J) = \frac{dH}{dJ} , \]

Landau and Lifshitz’s equations (49.8) and (49.9). Units: in section 2’s plane, \(A\) and \(J\) are in eV s and \(H\) in eV, so \(dH/dJ\) is in s⁻¹.

Check: the harmonic oscillator. The orbit is an ellipse of semi-axes \(\hat q\) and \(\hat p = M\Omega\hat q\), with \(E = \tfrac12M\Omega^2\hat q^2\). So \(A = \pi\hat q\hat p = 2\pi E/\Omega\), \(dA/dE = 2\pi/\Omega = T\), and \(H = \Omega J\). The frequency is the same for every action: that is what makes an oscillator linear.

In the bucket. For the pendulum, \(A(E)\) grows faster than linearly with \(E\), so \(T = dA/dE\) grows with amplitude. Near the separatrix, orbits of slightly different energy differ greatly in shape: they all pass close to the unstable point, where the particle crawls, and \(dA/dE\) grows without limit while \(A\) itself approaches the finite bucket area. So the frequency goes to zero at the separatrix even though the bucket’s area is finite. I computed the area of pendulum orbits (\(H = p^2/2 + 1 - \cos\theta\), small-amplitude frequency 1) and differentiated numerically. At amplitudes of 30°, 90°, 150° and 170°, \(dA/dH\) = 6.393, 7.416, 11.072 and 15.327, equal to the period \(4K(\sin(\hat\phi/2))\) of chapter 12 to all the digits shown, and the bucket’s area is 16.

Each particle’s frequency, from the area of its orbit. Since a slow change keeps each particle’s action (chapter 7), the action is the natural label for a particle in a bunch, and \(\Omega(J)\) tells how fast that particle oscillates. For the LHC at 7 TeV, with a bucket of 7.91 eV s and \(\Omega_s/2\pi\) = 23.0 Hz at its centre:

orbit area (eV s) 0.5 1.0 2.5 5.0
fraction of bucket 0.063 0.126 0.316 0.632
phase amplitude 32.8° 46.8° 76.2° 115.2°
synchrotron frequency (Hz) 22.5 22.0 20.5 17.2

A 2.5 eV s bunch has its two-sigma contour on the 2.5 eV s orbit. Its particles’ synchrotron frequencies therefore run from 23.0 Hz at the core to about 20.5 Hz at the two-sigma edge. That spread filaments a mismatched bunch (the section above), provides Landau damping (chapter 22), and lets a band of noise be aimed at the particles whose frequencies fall inside it (the next section).

\(\pi/(2K) = 20.0/23.0 = 0.870\) needs \(K = 1.806\), so \(k = \sin(\hat\phi/2) = 0.663\) and \(\hat\phi = 83.1°\). Integrating the area of that orbit gives 0.369 of the bucket, 2.92 eV s: just outside the two-sigma contour of a 2.5 eV s bunch.

Exercise A3. The period of an orbit grows without limit as it approaches the separatrix. How can the area, whose derivative is the period, stay finite?

Answer

\(A(E_{sep}) = A(0) + \int T\,dE\), and near the separatrix \(T\) grows only as \(\ln[1/(E_{sep} - E)]\) (the logarithm of the elliptic integral). A logarithm’s integral is finite: \(\int_0\ln(1/x)\,dx\) converges. So the bucket has a finite area, 8 times the bucket height in \((\phi, \Delta E)\) for a stationary bucket, while a particle on its edge would take for ever to go round.

Pre-requisite (continued): Random signals and noise, spectral density

Pre-requisite. Chapter 8 met noise as the thermal voltage of a resistor, \(\sqrt{4k_BTR\,\delta f}\), growing with the bandwidth. This section gives the general language behind that formula: the autocorrelation of a random signal, its spectral density, the one-sided and two-sided conventions, and phase noise in rad²/Hz and dBc/Hz. Then it derives what noise does to an oscillator: only the part of the spectrum at the oscillator’s own frequency pumps it, at a rate set by the density there. Section 8 uses this for the LHC’s deliberate blow-up of its bunches with band-limited noise, and section 13 for the phase noise the RF loops must keep small. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, Statistical Physics, §122 (spectral resolution of fluctuations); the Handbook of Accelerator Physics and Engineering, section 7.3.1.1 (RF noise and the emittance growth it causes, equation 4); and Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 3, section III (RF phase and voltage modulation).

Autocorrelation. Let \(x(t)\) be a random signal with mean zero whose statistics do not change with time: the jitter of an RF phase, say, in rad. Its autocorrelation is

\[ R(\tau) = \langle x(t)\,x(t + \tau)\rangle , \]

the average over many realisations (a realisation is one possible record of the signal: imagine many identical oscillators, each with its own independent jitter) or over a long time, of the product of the signal with itself a time \(\tau\) later. It does not depend on \(t\), and \(R(0) = \langle x^2\rangle\) is the mean square. \(R\) falls from that value over the correlation time: the time after which the signal has forgotten what it was.

Spectral density. The Fourier transform (F5, section 16; chapter 8) of the autocorrelation is the spectral density,

\[ S(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}R(\tau)\,e^{-i\omega\tau}\,d\tau , \qquad R(\tau) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}S(\omega)\,e^{i\omega\tau}\,\frac{d\omega}{2\pi} . \]

\(R\) is even, so \(S\) is real and even. Put \(\tau = 0\) in the second formula:

\[ \langle x^2\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}S\,\frac{d\omega}{2\pi} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}S\,df = \int_0^\infty S_1(f)\,df , \qquad S_1(f) = 2S(2\pi f) . \]

So \(S_1(f)\,df\) is the share of the mean square carried by frequencies between \(f\) and \(f + df\) (Landau and Lifshitz, §122, derive the same from the Fourier components of the signal). \(S\) is the two-sided density, spread over positive and negative frequencies; \(S_1\) is the one-sided density per hertz that instruments show. The thermal noise \(4k_BTR\) of chapter 8 is a one-sided density of \(v^2\) in V²/Hz, flat in frequency (“white”). A phase noise has \(S_1\) in rad²/Hz. Data sheets quote it as \(\mathcal{L}(f)\) in dBc/Hz: decibels (chapter 8, ten times the base-10 logarithm of a power ratio) relative to the power of the carrier, the clean RF wave whose phase is jittering, in a 1 Hz band. For small phase noise it is \(10\log_{10}[S_1(f)/2]\): \(-100\) dBc/Hz means \(S_1 = 2\times10^{-10}\) rad²/Hz. Noise that is flat at \(S_0\) between \(f_a\) and \(f_b\) and zero elsewhere is band-limited, and its rms is \(\sqrt{S_0(f_b - f_a)}\).

Noise driving an oscillator. If the RF phase jitters by \(\varphi_n(t)\), the bottom of the bucket moves with it, and a particle’s phase obeys

\[ \Delta\ddot\phi + \Omega_s^2\,\Delta\phi = \Omega_s^2\,\varphi_n(t) . \]

Take the general case \(\ddot x + \Omega^2x = g(t)\), with \(g\) random. Combine position and velocity into one complex number, \(\zeta = \dot x + i\Omega x\). Then \(\dot\zeta = \ddot x + i\Omega\dot x = i\Omega\zeta + g\), and \(|\zeta|^2 = \dot x^2 + \Omega^2x^2 = \Omega^2a^2\), where \(a\) is the oscillation’s amplitude. Starting from rest,

\[ \zeta(t) = e^{i\Omega t}\int_0^t e^{-i\Omega t'}\,g(t')\,dt' . \]

(Check by differentiating with the product rule: the first factor gives \(i\Omega\zeta\), and the derivative of the integral is its integrand at \(t\), \(e^{-i\Omega t}g(t)\), which the first factor turns into \(g(t)\). Without \(g\) this is the turning arrow \(e^{i\Omega t}\) of F5, section 3; the integral adds up the pushes, each turned back to the arrow’s starting direction.) The square of the magnitude of a complex number is the number times its conjugate (F5, section 1), and the two factors \(e^{i\Omega t}\) and \(e^{-i\Omega t}\) cancel:

\[ |\zeta|^2 = \int_0^t\!\!\int_0^t e^{-i\Omega(t' - t'')}\,g(t')\,g(t'')\,dt'\,dt'' . \]

Average over realisations; the average of \(g(t')g(t'')\) is the autocorrelation \(R_g(t' - t'')\):

\[ \Omega^2\langle a^2\rangle = \int_0^t\!\!\int_0^t e^{-i\Omega(t' - t'')}\,R_g(t' - t'')\,dt'\,dt'' . \]

\(R_g\) is significant only when \(t'\) and \(t''\) are within a correlation time of each other. If \(t\) is much longer than that, change variable from \(t'\) to the difference \(\tau = t' - t''\). For almost every \(t''\) the range of \(\tau\) then covers the whole correlation time on both sides, so the inner integral is \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\Omega\tau}R_g(\tau)\,d\tau = S_g(\Omega)\), by the definition of the spectral density. It is the same for every \(t''\), and the outer integral over \(t''\) gives a factor \(t\). So \(\langle a^2\rangle\) grows linearly:

\[ \frac{d\langle a^2\rangle}{dt} = \frac{S_g(\Omega)}{\Omega^2} . \]

For RF phase noise, \(g = \Omega_s^2\varphi_n\) and \(S_g = \Omega_s^4S_\varphi\):

\[ \frac{d\langle a^2\rangle}{dt} = \Omega_s^2\,S_\varphi(\Omega_s) = \frac{\Omega_s^2}{2}\,S_{\varphi,1}(f_s) . \]

Only the noise at the oscillator’s own frequency counts. This is resonance again (chapter 7), for a drive that is random: the components near \(\Omega_s\) keep pushing in step for a while, those far from it average away. The amplitude does not grow steadily; it random-walks, and its square grows on average in proportion to time. The Handbook’s equation 4 gives the rate for any amplitude, with \(\sin^2(\hat\phi/2)\) in place of \(\hat\phi^2/4\) and a factor \(\Omega_s^2/4\); it agrees with the result here if its \(S_\varphi\) is read as the two-sided density defined above.

A check by simulation. I gave 1,000 oscillators at 23.0 Hz independent band-limited noise, flat at \(S_1 = 10^{-7}\) rad²/Hz from 20 to 26 Hz (rms 0.77 mrad), for 40 s. The formula predicts \(\langle a^2\rangle = (2\pi\times23)^2\times0.5\times10^{-7}\times40 = 0.0418\) rad²; the simulation gave 0.0426, within its statistical scatter. Moved to 27–33 Hz, the same noise gave \(5.4\times10^{-6}\) rad², eight thousand times less.

At work: how quiet the LHC’s RF must be. At 7 TeV, a 2.5 eV s bunch has \(4\sigma_t\) = 1.007 ns (section 7), so \(\sigma_\phi = 2\pi\times400.79\ \text{MHz}\times0.252\ \text{ns} = 0.634\) rad, and with particles at random points of their oscillation \(\langle a^2\rangle = 2\sigma_\phi^2 = 0.80\) rad². In the linear approximation the emittance is proportional to \(\langle a^2\rangle\). To keep its growth below 1 per cent an hour, \(d\langle a^2\rangle/dt < 2.2\times10^{-6}\) rad²/s, so \(S_{\varphi,1}(23\ \text{Hz}) < 2\times2.2\times10^{-6}/(2\pi\times23)^2 = 2.1\times10^{-10}\) rad²/Hz: about \(-100\) dBc/Hz at 23 Hz from the 400.79 MHz carrier (a spectrum of the RF shows the carrier as a tall line at 400.79 MHz, with the phase noise spread either side of it), and over the whole band of 20.5 to 23 Hz where the bunch’s particles oscillate (previous section).

At work: blowing the bunch up on purpose. Section 8 explains why the LHC wants larger bunches during the ramp. To raise the emittance by 25 per cent in 60 s at 7 TeV needs \(d\langle a^2\rangle/dt = 0.25\times0.80/60 = 3.3\times10^{-3}\) rad²/s, so \(S_{\varphi,1} = 3.2\times10^{-7}\) rad²/Hz across the bunch’s band. Over 2.5 Hz that is an rms phase noise of only 0.9 mrad, 0.05°. Because only the band matters, a small, well-placed noise does the work, and moving the band chooses which particles, by their amplitude, are heated.

Exercise N1. The RF phase noise at 23 Hz from the carrier is \(-110\) dBc/Hz. How fast does the 2.5 eV s bunch above grow?

Answer

\(S_{\varphi,1} = 2\times10^{-11}\) rad²/Hz. \(d\langle a^2\rangle/dt = (2\pi\times23.0)^2\times10^{-11} = 2.09\times10^{-7}\) rad²/s, or \(7.5\times10^{-4}\) rad² an hour: 0.094 per cent of 0.80 an hour. Ten decibels quieter gives ten times slower growth.

8. Slow change: adiabatic invariance and its scaling laws

When the parameters change slowly compared with one synchrotron period, the area enclosed by each particle’s trajectory is an adiabatic invariant (Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, section 49; Tong, Classical Mechanics, section 10.6). The bunch’s emittance in eV s stays constant through the ramp. Garoby measures slowness by the adiabaticity parameter

\[ \varepsilon_{ad} = \frac{1}{\Omega_s^2}\left|\frac{d\Omega_s}{dt}\right|, \]

and counts a process adiabatic when \(\varepsilon_{ad} < 0.1\).

With \(\varepsilon_l = 4\pi\sigma_E\sigma_t\) fixed and \(\sigma_E = \beta^2E\,\Omega_s\sigma_t/|\eta|\) (section 7), the emittance is \(\varepsilon_l = 4\pi\beta^2E\,\Omega_s\sigma_t^2/|\eta|\), so \(\sigma_t^2 \propto |\eta|/(\beta^2E\,\Omega_s)\). Section 3’s \(\Omega_s\) is proportional to \((h\omega_0^2|\eta|\hat V|\cos\phi_s|/\beta^2E)^{1/2}\); dividing by it, one power of \(|\eta|\) and of \(\beta^2E\) is halved, and taking the square root:

\[ \sigma_t \propto \left(\frac{|\eta|}{\beta^2E\,\hat{V}\,h\,|\cos\phi_s|\,\omega_0^2}\right)^{1/4}, \qquad \sigma_E \propto \left(\frac{\beta^2E\,\hat{V}\,h\,|\cos\phi_s|\,\omega_0^2}{|\eta|}\right)^{1/4}. \]

The factor \(\omega_0^2\) is constant once \(\beta \approx 1\). The bunch then shortens as the fourth root of the energy and of the voltage. This is adiabatic damping in the longitudinal plane, the same law that chapter 14 found in a linac.

Worked number (LHC ramp). Hold 1.0 eV s from 450 GeV at 8 MV to 7 TeV at 16 MV: the bunch would shrink by \((450/7000/2)^{1/4}\) = 0.423, from 1.50 ns to 0.64 ns (\(4\sigma\)). Such short, dense bunches have lower thresholds for instabilities, and suffer more intrabeam scattering: collisions between particles of the same bunch, which trade momentum between the planes and slowly enlarge the emittance. So the LHC design blows the emittance up on purpose during the ramp, by exciting the beam with band-limited noise, from 1.0 to 2.5 eV s (Evans and Bryant 2008). At 2.5 eV s the bunch is 1.0 ns long at 7 TeV.

The price of shortening. At fixed emittance, halving the bunch length adiabatically takes \(2^4\) = 16 times the voltage: 256 MV instead of 16 MV in the LHC. This fourth-power law is one reason gymnastics (section 11) exist.

9. Capture, filamentation and debunching

Adiabatic capture of a coasting beam. A beam injected without RF, or debunched, is a uniform band across all phases with some energy spread. To capture it, raise the voltage from zero slowly. At first the buckets are tiny and the motion slow, so a linear ramp is far from adiabatic at the start; good practice raises the voltage so that \(\varepsilon_{ad}\) stays constant. I tracked the stationary map for a coasting beam of uniform energy spread whose area was a quarter of the final bucket, raising the voltage linearly over a given number of final synchrotron periods and then waiting 50 periods:

Over 30 periods the growth is under 10 per cent. For a beam of half the bucket’s area the same ramps gave 89.4 per cent and 100 per cent captured, and 1.00 and 0.47 of the bucket.

Filamentation. Inject a bunch whose ellipse does not match the bucket’s trajectories (energy spread too small for its length, say, or voltage too high). The bunch rotates in phase space as a whole, and its length oscillates at \(2\Omega_s\): a quadrupole (breathing) oscillation. If it is also off in phase or energy, its centre moves at \(\Omega_s\): a dipole oscillation. Because \(\Omega\) falls with amplitude, outer particles lag inner ones and the bunch winds into a spiral, then smears into a larger, matched distribution. Liouville’s theorem holds for the fine-grained density, but the area of the smallest stable contour enclosing all particles has grown. For a linear mismatch, where the bunch’s aspect ratio is \(r\) times the matched one, the final rms emittance is \((r + 1/r)/2\) times the initial. I tracked Gaussian bunches:

20° 1.2 1.012 1.017 1.00 to 0.86

For long bunches the nonlinear well distorts the result; the rms measure then understates the damage done to the edges.

Debunching. Switch the RF off and each particle drifts at \(dt/dn = T_0\eta\delta\) per turn. In phase, \(d\phi/dt = h\omega_0\eta\delta\), so the leading edge of one bunch and the trailing edge of the next drift towards each other at \(h\omega_0|\eta|\,\Delta\delta/2\) each, across a gap of \(2\pi - \Delta\phi\). Successive bunches begin to overlap after a time of order

\[ t_D \approx \frac{2\pi - \Delta\phi}{h\omega_0|\eta|\,\Delta\delta}, \]

with \(\Delta\phi\) and \(\Delta\delta\) the full phase and momentum spreads. Garoby (eq. 20, and in Chao and Tigner’s Handbook, section 4.9.2.3) writes the same time as \((\pi - \Delta\phi)/(h\omega|\eta|\Delta p/p)\), but there \(2\Delta\phi\) and \(2\Delta p\) are the full spreads: with half-spreads the two forms are identical. Good debunching needs a time without RF much longer than \(t_D\); the residual modulation is then small.

Worked number (LHC RF trip). A trip is the RF switching itself off, by its protection, after a fault. At 7 TeV with \(\sigma_\delta = 1.13\times10^{-4}\), a one-sigma particle slips by one RF period, 2.495 ns, in \(1/(f_{RF}\eta\sigma_\delta)\) = 69 ms, 770 turns. Within a fraction of a second after an RF trip the LHC beam would spread round the ring, into the gap kept free for the abort kicker. (A kicker is a magnet pulsed on in a fraction of a microsecond to deflect the beam out of the ring; the abort kicker sends the whole beam to an absorber when something goes wrong. Its field cannot rise instantly, so a stretch of the ring, the abort gap, must be empty when it fires, or the particles there are sprayed into the machine. Chapter 21 describes kickers.)

10. Transition crossing

What happens. Near transition, expand \(\eta = \alpha_c - 1/\gamma^2\) about \(\gamma_t\): \(d\eta/d\gamma = 2/\gamma^3\), so

\[ \eta(t) \approx \frac{2\dot\gamma}{\gamma_t^3}\,t, \]

with \(t = 0\) at transition and \(\dot\gamma\) the rate of rise of \(\gamma\) (s\(^{-1}\)). Three consequences follow from sections 3 and 8.

  • \(\Omega_s \propto \sqrt{|\eta|} \propto |t|^{1/2}\) falls to zero: the synchrotron motion freezes.
  • Adiabatically, \(\sigma_t \propto |\eta|^{1/4} \to 0\) and \(\sigma_\delta \propto |\eta|^{-1/4} \to \infty\). The bunch gets short and its energy spread large: high peak current, and a wide beam where the dispersion is large (the dispersion \(D_x\) of chapter 16 moves a particle sideways by \(D_x\delta\), so a large \(\delta\) widens the beam).
  • \(\eta\) changes sign. A particle with excess energy that arrived early now arrives late. Kept on the rising slope, the bunch sits at the unstable fixed point and falls apart. The RF phase relative to the bunch must jump from \(\phi_s\) to \(\pi - \phi_s\) at the instant of transition. In practice the jump is applied to the phase loop (section 13) as \(180° - 2\phi_s\) (Damerau 2019).

The non-adiabatic time. Adiabatic scaling must fail before \(t = 0\), because \(\Omega_s \to 0\). Putting the linear \(\eta(t)\) into section 3’s \(\Omega_s^2\) gives \(\Omega_s^2 = \Lambda|t|\), with

\[ \Lambda = \frac{h\omega_0^2\,q\hat{V}|\cos\phi_s|}{2\pi\beta^2E}\cdot\frac{2\dot\gamma}{\gamma_t^3}\quad(\text{s}^{-3}). \]

(This \(\Lambda\) is a new constant; the letter \(K\) is already taken by the standard map and the elliptic integral.) Then \(\Omega_s = \sqrt{\Lambda|t|}\), whose rate of change is \(|\dot\Omega_s| = \Lambda/(2\Omega_s)\) (the chain rule on a square root), so the adiabaticity parameter is \(\varepsilon_{ad} = |\dot\Omega_s|/\Omega_s^2 = \Lambda/(2\Omega_s^3) = \Lambda/(2\Omega_s\,\Lambda|t|) = 1/(2|t|\,\Omega_s)\): it grows without limit as \(t \to 0\). Define \(T_c\) by \(\Omega_s(T_c)\,T_c = 1\), so \(\varepsilon_{ad} = 1/2\) there: \(\Lambda T_c\cdot T_c^2 = 1\), \(T_c^3 = 1/\Lambda\). With \(E = \gamma_tmc^2\), the factors combine as \(\gamma_t\cdot\gamma_t^3 = \gamma_t^4\) and \(2\pi/2 = \pi\):

\[ T_c = \left(\frac{\pi\beta^2mc^2\gamma_t^4}{h\omega_0^2\,q\hat{V}|\cos\phi_s|\,\dot\gamma}\right)^{1/3}. \]

Inside \(|t| < T_c\) the bunch shape is frozen at what it was at about \(-T_c\): the motion is too slow to follow the changes, and the bunch cannot adjust its length (Figure 19.6). The constant in front depends on the convention; Hardt (1974) measures time in units of a quantity of the same form, and Wei’s section on transition crossing in Chao and Tigner’s Handbook (section 4.9.1, eq. 1) gives exactly this expression, with \(E_s = \gamma_tmc^2\).

Worked numbers (PS). \(\gamma_t\) = 6.08 (Aumon et al. 2010): total energy 5.70 GeV, kinetic 4.77 GeV, momentum 5.63 GeV/\(c\). At 2 T/s, \(\dot\gamma\) = 44.1 s\(^{-1}\) and \(f_0\) = 0.471 MHz. With \(h\) = 21 and 200 kV (\(\phi_s\) = 26.1°), \(T_c\) = 1.39 ms, 655 turns; with \(h\) = 8, 1.92 ms; with \(h\) = 21 and 100 kV (so \(\phi_s\) = 61.6°), 2.17 ms. And, in the linear expansion of \(\eta\), \(|\eta|\) stays below 0.004 for \(2\times0.004\gamma_t^3/(2\dot\gamma)\) = 20.4 ms (20.7 ms with the exact \(\eta = 1/\gamma_t^2 - 1/\gamma^2\)). Aumon et al. (2008) state that instabilities develop if \(|\eta|\) stays below 0.004 for more than 1 ms.

Nonlinear and collective effects. Particles of different momenta cross transition at different times, because \(\gamma_t\) depends on \(\delta\) through \(\alpha_1\): the Johnsen effect, after the analysis Johnsen presented in 1956 (the name and reference as in Wei, Handbook section 4.9.1, which gives the emittance growth it causes). Space charge acts as a longitudinal defocusing below transition and focusing above, so a bunch matched before is mismatched after. And at high intensity, the negative-mass and microwave instabilities, and a fast transverse instability, grow while the bunch is short and \(\eta\) small (chapter 22). Every cure shortens the time spent near \(\eta = 0\).

The \(\gamma_t\) jump. Pulsed quadrupoles at places with dispersion change \(\alpha_c\), and so \(\gamma_t\), quickly, without moving the tunes: Hardt’s scheme pairs lenses of opposite sign half a betatron wavelength apart. Raise \(\gamma_t\) before transition, then drop it suddenly through the beam’s \(\gamma\). In 1973 the PS replaced an earlier tune-jump system with 8 fast and 6 slow quadrupoles in 4 doublets and 2 triplets. They changed \(\gamma_t\) by 2.3 within 0.8 ms; transition was crossed 50 times faster than without a jump, and more than \(5\times10^{12}\) protons per pulse were brought through (Hardt 1974). In recent operation the jump is \(\Delta\gamma_t = -1.24\) in 500 µs (Aumon et al. 2010): 2480 s\(^{-1}\), 56 times the natural \(\dot\gamma\). With such a jump in my tracking, the time with \(|\eta| < 0.004\) fell from 20.7 ms to 0.36 ms and the shortest bunch was 3.58 ns instead of 1.92 ns, at the cost of a slightly larger peak \(\sigma_\delta\) (\(3.80\times10^{-3}\) instead of \(3.45\times10^{-3}\)) and some mismatch after the sudden change. The price is a large distortion of the optics during the jump, and losses that Aumon et al. (2008) studied. Machines built later avoid transition where they can: by injecting above it (LHC beams enter the SPS just above it, Damerau 2019), or with lattices whose momentum compaction is negative, so that \(\gamma_t\) is imaginary and never reached (CERN’s Low Energy Antiproton Ring, LEAR, had such an “imaginary transition energy”, with a phase advance of about 250° per period; Gilardoni and Manglunki 2013).

11. RF gymnastics

Liouville’s theorem allows any area-preserving change to the shape of the distribution. Garoby’s survey (2011) names the standard ones; all use the equations above with several voltages, each switched or ramped on a programme.

Bunch rotation. To shorten a bunch quickly, let it rotate in a bucket to which it is not matched. Suppose a bunch is matched at voltage \(\hat{V}_1\), with half-length \(\hat t_1\) and half-height \(\Delta\hat{E}_1 = k_1\hat t_1\). Switch suddenly to \(\hat{V}_2 > \hat{V}_1\), whose matched ratio is \(k_2 = k_1\sqrt{\hat{V}_2/\hat{V}_1}\) (since \(\Delta\hat E/\hat t \propto \Omega_s \propto \sqrt{\hat V}\)). A quarter synchrotron period later the energy extent has turned into time extent: \(\hat t = \Delta\hat{E}_1/k_2 = \hat t_1\sqrt{\hat{V}_1/\hat{V}_2}\). Rotation shortens as \(\hat V^{-1/2}\), against \(\hat V^{-1/4}\) adiabatically, and takes a quarter period instead of many. The energy spread grows by the same factor, and the bunch must be extracted at that moment. Variants stretch the bunch first, by lowering the voltage, or by jumping the phase by \(\pi\) so that the bunch lies along the separatrix (Garoby). The outer particles rotate more slowly (section 3), so the rotated bunch has an S shape and the compression is less than the linear estimate; Garoby gives, for a bunch filling 1 per cent of the bucket, an optimum length of 0.27 bucket length before rotation and 0.02 after.

Worked numbers (PS, 40 MHz). At 26.4 GeV/\(c\) the PS’s 40.055 MHz cavity (\(h\) = 84) captures the beam adiabatically up to 100 kV, jumps non-adiabatically to 300 kV, and the 80 MHz cavities, 600 kV together, are switched on during the rotation to reach 3.8 ns and 0.35 eV s (Garoby et al. 1997). At 300 kV the synchrotron period is 1.06 ms (the paper says about 1 ms) and a quarter of it 265 µs. A 0.35 eV s bunch matched at 100 kV fills 40 per cent of the 0.885 eV s bucket. Tracked, its \(4\sigma\) length is 11.95 ns at 100 kV; after the jump to 300 kV it reaches 6.87 ns after 304 µs, matching the linear estimate \(11.95/\sqrt3\) = 6.90 ns. The 40 MHz system alone cannot reach 4 ns; the 80 MHz system is needed.

Splitting and merging. Hold a bunch with \(V_1\) at \(h_1\). Bring up \(V_2\) at \(h_2 = 2h_1\) with its unstable phase at the bunch centre, and lower \(V_1\). The single well grows a hump in the middle and becomes two wells (Figure 19.7); the bunch divides into two, each of half the emittance. Done over more than five synchrotron periods it preserves emittance with almost no blow-up (Garoby). The reverse is merging. Splitting in three needs \(h_1\), \(2h_1\) and \(3h_1\) together, with voltages and relative phases controlled precisely so that the three parts are equal. In the PS a bunch on \(h\) = 8 was split into two on \(h\) = 16 within 25 ms at 3.57 GeV/\(c\) with no visible blow-up.

The LHC’s 25 ns train. The PS scheme in use since about 2000 (Evans and Bryant 2008; Damerau et al. 2013):

  1. Six bunches from the PS Booster, in two batches of 4 + 2 (or 3 + 3), are captured on \(h\) = 7 at 1.4 GeV.
  2. Each is split in three by \(h\) = 7, 14 and 21: 18 bunches on \(h\) = 21. The phase and radial loops switch harmonic once, from 7 to 21. The bunches must not exceed about 1.3 eV s, or particles leak into the gap reserved for the kicker.
  3. The beam is accelerated on \(h\) = 21 to 25 GeV (26 GeV total energy).
  4. Each bunch is split in two (\(h\) = 21 to 42), and again in two (\(h\) = 42 to 84), using 20 and 40 MHz cavities: 72 bunches on \(h\) = 84, with 12 buckets empty, a gap of about 320 ns for the extraction kicker’s rise.
  5. The 40 and 80 MHz systems rotate the bunches to about 4 ns to fit the SPS’s 200 MHz buckets.

Each of the six bunches has become twelve (figure 19.8). The scheme had to give 14 per cent more intensity per Booster bunch than the abandoned 8-into-84 debunching scheme, for the same intensity per LHC bunch.

Batch compression. Raise the harmonic in small steps, from \(h_0\) to a slightly higher \(h_1\) and so on, with the relative phase chosen so the bunches converge on the batch centre. The bunch count is kept and the spacing shortens. Each step is limited by the acceptance of the outer buckets, which is reduced because two waves of neighbouring harmonics beat around the ring. Garoby (1985) introduced it in the PS for the antiproton production beam; a PS example takes four bunches from \(h\) = 8 to \(h\) = 20 in steps of 2 at 26 GeV.

Batch compression, merging and splitting (BCMS). For higher brightness the PS takes 4 + 4 bunches into \(h\) = 9, accelerates them to an intermediate plateau at 2.5 GeV (where the buckets are twice as large for the same voltage), raises \(h\) from 9 to 14 in five steps, merges pairs (\(h\) = 14 to 7), splits each in three to \(h\) = 21, and then does the usual double splitting at top energy: 48 bunches from 8. Each Booster bunch is divided by 6 instead of 12, so the Booster need deliver less intensity per bunch, and the transverse emittance is smaller. The uncontrolled longitudinal blow-up stays below 10 per cent, and the brightness at LHC entry was double that of the standard beam (Damerau et al. 2013).

Slip stacking. Two RF systems at slightly different frequencies hold two families of buckets that drift past each other; when they coincide, a larger bucket captures pairs. It doubles the bunch population at the cost of a much larger emittance (Garoby).

Barrier buckets. A broadband cavity can give short voltage pulses once a turn instead of a sine wave. Between the pulses the voltage is zero and particles drift freely; a pulse of the right sign reflects them. A particle of energy offset \(\Delta E\) entering a rectangular barrier of voltage \(V_0\) loses energy at \(qV_0\) per turn while it drifts at \(\eta\,\Delta E\,T_0/(\beta^2E)\) per turn. Its energy offset reverses after penetrating a time \(\tau\) with \(\Delta E^2 = 2\beta^2E\,qV_0\,\tau/(|\eta|T_0)\). A barrier of width \(T_1\) therefore holds particles up to

\[ \Delta\hat{E} = \sqrt{\frac{2\beta^2E\,qV_0T_1}{|\eta|\,T_0}}, \]

which depends only on the voltage-time product of the pulse, not its shape (Bhat 2011, eq. 4). The space between barriers can be made any length, and moved, so barrier buckets hold long flat bunches, keep gaps free for kickers and let beams be stacked. Griffin and colleagues at Fermilab developed them from 1983, first to keep a gap in the antiproton Debuncher’s beam. The Fermilab Recycler, 3319.4 m round and holding antiprotons at 8 GeV kinetic energy (8.938 GeV total), used only barrier RF: four ferrite-loaded broadband cavities giving about 2 kV in total.

Worked number (Recycler). With \(\eta = -0.00869\), \(T_0\) = 11.13 µs, \(V_0\) = 2 kV and \(T_1\) = 0.91 µs, \(\Delta\hat{E}\) = 18.2 MeV, \(\delta = 2.06\times10^{-3}\) (figure 19.9).

Double-harmonic (Landau) cavities. Add a second voltage at \(n\) times the RF frequency. In the bunch-lengthening mode it flattens the bottom of the well. For a stationary bucket with \(V(\phi) = \hat V_1[\sin\phi - (1/n)\sin n\phi]\), the slope at the centre vanishes and the potential \(1 - \cos\phi - (1 - \cos n\phi)/n^2\) starts as \(\phi^4\): for \(n\) = 3, as \(\phi^4/3\). (With the Taylor series \(1 - \cos x = x^2/2 - x^4/24 + \dots\), the potential is \(\phi^2/2 - \phi^4/24 - (n^2\phi^2/2 - n^4\phi^4/24)/n^2 = (n^2 - 1)\phi^4/24\): the \(\phi^2\) terms cancel, and for \(n = 3\) the rest is \(8\phi^4/24 = \phi^4/3\).) The small-amplitude synchrotron frequency goes to zero, the spread of frequencies across the bunch becomes large, and the charge density falls. In the bunch-shortening mode the two waves add at the centre and steepen the well. Both raise the frequency spread, and with it Landau damping of coherent oscillations (chapter 22).

Worked numbers (MAX IV). MAX IV uses passive third-harmonic cavities excited by the beam itself (Tavares et al. 2014). For a flat potential with 1.6 MV main voltage and 856 keV lost per turn, I solved for the third-harmonic voltage and phase that make the voltage equal to the loss with zero first and second derivatives at the bunch centre: 439 kV, 0.274 of the main voltage. The equilibrium electron distribution in that well, with the bare-lattice energy spread, has \(\sigma_z\) = 54.0 mm, against 10.06 mm with the main RF alone: a factor 5.4. Tavares et al. give 54.1 mm and 10.1 mm. In proton machines the CERN SPS runs an 800 MHz (fourth-harmonic) system with its 200 MHz RF; Bohl et al. (1998) found the bunch-shortening mode the more effective against multi-bunch instabilities.

12. The linac as a ring without transition

In a linac there is no closed path, so \(\alpha_c = 0\) and \(\eta = -1/\gamma^2\): the magnitude is \(1/\gamma^2\), and in this course’s sign the linac is always below transition. Replace the turn by a distance: a particle travelling \(L\) with momentum offset \(\delta\) lags by \(\Delta t = -(L/\beta c)\,\delta/\gamma^2\). The ring formula then becomes, with the energy gain per unit length \(qE_0T_t\) in place of \(q\hat V/C\) (\(E_0\) the average axial field in V/m, \(T_t\) the transit-time factor), the distance \(\beta\lambda\) travelled in one RF period (\(\lambda = c/f_{RF}\) the RF wavelength) in place of \(C/h\), a wavenumber \(k_l\) (m\(^{-1}\), oscillation per metre) in place of \(\Omega_s\), and \(\beta^2E = mc^2\beta^2\gamma\):

\[ k_l^2 = \frac{2\pi qE_0T_t\cos\phi_s}{mc^2\lambda\beta^3\gamma^3}, \]

the result derived directly in chapter 14. The factor \(1/\gamma^2\) in \(\eta\) supplies two of the three powers of \(\gamma\). As \(\gamma\) grows, \(|\eta|\) falls and the phase motion of an electron linac freezes within the first metres. Linear-accelerator texts write \(\Delta W = qE_0T_tL\cos\phi\) with stable \(\phi_s\) between −90° and 0; their phase is this course’s \(\phi - 90°\) (chapter 14).

13. The RF loops

No open-loop programme makes the RF frequency follow the field to the accuracy needed (chapter 15, section 8). Every hadron synchrotron closes loops around the beam (Baudrenghien 2011); figure 19.10 draws them.

  • Frequency programme. The frequency is computed from the measured field (a “B-train” of pulses from a coil in a reference magnet) or from a modelled function. In the LHC the swing from 450 GeV/\(c\) to 3.5 TeV/\(c\) was from 400.788 860 to 400.789 713 MHz, under 1 kHz. In the original PS at \(h\) = 20 it was from about 3.0 MHz at 50 MeV to 9.54 MHz at 25 GeV/\(c\).
  • Beam phase loop. A longitudinal pick-up measures the phase of the beam’s RF component; it is compared with the phase of the cavity voltage (the vector sum of all cavities, corrected for time of flight). The error minus the stable phase corrects the RF frequency. From equation (51) of Baudrenghien, a frequency modulation enters the phase equation as \(\ddot\phi + \Omega_s^2\Delta\phi = -d\Delta\omega_{RF}/dt\); proportional feedback on the measured phase therefore adds a damping term. (If the loop sets \(\Delta\omega_{RF} = G\,\Delta\phi\), with \(G\) its gain in s⁻¹, then \(d\Delta\omega_{RF}/dt = G\,\Delta\dot\phi\) and the equation becomes \(\Delta\ddot\phi + G\,\Delta\dot\phi + \Omega_s^2\Delta\phi = 0\): a term proportional to the velocity, the friction term of the damped oscillator, which makes the oscillation decay as \(e^{-Gt/2}\).) The phase loop damps dipole oscillations and reduces RF phase noise inside the synchrotron frequency band. In proton rings there is no radiation damping, so it is the only damping of dipole motion.
  • Radial loop. A transverse pick-up measures the beam’s radial position, a proxy for its momentum, and slowly trims the frequency to keep it centred. At fixed field a frequency error \(\Delta f/f\) moves the mean radius by \(\Delta R/R = -(\alpha_c/\eta)\,\Delta f/f = -[\gamma^2/(\gamma^2 - \gamma_t^2)]\,\Delta f/f\), which diverges at transition; its gain must change sign there. The radial loop is required to cross transition.
  • Synchronisation loop. Instead of the radial position, the RF is softly locked to a reference synthesiser that follows the frequency programme, and at the end to the receiving machine’s RF for transfer. It must be slower than a synchrotron period. The LHC uses phase loop and synchronisation loop together; Baudrenghien notes it is not practical when transition must be crossed.

The PS’s ferrite cavities are themselves tuned by a further loop: the bias current in the ferrite sets its permeability and so the cavity’s frequency between 2.8 and 10 MHz (Favia et al. 2016).

Pre-requisite (continued): Fourier series and transforms

Pre-requisite. Chapter 8 showed that a train of short bunches, one per turn, has a Fourier series with a line at every harmonic of the revolution frequency, each of amplitude twice the average current, and that a Gaussian bunch multiplies line \(\omega\) by \(e^{-\omega^2\sigma_t^2/2}\). This section shows what happens to those lines when the bunch oscillates: a slow modulation at \(\Omega\) splits each line into a carrier and sidebands at \(\pm\Omega\), \(\pm2\Omega\), and so on, with sizes given by Bessel functions. Section 14 uses the sidebands at \(h\omega_0 \pm \Omega_s\) to explain Robinson’s instability. Taught from the Handbook of Accelerator Physics and Engineering, section 3.2.5 (the spectrum of a bunch oscillating in mode \(m\) has lines at \(p\omega_0 + m\omega_s\)); Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 3, section VII.1 (beam spectra of synchrotron motion: sidebands round every revolution harmonic, with Bessel-function amplitudes); and, for Bessel functions, the chapter 2 section.

Amplitude modulation. Take a carrier \(\cos\omega_ct\) and let its amplitude swing slowly at \(\Omega \ll \omega_c\), by a fraction \(\mu\):

\[ (1 + \mu\cos\Omega t)\cos\omega_ct = \cos\omega_ct + \frac{\mu}{2}\cos(\omega_c + \Omega)t + \frac{\mu}{2}\cos(\omega_c - \Omega)t , \]

by the product-to-sum rule \(\cos A\cos B = \tfrac12[\cos(A + B) + \cos(A - B)]\). A spectrum analyser sees three lines: the carrier, and two sidebands \(\Omega\) either side, each \(\mu/2\) of the carrier.

Phase modulation. Now let the carrier’s phase swing instead, by an amplitude \(m\) (rad), the modulation index: \(\cos(\omega_ct - m\sin\Omega t)\). The factor \(e^{-im\sin\Omega t}\) is periodic in \(\Omega t\), so it has a Fourier series, and its coefficients are Bessel functions. Bessel’s integral defines

\[ J_n(m) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{i(m\sin u - nu)}\,du , \]

which is precisely the \(n\)th Fourier coefficient of \(e^{im\sin u}\). So \(e^{im\sin u} = \sum_n J_n(m)\,e^{inu}\), and taking real parts,

\[ \cos(\omega_ct - m\sin\Omega t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}J_n(m)\cos(\omega_c - n\Omega)t . \]

There are sidebands at every multiple of \(\Omega\), of size \(|J_n(m)|\) (with \(J_{-n} = (-1)^nJ_n\)). For small \(m\), \(J_0 \approx 1 - m^2/4\), \(J_1 \approx m/2\) and \(J_2 \approx m^2/8\): the first pair of sidebands is \(m/2\) of the carrier, like amplitude modulation with \(\mu = m\), and the second pair is second order. (The two first sidebands of phase modulation have opposite signs, where those of amplitude modulation have the same sign; a spectrum analyser, which shows only magnitudes, cannot tell them apart.)

A bunch in a dipole oscillation. Let one short bunch pass a pick-up each turn, at times \(t_k = kT_0 + \tau(t_k)\), where \(\tau(t) = \hat\tau\sin\Omega_st\) is its synchrotron oscillation. Each line of the beam current, \(2I_0\cos p\omega_0t\), is shifted by the delay: it becomes \(2I_0\cos[p\omega_0(t - \tau(t))]\). (This holds because \(\tau\) changes little in a turn, \(\Omega_s \ll \omega_0\).) That is phase modulation with index

\[ m = p\,\omega_0\,\hat\tau , \]

the amplitude of the oscillation measured in radians of harmonic \(p\). Line \(p\) splits into a carrier \(2I_0J_0(m)\) at \(p\omega_0\) and sidebands \(2I_0J_n(m)\) at \(p\omega_0 \pm n\Omega_s\). The same oscillation shows more strongly at high harmonics, where it is a larger fraction of a period.

A check. A toy ring with \(f_0\) = 1 MHz, a bunch oscillating by \(\hat\tau\) = 5 ns at 1 kHz, harmonic \(p\) = 10 (\(m = 0.314\)): summing \(e^{-i\omega t_k}\) over 20,000 turns gives line amplitudes of 1.9510, 0.3103 and 0.0245 times \(I_0\) at offsets 0, ±1 and ±2 kHz. \(2J_0(m)\), \(2J_1(m)\) and \(2J_2(m)\) are 1.9510, 0.3103 and 0.0245.

At work (LHC). At 400.79 MHz (\(p = h\)) a dipole oscillation of 10 ps is \(m = 2\pi\times400.79\times10^6\times10^{-11} = 0.0252\) rad, 1.44° of RF phase. The sidebands at ±23.0 Hz are each \(J_1/J_0 = 0.0126\) of the carrier, \(-38\) dB. A spectrum analyser with a resolution of a few hertz shows them, and their distance from the carrier is the synchrotron frequency: this is how \(f_s\) is measured on a running beam.

A bunch in a quadrupole oscillation. If instead the bunch length breathes, \(\sigma_t = \bar\sigma(1 + \epsilon\cos2\Omega_st)\), the line at \(\omega\), \(2I_0e^{-\omega^2\sigma_t^2/2}\), changes in amplitude. To first order its logarithm changes by \(-\omega^2\bar\sigma^2\,\epsilon\cos2\Omega_st\): amplitude modulation at \(2\Omega_s\) with \(\mu = (\omega\bar\sigma)^2\epsilon\). The sidebands sit at \(\pm2\Omega_s\), of relative size \(\mu/2\). In the LHC at 7 TeV, \(\bar\sigma = 0.252\) ns and \(\omega\bar\sigma = 0.634\) at 400.79 MHz, so a 10 per cent breathing gives \(\mu = 0.040\) and sidebands at ±46 Hz of 0.020, \(-34\) dB. In general a bunch oscillating in its \(m\)th mode (\(m\) here a mode number, not the modulation index: 1 for dipole, 2 for quadrupole) puts sidebands at \(p\omega_0 \pm m\Omega_s\), as the Handbook tabulates.

Why the sidebands matter. A cavity is a narrow resonator. Its impedance at \(h\omega_0 + \Omega_s\) and at \(h\omega_0 - \Omega_s\) is the same only if it is tuned exactly to \(h\omega_0\); otherwise the two sidebands of an oscillating bunch see different resistances, and the beam delivers different power into the cavity at the two frequencies. The difference has to come from, or go into, the bunch’s oscillation. Section 14 follows the argument to Robinson’s criterion.

Exercise F1. In the LHC, a bunch oscillates with an amplitude of 100 ps. How large are the first and second sidebands of the 400.79 MHz line, relative to the carrier?

Answer

\(m = 2\pi\times400.79\times10^6\times10^{-10} = 0.252\). \(J_0 = 0.984\), \(J_1 = 0.125\), \(J_2 = 0.0078\): first sidebands \(0.127\) of the carrier, \(-17.9\) dB; second sidebands \(-41.9\) dB. The carrier itself has dropped by 1.6 per cent.

Exercise F2. A dipole oscillation in a proton ring has an amplitude of 5° of RF phase. What is the level of the first sidebands at the RF harmonic, and at a harmonic three times higher?

Answer

At the RF harmonic, \(m = 5° = 0.0873\) rad, and \(J_1/J_0 = 0.0437\), \(-27.2\) dB. At three times the harmonic, \(m = 0.262\) and \(J_1/J_0 \approx m/2 = 0.13\), about \(-17.6\) dB: harmonics above the RF show the oscillation more strongly.

Exercise F3. Show that for small \(m\) the power in the two first sidebands together, as a fraction of the power in the carrier, is \(m^2/2\), and use it to check F1.

Answer

Power goes as amplitude squared. Each sideband is \(J_1/J_0 \approx m/2\) of the carrier in amplitude, \(m^2/4\) in power; two of them give \(m^2/2\). For F1, \(0.252^2/2 = 0.032\), against \(2\times0.127^2 = 0.032\).

14. Beam loading and Robinson stability, in outline

A bunched beam is a current at the RF frequency. Its RF component (twice the average current for short bunches) flows through each cavity and induces a voltage there; at resonance, ignoring the coupler, \(V_b = I_b R_{sh}/2\), with \(I_b\) the amplitude of that RF component (\(R_{sh}/2\) is the shunt impedance of the circuit convention: circuit texts use half the course’s linac shunt impedance). The beam’s component in phase with the cavity voltage, \(I_b\sin\phi_s\), takes power; the component in quadrature, \(I_b\cos\phi_s\), detunes the cavity. To keep the generator matched (so that it sees a pure resistance and no power is reflected back to it, chapter 13), the cavity is detuned by \(\Delta\omega\), a detuning angle \(\psi_d\) (not the betatron phase advance) with \(\tan\psi_d = 2Q_L\Delta\omega/\omega_{RF} = I_b(R_{sh}/2)(Q_L/Q_0)|\cos\phi_s|/\hat V\).

K. W. Robinson (Cambridge Electron Accelerator, report CEAL-1010, 1964) showed that the coupled system of beam and cavity can be unstable. A dipole oscillation of the bunch modulates the beam current at \(h\omega_0 \pm \Omega_s\), the two sidebands see different cavity impedances, and the difference either damps or drives the oscillation. Above transition a particle with excess energy circulates more slowly, so the motion is damped if the impedance is larger at the lower sideband: the cavity must be tuned below the RF frequency; below transition, above it. The reason, in a picture (Figure 19.11): a bunch that oscillates spends half of each synchrotron period with too much energy and half with too little. Above transition, with too much energy it goes round more slowly, so its current at the RF harmonic is at a frequency a little below \(h\omega_0\); with too little, a little above. The power a current leaves in a cavity is proportional to the cavity’s resistance at that frequency (F5, section 6). If the resonance lies below \(h\omega_0\), the bunch loses more energy when it has too much and less when it has too little: its energy error shrinks, and the oscillation is damped. Tuned the other way, the same argument makes it grow. The detuning that compensates beam loading is in the stable direction. At high current a second limit appears, when the beam-induced voltage dominates and the coherent frequency is pulled to zero.

Figure 19.11. Robinson’s argument, not to scale. The cavity’s resistance (the real part of its impedance) against frequency, for a cavity tuned below the RF harmonic \(h\omega_0\). A bunch in a dipole oscillation has current at the two sidebands \(h\omega_0 \pm \Omega_s\). Above transition the lower sideband belongs to the half of the oscillation when the bunch has too much energy and goes round slowly: there the resistance is larger, the bunch gives up more energy, and the oscillation is damped. In a real ring the resonance is far wider than \(\Omega_s\) and the difference between the two sidebands is small, but only its sign matters. Both, and the feedback loops that push them back, are in chapter 22.

Worked number (LHC). The design RF component of the beam current is 0.87 A at injection and 1.05 A at 7 TeV, and the gap of about 3 µs kept for the abort kicker makes the loading transient. The LHC uses superconducting cavities with a 30 cm aperture: their low \(R/Q\) and high stored energy keep the phase swing along the train small (Evans and Bryant 2008).

How it was researched and built

The theory, 1945 to 1956. After McMillan (1945) and Bohm and Foldy (1946, 1947), Symon and Sessler (CERN Symposium, 1956) wrote of buckets, of carrying particles in buckets moved up and down in energy, and of “phase displacement” by the passage of empty buckets, using Liouville’s theorem: the Hamiltonian view of longitudinal phase space, applied to fixed-field accelerators and stacking. Johnsen and Green analysed transition crossing at the same symposium.

The PS through transition, 1959 to 1973. The PS’s RF ran at \(h\) = 20, and Schnell’s switching circuit brought the beam through transition on 24 November 1959. An earlier tune-jump system was replaced in 1973 by Hardt’s \(\gamma_t\) jump. Hardt’s scaling laws for the achievable longitudinal density, from bunch-matching theory and the negative-mass instability, agreed with the experiments (Hardt 1974). The scheme is still in use. Damerau (2019) described a compensated phase jump, applied to drive and return signals together so that the phase loop no longer unlocks for about 400 µs at transition, tested before the second long shutdown (LS2, from 2019) and planned for operation from the restart.

Gymnastics at CERN, 1983 to 2012. At Fermilab, Griffin and colleagues described isolated and barrier buckets for the antiproton source in 1983. In the PS Garoby proposed batch compression in 1985 for the antiproton production beam of the ACOL machines; Garoby and Hancock reported bunch splitting in 1994. The PS conversion for the LHC ran from 1995 to about 2001: new \(h\) = 1 cavities in the Booster, Booster energy raised from 1 to 1.4 GeV, new 40 and 80 MHz cavities in the PS, and digital beam control. TRIUMF, under the Canada-CERN agreement, provided about a quarter of the funds and effort (Evans and Bryant 2008). The 40 MHz cavity: copper-coated steel, \(R/Q\) = 33 Ω (circuit convention), unloaded \(Q_0\) = 12 000, about 100 kW for 300 kV, 5.4 J stored, a fast RF feedback with 220 ns group delay against beam loading of \(10^{11}\) protons per bunch; installed in October 1996, it reached 300 kV and bunches below 8 ns in first tests (Garoby et al. 1997). Two 80 MHz cavities followed (Grier et al. 1998). When tests of the full debunching scheme in 1999 failed on a microwave instability, the multiple splitting scheme was adopted, which needed a 20 MHz system not in the original project. Triple splitting was demonstrated in 2000 (Garoby, Hancock and Vallet). The BCMS beam was commissioned in 2012 (Damerau et al. 2013).

Tomography, 1998 to 2000. Hancock, Knaus and Lindroos reported tomography of longitudinal phase space at CERN in 1998; Hancock, Lindroos and Koscielniak (2000) added space charge. The method tracks test particles with the map of section 1 to learn where each phase-space cell projects on each measured profile, then iterates a density whose projections converge on the measurements.

Light sources. Electron rings sit far above transition; their bunch length is set by radiation and the voltage. MAX IV, with a 100 MHz main RF and passive third-harmonic cavities, was designed from the start to lengthen its bunches by a factor of five, to lower the charge density and intrabeam scattering and to strengthen Landau damping (Tavares et al. 2014).

How it is designed today. The designer chooses \(h\) (from bunch spacing, extraction gaps and frequency swing), the voltage programme (from the bucket area needed, with margin over the emittance, and the ramp rate through \(\sin\phi_s\)), the emittance budget of each machine in the chain, and the gymnastics that turn one machine’s bunches into the next machine’s. Matching from one ring to the next is checked by requiring the bunch length and energy spread of the extracted bunch to fit the receiving bucket. Every step is tracked with BLonD (CERN), ESME (Fermilab) or similar codes, which iterate the turn-by-turn map with the full RF voltages, the measured impedance model and the loops. Typical simulations use \(10^4\) to \(10^7\) macroparticles per bunch (Timko et al. 2023).

At the bench

The instruments. A wall-current monitor (chapter 8) gives the bunch’s line density with GHz bandwidth: about 1 V per ampere of bunch current. A fast digitiser records it at chosen turns. A longitudinal pick-up gives the beam phase for the phase loop; a peak detector on the same signal gives a slow trace of the bunch’s peak current. A DC beam current transformer gives the total intensity through the cycle.

The mountain range. Plot the bunch profile of one turn as a horizontal trace, take another trace every few turns, and stack them up the screen: a “mountain range”. A matched bunch on a steady orbit is a ridge running straight up. Baudrenghien shows 1000 turns after an injection: the peak swinging from side to side as a sine wave at the synchrotron frequency is a dipole oscillation, an energy or phase error at injection, and a sine (rather than a cosine) means an energy error, cured by trimming the frequency. A ridge that pulses wider and narrower at twice the synchrotron frequency is a quadrupole oscillation: the voltage is mismatched. If it first narrows, the voltage is too high; if it first widens, too low. Figure 19.12 shows both, tracked for the LHC at injection. Damerau’s PS mountain range of a lead-ion bunch through transition records a profile every three turns, with an RF period of 102.5 ns.

Figure 19.12. Mountain ranges computed with the map of section 1 for the LHC at injection: 450 GeV, 8 MV, synchrotron period 177 turns, a 0.6 eV s bunch from the SPS. A profile is drawn every 6 turns for 300 turns, later turns higher; the dashed line is the bucket’s centre. (a) A bunch matched to 8 MV but injected with an energy error of 100 MeV (an assumed \(2.2\times10^{-4}\)): its centre swings as a sine, by 0.2 ns, later first, because above transition a particle with more energy goes round more slowly. (b) A bunch matched to 2.5 MV, 1.55 ns long (\(4\sigma_t\)), injected into 8 MV: the voltage is too high, so it first narrows, from 0.385 to 0.273 ns rms, then breathes at twice the synchrotron frequency, its peak rising each time it is short.

Matching the voltage. In the absence of loss the product of peak current and bunch length is constant, so a quadrupole oscillation is seen at once on the peak detector. At LHC injection Baudrenghien shows the peak signal oscillating at 8 MV and flat at 2.5 MV, the matched voltage; in 2010 the LHC captured at 3.5 MV, accepting a little blow-up in exchange for robustness against beam loading. The SPS delivered bunches of 0.6 eV s, 1.6 ns long at 450 GeV (Evans and Bryant 2008).

The phase loop holding the beam. On the LHC, after a four-turn latency, the phase loop brings an injection phase error to zero in about ten turns; the synchronisation loop then takes about 300 turns, 27 ms, to bring the RF back to the reference. During a store, raising the phase loop gain visibly slows the growth of the bunch length from RF noise; the phase-noise spectrum of a cavity shows a notch at the synchrotron frequency, about 24 Hz at 3.5 TeV, whose depth depends on the gain (Baudrenghien).

Tomography. Record bunch profiles over a fraction of a synchrotron period, feed them with the RF voltage, \(\eta\) and energy to the reconstruction code, and obtain an image of the density in the \((t, \Delta E)\) plane, with the emittance in eV s. It shows hollow bunches, filaments, the S shape of a rotated bunch and the unequal parts of a badly tuned split, and is used in the PS and its Booster to set up the gymnastics (Garoby 2011 shows a splitting reconstructed this way). It cannot work near transition, because the phase-space motion is frozen there (Hancock, Lindroos and Koscielniak 2000).

A transition crossing as seen. On the mountain range the bunch narrows into transition and widens after it, as in the table of section 10. On a phase display the stable-phase programme flips sign at transition; with the conventional scheme the loop unlocked and relocked, taking about 400 µs, almost 200 turns, and the bunch’s arrival time glitched (Damerau 2019). On the intensity trace a well-tuned crossing shows no step. A mistimed phase jump shows a step loss within a few milliseconds of transition, and debunched beam spreading between the bunches on the wall-current trace. Losses during the \(\gamma_t\) jump appear on loss monitors where the optics distortion makes the beam widest. For the intense single-bunch beam of the PS neutron time-of-flight facility, a fast vertical instability near transition, with a hole eaten into the bunch profile, was cured by blowing the longitudinal emittance up at flat bottom from 2 to 2.3 eV s (Aumon et al. 2010).

Setting up a cycle, in order. Steer the first turns; trim the frequency or field until the injected bunches show no dipole oscillation; adjust the capture voltage until the quadrupole oscillation disappears; close the phase loop, then the radial or synchronisation loop; ramp, watching the bunch length and losses; scan the timing of the transition phase jump and the \(\gamma_t\) jump for least loss; set the voltage ratios and relative phases of each splitting step, checking with tomography that the daughter bunches are equal; time the bunch rotation so the bunch is extracted at its shortest; check the receiving machine for capture losses.

How it fails. A missing or mistimed phase jump: the beam is lost after transition. An RF trip: the beam debunches in tens of milliseconds and fills the abort gap. Unequal splitting: uneven peaks along the train on the mountain range. Too large an emittance before splitting or compression: particles in the kicker gap, lost at extraction. RF noise: slow bunch lengthening during a store. Too short a bunch at high intensity: coupled-bunch or microwave instability, seen as growing oscillations along the train or a widening spectrum (chapter 22).

Where it stops

Everything in this chapter is Hamiltonian, and Liouville’s theorem holds for it. RF can rotate, shear, stretch, split, merge and move the longitudinal phase-space area, but it cannot make the area smaller. Every non-adiabatic step, every mismatch and every source of noise makes the effective emittance larger after filamentation. And shortening a bunch of given emittance is expensive: as \(\hat V^{-1/4}\) adiabatically, so that halving the LHC’s bunches would need 256 MV instead of 16 MV, or as \(\hat V^{-1/2}\) by rotation, at the cost of a larger energy spread.

Two things get past Liouville. In electron rings, radiation damps the emittance towards an equilibrium set by quantum excitation (chapter 20). In proton and antiproton rings, cooling, stochastic or by electrons, brings in a dissipative force from outside the beam (chapter 24). The other limit is the beam itself: at high intensity the beam’s own fields add to the RF voltage, and the bunches become unstable (chapter 22).

Exercises

1. The LHC RF runs at 400.789 MHz with \(h\) = 35640. Find the revolution frequency, the period and the bucket spacing.

Answer

\(f_0 = 400.789\ \text{MHz}/35640 = 11\,245.5\) Hz, \(T_0 = 88.92\ \mu\)s, and the buckets are \(1/f_{RF} = 2.495\) ns apart. Bunches every 25 ns fill one bucket in ten.

2. Compute the LHC’s synchrotron tune and frequency at 7 TeV with 16 MV, \(\alpha_c = 3.225\times10^{-4}\). How many turns is one oscillation?

Answer

\(\gamma = 7460.5\), so \(\eta = 3.225\times10^{-4} - 1/\gamma^2 = 3.2248\times10^{-4}\). With \(\phi_s = \pi\), \(Q_s^2 = h\eta q\hat V/(2\pi\beta^2E) = 35640\times3.2248\times10^{-4}\times16\times10^6/(2\pi\times7\times10^{12})\), so \(Q_s = 2.045\times10^{-3}\), \(\Omega_s/2\pi = Q_sf_0 = 23.0\) Hz, and one oscillation takes \(1/Q_s\) = 489 turns.

3. Find the height and area of the stationary bucket at LHC injection (450 GeV, 8 MV). What fraction does a 1.0 eV s bunch fill?

Answer

\(\eta = 3.1815\times10^{-4}\). \(\Delta E_{bucket} = \sqrt{2q\hat V\beta^2E/(\pi h|\eta|)} = 449.6\) MeV, \(\delta = 1.0\times10^{-3}\). Area \(8\Delta E_{bucket}/(h\omega_0) = 1.43\) eV s. A 1.0 eV s bunch fills 70 per cent.

4. A 2.5 eV s bunch (\(4\pi\sigma_E\sigma_t\)) sits in the LHC at 7 TeV and 16 MV. Find its \(4\sigma\) length and \(2\sigma\) relative energy spread from the small-amplitude theory.

Answer

\(\sigma_E = \beta^2E\Omega_s\sigma_t/\eta\) and \(\varepsilon_l = 4\pi\sigma_E\sigma_t\) give \(\sigma_t = \sqrt{\varepsilon_l\eta/(4\pi\beta^2E\Omega_s)}\). With \(\Omega_s = 2\pi\times23.0\) Hz: \(4\sigma_t\) = 1.007 ns and \(2\sigma_E/E = 2.26\times10^{-4}\). The design table gives 1.06 ns and \(2.2\times10^{-4}\).

5. Show that a stationary bucket’s small-amplitude frequency falls with amplitude as \(1 - \hat\phi^2/16\). Evaluate at \(\hat\phi = 90°\) and compare with the exact value.

Answer

The period of the pendulum \(\ddot{\phi} = -\Omega_s^2\sin\phi\) is \(T_{osc} = (4/\Omega_s)K(\kappa)\), \(\kappa = \sin(\hat\phi/2)\). With \(K(\kappa) = (\pi/2)(1 + \kappa^2/4 + \dots)\), \(\Omega/\Omega_s = 1/(1 + \kappa^2/4) \approx 1 - \kappa^2/4 \approx 1 - \hat\phi^2/16\) for small \(\hat\phi\). At 90°: \(1 - (\pi/2)^2/16 = 0.846\); exact \(\pi/(2K(\sin 45°)) = 0.847\).

6. The LHC RF trips at 7 TeV. How long before a particle at one standard deviation, \(\delta = 1.13\times10^{-4}\), has slipped one bucket?

Answer

It slips \(T_0\eta\delta\) = 3.24 ps per turn, so one RF period of 2.495 ns takes \(1/(f_{RF}\eta\delta)\) = 69 ms, 770 turns.

7. In the PS, \(\gamma_t\) = 6.08, the field rises at 2 T/s on \(\rho\) = 70.0 m, \(C = 200\pi\) m, and \(h\) = 21 with 200 kV. Find \(\phi_s\), \(\dot\gamma\), the non-adiabatic time, and how long \(|\eta|\) stays below 0.004, with and without a \(\gamma_t\) jump of −1.24 in 500 µs.

Answer

Energy gain per turn \(C\rho\dot B = 88.0\) keV, so \(\sin\phi_s = 0.440\), \(\phi_s = 26.1°\). At transition \(f_0\) = 0.4706 MHz, \(\dot\gamma = 88.0\ \text{keV}\times f_0/m_pc^2 = 44.1\) s\(^{-1}\). \(T_c = (\pi\beta^2m_pc^2\gamma_t^4/(h\omega_0^2q\hat V|\cos\phi_s|\dot\gamma))^{1/3}\) = 1.39 ms, 655 turns. \(|\eta| < 0.004\) while \(|\gamma - \gamma_t| < 0.004\gamma_t^3/2 = 0.450\): 20.4 ms. The jump changes \(\gamma - \gamma_t\) at 2480 s\(^{-1}\) plus 44 s\(^{-1}\); in tracking the time with \(|\eta| < 0.004\) fell to 0.36 ms.

8. For the moving bucket of exercise 7, find the area and height factors and the phase extent of the bucket.

Answer

\(Y = \sqrt{\cos\phi_s - (\pi/2 - \phi_s)\sin\phi_s} = 0.638\). Integrating the separatrix numerically gives \(\alpha = 0.391\) (the approximation \((1-\sin\phi_s)/(1+\sin\phi_s)\) gives 0.389), and the bucket extends from −48.8° to 153.9°.

9. The Fermilab Recycler holds antiprotons at 8 GeV kinetic energy (8.938 GeV total) with \(\eta = -0.00869\), \(T_0\) = 11.13 µs, in rectangular barriers of 2 kV and 0.91 µs. Derive the barrier bucket height and evaluate it.

Answer

Inside the barrier \(d\Delta E/dn = -qV_0\) and \(d\tau/dn = |\eta|\Delta E\,T_0/(\beta^2E)\). Dividing, \(\Delta E\,d\Delta E = -[\beta^2E\,qV_0/(|\eta|T_0)]\,d\tau\), so a particle of offset \(\Delta E\) turns round after penetrating \(\tau = |\eta|T_0\Delta E^2/(2\beta^2EqV_0)\). Setting \(\tau = T_1\): \(\Delta\hat E = \sqrt{2\beta^2EqV_0T_1/(|\eta|T_0)}\) = 18.2 MeV, \(\delta = 2.06\times10^{-3}\).

10. MAX IV: 3 GeV, 528 m, 99.931 MHz, \(\alpha_c = 3.06\times10^{-4}\), 1.6 MV, 856 keV lost per turn, \(\sigma_\delta = 0.769\times10^{-3}\). Find \(h\), \(\phi_s\), \(Q_s\), the synchrotron frequency and \(\sigma_z\). Then explain in a sentence why a flat-potential third harmonic lengthens the bunch fivefold.

Answer

\(h = f_{RF}C/(\beta c) = 176\). Above transition, \(\phi_s = 180° - \arcsin(0.856/1.6) = 147.7°\). \(Q_s = 1.97\times10^{-3}\), \(\Omega_s/2\pi = 1.12\) kHz, \(\sigma_z = c\alpha_c\sigma_\delta/\Omega_s\) = 10.06 mm. With the harmonic voltage chosen to cancel the slope and curvature of the total voltage at the bunch, the well is quartic instead of quadratic; electrons with the same energy spread spread much further in time, and the equilibrium distribution in that well has \(\sigma_z\) = 54.0 mm.
