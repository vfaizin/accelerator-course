17 Magnets, from iron to superconducting

1820 to today: Ørsted’s needle and Sturgeon’s horseshoe, the iron yokes of the CERN PS and the Fermilab Main Ring, the superconducting rings of the Tevatron, HERA, RHIC and the LHC, and the permanent magnets of the Recycler; past the wall of iron that saturates near 2 T and copper that turns megawatts into heat.

In one page

The wall. A ring’s size is set by its bending field: the radius is \(\rho = B\rho/B\), the rigidity over the field. An iron magnet concentrates the flux of a copper coil into a gap, but iron saturates at about 1.6 to 2 T, and the copper dissipates power for as long as the field is on. A 7 TeV proton ring built of iron at 2 T would need a bending radius of 11.7 km. At 8.33 T it needs 2.8 km.

The idea. In an iron magnet the field is shaped by the iron surfaces, which are equipotentials of the magnetic scalar potential. Above 2 T the field must come from the current itself. A current that varies as \(\cos\theta\) around a cylinder gives a pure dipole field inside, and superconductors carry such currents without loss: niobium-titanium up to about 10 T at 1.9 K, niobium-tin to 11 T and beyond. The price is forces of tens of megapascals, stored energies of megajoules per magnet that must be dumped safely when the conductor turns resistive (a quench), and refrigeration at 1.9 K or 4.5 K, each watt of which costs hundreds of watts at room temperature.

The equations to remember. For a gap of height \(G\) (m; a capital letter, because \(g\) is the quadrupole gradient) with \(N\) turns carrying \(I\) (A) and iron of large permeability,

\[ B = \frac{\mu_0 N I}{G}. \]

A two-dimensional field is a power series in \(z = x + iy\), with reference radius \(R_r\) (m), normal and skew coefficients \(B_n\), \(A_n\) (T), and errors in units of \(10^{-4}\):

\[ B_y + iB_x = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (B_n + iA_n)\left(\frac{z}{R_r}\right)^{n-1}, \qquad b_n = 10^4\,\frac{B_n}{B_1}. \]

A surface current \(K = K_0\cos\theta\) (A/m) on a cylinder gives a uniform field inside, and a 60° sector coil of width \(w\) (m) and current density \(J\) (A/m\(^2\)) gives nearly the same:

\[ B = \frac{\mu_0 K_0}{2}, \qquad B \approx \frac{\sqrt{3}}{\pi}\,\mu_0 J w. \]

The magnetic pressure and energy density \(p_B\) (Pa, J/m\(^3\); the subscript keeps it apart from the momentum \(p\)) and the ideal cost of removing heat \(Q\) at temperature \(T\) to a room at \(T_a\) (here and in section 13 \(Q\) is a heat flow in W, not a tune or a quality factor, and \(W_{el}\) the electrical power):

\[ p_B = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}, \qquad \frac{W_{el}}{Q} \geq \frac{T_a - T}{T}. \]

Numbers to know. Iron saturates at 1.6 to 2 T. The LHC has 1232 dipoles, 15 m long (14.3 m magnetic length), designed for 8.33 T at 11 850 A and 1.9 K, each storing about 7 MJ. At 8.33 T the magnetic pressure is 27.6 MPa, 276 bar (272 atmospheres). Removing 1 W at 1.9 K costs at least 157 W at room temperature, and about 560 W in a real plant. The first superconducting synchrotron, Fermilab’s Tevatron, ran its first beam on 3 July 1983 with about 4.4 T dipoles at 4.5 K. Field quality of one part in \(10^4\) at two-thirds of the aperture is the usual demand.

The wall

In 1820 Hans Christian Ørsted found that a current deflects a compass needle, and in the same year André-Marie Ampère showed that iron inside a current-carrying coil becomes magnetised. In 1824 William Sturgeon wound about 18 turns of bare copper on a horseshoe of iron and made the first electromagnet. Every accelerator magnet from Lawrence’s cyclotron to the 1970s was a descendant: copper coils, an iron yoke, and a gap for the beam. Iron multiplies the field of the coil enormously for little current, because its permeability is thousands of times that of empty space (the relative permeability \(\mu_r\) of F4, section 15). But only until the iron’s magnetic domains are all aligned. Above about 1.6 to 2 T the iron saturates and adds no more; the coil must then supply the field on its own, as it would in air.

That sets the size of a ring. A proton of momentum \(p\) bends on a radius \(\rho = p/(qB)\): the magnetic force \(qvB\) (F4, section 10) supplies the pull \(pv/\rho\) towards the centre (\(mv^2/\rho\) for a slow particle, and true at any speed with the relativistic momentum \(p = \gamma mv\)), so \(qvB = pv/\rho\); the product \(B\rho = p/q\) is the rigidity of chapter 1, section 4.1, fixed by the particle alone. The synchrotrons of chapter 15 and the strong-focusing rings of chapter 16 grew to 200 GeV and then 400 GeV and beyond with iron magnets near 1 to 2 T, and their tunnels grew to kilometres. Fermilab’s Main Ring, 6.3 km round, reached its 200 GeV design energy on 1 March 1972 and 500 GeV on 14 May 1976. A 7 TeV ring with iron at 2 T would need a bending radius of 11.7 km and about 73 km of dipoles.

Power is the second half of the wall. A copper coil dissipates \(\rho_e J^2\) per unit volume for as long as the current flows, with \(\rho_e\) the copper’s resistivity and \(J\) its current density, and the larger the ring the more magnets there are to feed. Superconductors remove the resistance. Heike Kamerlingh Onnes liquefied helium at Leiden on 10 July 1908 and found on 8 April 1911 that the resistance of mercury vanished below about 4 K. But the pure metals he and his successors studied lose superconductivity in weak magnetic fields, far below a tesla, and the dream of a lossless high-field coil waited half a century. In 1955 G. B. Yntema made the first successful superconducting magnet, from niobium wire, reaching 0.7 T at 4.2 K. In 1961 J. E. Kunzler and his colleagues at Bell Laboratories found that Nb\(_3\)Sn carried more than \(10^5\) A/cm\(^2\) in 8.8 T, and in 1962 T. G. Berlincourt and R. R. Hake, at Atomics International, found that niobium-titanium alloys kept high critical fields and current densities; the alloy is ductile and can be drawn into wire.

Wire is not a magnet. The first coils of the 1960s quenched far below the critical current of their wire, jumped flux, and trained slowly upward from one quench to the next. Magnets for an accelerator also need field errors below \(10^{-4}\), reproducible from one cycle to the next and from one of a thousand magnets to another. Brookhaven’s ISABELLE, a 200 to 400 GeV proton collider recommended in 1974 and started in 1978, showed how hard that was: a prototype magnet worked in 1979, but in 1981 production magnets failed below the field needed, and the Department of Energy cancelled the project in July 1983 after spending more than 200 million US dollars. In the same month, on 3 July 1983, Robert Wilson’s Energy Doubler at Fermilab, later the Tevatron, accelerated its first beam to 512 GeV with superconducting magnets. This chapter is how iron magnets work, why they stop, and how the superconducting magnet was made to work in their place.

The physics

Pre-requisite (continued): Biot-Savart and Ampere’s law

Pre-requisite. Chapter 2 taught the Biot-Savart law, Ampère’s law round a closed path and the field of a straight current, and chapter 8 wrote Ampère’s law with \(\mathbf{H}\), \(\oint\mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = NI\), for circuits that contain iron. This section adds three tools for long, straight magnets: the vector potential \(A_z\), one function that gives both field components and whose contours are the field lines; the jump in the field across a thin sheet of current; and the force on a current, including why a sheet of current feels the average of the fields on its two sides. Section 1 uses Ampère’s law with \(\mathbf{H}\); sections 9 and 10 use the rest for the \(\cos\theta\) dipole and its forces. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, sections 3.1.1 and 3.1.2 (the straight wire, surface currents and discontinuities), 3.2 (the vector potential) and 3.4.1 (the force between currents), and Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 2, §43.

Ampère’s law with \(\mathbf{H}\), once more. Round any closed loop, the line integral of \(\mathbf{H}\) (A/m) equals the free current threading the loop: \(NI\) if the loop links a coil of \(N\) turns each carrying \(I\) (A). The currents bound inside magnetised iron are not counted; their effect is in the difference between \(\mathbf{H}\) and \(\mathbf{B}/\mu_0\). In empty space \(\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{B}/\mu_0\), in unsaturated iron \(\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{B}/(\mu_0\mu_r)\). Section 1 runs such a loop through the gap and back through the yoke.

Two-dimensional fields and one function for both components. In the long body of a magnet the currents run along the axis, which we call \(z\) in this section (the chapter calls the beam direction \(s\) when it needs a third coordinate), and the field has only \(x\) and \(y\) components, which do not depend on \(z\). Such a field can be written with a single function \(A_z(x, y)\), in tesla metres:

\[ B_x = \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial y}, \qquad B_y = -\frac{\partial A_z}{\partial x}. \]

Why can it? Because then \(\partial B_x/\partial x + \partial B_y/\partial y = \partial^2 A_z/\partial x\,\partial y - \partial^2 A_z/\partial y\,\partial x = 0\) automatically, and that is the law that magnetic field lines never end, \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0\) (F4, section 13; the divergence is F2, section 10). (The two mixed second derivatives are equal because the order in which partial derivatives are taken does not matter for a smooth function, F1, section 9.) \(A_z\) is the only component of the vector potential \(\mathbf{A}\), defined in general by \(\mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}\) (Tong, section 3.2; Landau and Lifshitz, §43); with \(\mathbf{A} = A_z\hat{\mathbf{z}}\) the curl of F2, section 11 has the components \((\partial A_z/\partial y,\ -\partial A_z/\partial x,\ 0)\), exactly the two formulas above.

The magnets of this chapter are round, so polar coordinates \((r, \theta)\) are often easier (F2, section 13, there with the angle called \(\phi\)). The radial and azimuthal components are \(B_r = B_x\cos\theta + B_y\sin\theta\) and \(B_\theta = -B_x\sin\theta + B_y\cos\theta\). The chain rule for several variables (F1, sections 2 and 9: a small change of \(\theta\) moves \(x\) by \(\partial x/\partial\theta = -r\sin\theta\) and \(y\) by \(\partial y/\partial\theta = r\cos\theta\), and \(A_z\) changes by its slope along each times the move), with \(x = r\cos\theta\) and \(y = r\sin\theta\), gives \(\partial A_z/\partial\theta = -r\sin\theta\,\partial A_z/\partial x + r\cos\theta\,\partial A_z/\partial y = r(B_y\sin\theta + B_x\cos\theta)\), and in the same way \(\partial A_z/\partial r = B_x\sin\theta - B_y\cos\theta\). So

\[ B_r = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial A_z}{\partial\theta}, \qquad B_\theta = -\frac{\partial A_z}{\partial r}. \]

Field lines are the contours of \(A_z\). Take a small step \((dx, dy)\) along the field, so that \((dx, dy)\) is proportional to \((B_x, B_y)\). The change of \(A_z\) is \(dA_z = (\partial A_z/\partial x)\,dx + (\partial A_z/\partial y)\,dy = -B_y\,dx + B_x\,dy\), which is proportional to \(-B_yB_x + B_xB_y = 0\). Along a field line \(A_z\) does not change. A map of the contours of \(A_z\) is a picture of the field lines; this is how two-dimensional magnet codes draw them.

The flux between two contours. Take a strip of length \(L\) (m) along \(z\), standing on a straight segment from a point \(P_1\) to a point \(P_2\) in the \((x, y)\) plane. Let \(\hat{\mathbf{t}} = (t_x, t_y)\) be the unit vector along the segment and \(\hat{\mathbf{n}} = (t_y, -t_x)\) the unit normal to the strip. The flux through the strip is \(\int\mathbf{B}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dA\) (F2, section 9), and \(\mathbf{B}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}} = B_xt_y - B_yt_x = (\partial A_z/\partial y)\,t_y + (\partial A_z/\partial x)\,t_x\), which is the rate of change of \(A_z\) along the segment. Integrating along it,

\[ \Phi_B = L\left[A_z(P_2) - A_z(P_1)\right]. \]

The flux through the strip is the length times the difference of \(A_z\) at its two edges, whatever lies between. Section 7 uses this for a measuring coil whose two long sides sit at two radii.

At work: a straight conductor. For a line current \(I\) along \(z\) at the origin,

\[ A_z = -\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi}\ln\frac{r}{r_{ref}}, \]

with \(r_{ref}\) any fixed radius: adding a constant to \(A_z\) changes no field. Then \(B_r = 0\) and \(B_\theta = -\partial A_z/\partial r = \mu_0I/(2\pi r)\), the field of a straight wire from chapter 2. The contours of \(A_z\) are circles, as the field lines are. One LHC dipole conductor carrying 11 850 A makes 0.237 T at 10 mm, and the flux per metre between radii of 10 mm and 20 mm is \((\mu_0I/2\pi)\ln 2 = 1.64\) mWb.

A sheet of current. A coil made of many thin conductors side by side behaves, seen from a distance larger than one conductor, like a thin sheet of current. Its strength is the surface current density \(K\) (A/m): the current crossing each metre of width measured across the current (Tong, section 3.1.2). Let the current in a flat sheet run along \(z\), and let the sheet separate a region 1 from a region 2, with \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) the unit normal pointing from 1 to 2.

  • The normal component does not jump. A flat box straddling the sheet, thin compared with its area, has as much flux entering through one face as leaving through the other, because field lines do not end. So \(B_{n,2} = B_{n,1}\).
  • The tangential component across the current jumps by \(\mu_0K\). A thin rectangle straddling the sheet, with long sides of length \(l\) on either side, parallel to the sheet and at right angles to the current, encloses the current \(Kl\). Ampère’s law, with the short sides negligible, gives a jump \(B_{t,2} - B_{t,1} = \mu_0K\) in the tangential component along that direction, with the sign fixed by the right-hand rule. In one vector formula, with the cross product of F2, section 3,

\[ \hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\left(\mathbf{B}_2 - \mathbf{B}_1\right) = \mu_0\mathbf{K}. \]

Two checks. A single flat sheet makes a field \(\mu_0K/2\) on each side, pointing opposite ways, parallel to the sheet: a jump of \(\mu_0K\). A long solenoid is a sheet rolled into a tube with \(K = nI\) (\(n\) turns per metre): the field is \(\mu_0K\) inside and zero outside, a jump of \(\mu_0K\), and \(B = \mu_0nI\), as chapter 2 found. In section 9 a sheet whose current varies as \(\cos\theta\) around a cylinder makes a uniform field \(\mu_0K_0/2\) inside. For 8.33 T this needs \(K_0 = 2B/\mu_0 = 1.33\times10^7\) A/m, thirteen thousand amperes for each millimetre of circumference at the peak.

The force on a current. A charge \(q\) moving at velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) in a field feels \(q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\). A short piece \(d\mathbf{l}\) of wire carrying current \(I\) holds moving charges whose \(q\mathbf{v}\) add up to \(I\,d\mathbf{l}\), so the force on it is (Tong, section 3.4; F4, section 10)

\[ d\mathbf{F} = I\,d\mathbf{l}\times\mathbf{B}. \]

For a straight conductor along \(z\) in a transverse field the force per metre is \(IB\), at right angles to both. A conductor carrying 11 850 A in 8.33 T is pushed with 98.7 kN per metre, the weight of about ten tonnes. Two parallel wires a distance \(d\) apart, carrying \(I_1\) and \(I_2\), each sit in the other’s field \(\mu_0I/(2\pi d)\) and feel \(\mu_0I_1I_2/(2\pi d)\) per metre, attracting if the currents run the same way and repelling if they run opposite ways. Two conductors with 11 850 A, 60 mm apart, feel only 468 N per metre from each other: the large forces in a magnet come from the field of all its conductors together.

The force on a sheet: the average field. For a sheet, which \(\mathbf{B}\) goes in \(\mathbf{K}\times\mathbf{B}\), since the field is different on its two sides? Split the field near a small patch of the sheet into the patch’s own field and everything else. The patch’s own field is \(\pm\mu_0K/2\), equal and opposite on the two sides, and a flat patch cannot push itself. Everything else is continuous across the patch. If \(\mathbf{B}_{self}\) is the patch’s field on side 2, then \(\mathbf{B}_2 = \mathbf{B}_{ext} + \mathbf{B}_{self}\) and \(\mathbf{B}_1 = \mathbf{B}_{ext} - \mathbf{B}_{self}\), so \(\mathbf{B}_{ext} = (\mathbf{B}_1 + \mathbf{B}_2)/2\), and the force per unit area (N/m², that is Pa) is

\[ \mathbf{f} = \mathbf{K}\times\frac{\mathbf{B}_1 + \mathbf{B}_2}{2}. \]

When both fields are tangential, \(K = (B_{t,2} - B_{t,1})/\mu_0\) and the size of the force is

\[ f = \frac{B_{t,2} - B_{t,1}}{\mu_0}\cdot\frac{B_{t,1} + B_{t,2}}{2} = \frac{B_{t,2}^2}{2\mu_0} - \frac{B_{t,1}^2}{2\mu_0}, \]

pushing the sheet from the side of strong field towards the side of weak field. The field acts like a gas with pressure \(B^2/2\mu_0\): this is the magnetic pressure of section 10. The winding of a long solenoid, with \(B\) inside and none outside, is pushed outward with \(B^2/2\mu_0\): 9.95 MPa at 5 T, 27.6 MPa at 8.33 T.

Exercise V1. A straight conductor carries 5000 A. Find the field at 5 mm and at 15 mm, and the flux per metre of length between those two radii.

Answer

\(B = \mu_0I/(2\pi r) = 2\times10^{-7}\times5000/r\): 0.200 T at 5 mm and 0.0667 T at 15 mm. The flux per metre is \(A_z(5\ \text{mm}) - A_z(15\ \text{mm}) = (\mu_0I/2\pi)\ln 3 = 10^{-3}\times1.0986 = 1.10\) mWb.

Exercise V2. In section 9 a thin sheet with \(K_z = -K_0\cos\theta\) on a cylinder of radius \(a\) makes a uniform field \(\mu_0K_0/2\) inside. For 8.33 T at \(a = 28\) mm, find \(K_0\) and the total current flowing in one half of the sheet, and compare it with the \(9.48\times10^5\) A that one side of an LHC dipole aperture carries.

Answer

\(K_0 = 2\times8.33/(4\pi\times10^{-7}) = 1.33\times10^7\) A/m. The half with \(-\pi/2 < \theta < \pi/2\) carries \(\int K_0\cos\theta\,a\,d\theta = 2K_0a = 7.42\times10^5\) A. The real coil carries about 28 per cent more, because its current lies in a thick layer reaching out to about 62 mm, where each ampere makes less field at the centre. The iron yoke works the other way, supplying part of the field, so without it the excess would be larger still (section 9 works this out).

Exercise V3. A long solenoid makes 5 T inside. Find the surface current of its winding and the force per unit area on it from \(\mathbf{K}\times\mathbf{B}_{avg}\), and check it against \(B^2/2\mu_0\).

Answer

\(K = B/\mu_0 = 3.98\times10^6\) A/m. The average of the fields on the two sides is \((5 + 0)/2 = 2.5\) T, so \(f = KB_{avg} = 3.98\times10^6\times2.5 = 9.95\times10^6\) Pa, outward. \(B^2/2\mu_0 = 25/(2\times4\pi\times10^{-7}) = 9.95\times10^6\) Pa, the same.

Pre-requisite (continued): Magnetic circuits and iron

Pre-requisite. Chapter 8 taught magnetisation, \(\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mu_r\mathbf{H}\), the magnetic circuit, domains and saturation, and chapter 11 why the gap sets the field and why iron is laminated. This section adds what happens at the surface of iron, where the field leaves it, and the whole hysteresis loop: the remanence and the coercive field, the energy lost on each trip round the loop, and how a gap decides where on the loop the iron sits when the current is off. Section 1 uses the boundary conditions, section 3 the rule that the field leaves good iron at right angles, section 4 the loop and the remanent field, and section 14 the same loop for permanent magnets. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, sections 8.2 (magnetic fields in matter) and 8.3 (the boundary conditions between two materials), Livingston and Blewett, section 8-2 (the magnetic circuit), and the Handbook, section 6.3 (properties of magnet steels, Tigner).

Two conditions at the surface of iron. Let iron (region 1) meet air (region 2), and let no free current flow on the surface itself.

  • Normal \(\mathbf{B}\) is continuous. A flat box straddling the surface has as much flux going in as coming out, so \(B_{n,1} = B_{n,2}\). This is why, in the magnetic circuit of section 1, the field in the gap equals the field in the pole when the flux does not spread.
  • Tangential \(\mathbf{H}\) is continuous. A thin rectangle straddling the surface encloses no free current, and Ampère’s law for \(\mathbf{H}\) counts only free current (the bound currents of the magnetised iron, which do flow round its surface, are in \(\mathbf{M}\), not in the law for \(\mathbf{H}\)). So \(H_{t,1} = H_{t,2}\). Tangential \(\mathbf{B}\) does jump, by the factor \(\mu_r\).

Tong (section 8.3) derives both from the same box and loop as the current sheet above.

Field lines bend at the surface. Let the field in the iron make an angle \(\alpha_1\) with the normal, and in the air an angle \(\alpha_2\). The two conditions read \(B_1\cos\alpha_1 = B_2\cos\alpha_2\) and \((B_1/\mu_0\mu_r)\sin\alpha_1 = (B_2/\mu_0)\sin\alpha_2\). Dividing the second by the first,

\[ \tan\alpha_2 = \frac{\tan\alpha_1}{\mu_r}. \]

At work. Inside iron with \(\mu_r = 1000\), a field running at 80° to the normal, almost along the surface, leaves into the air at 0.32° to the normal; even at 89° it leaves at 3.3°. While \(\mu_r\) is large, the field in the air leaves the iron at right angles to its surface whatever it does inside. The reason is simple: \(H\) in good iron is tiny, so the tangential \(H\) just outside is tiny too. The two-dimensional potentials before section 2 show that a surface the field crosses at right angles is a surface of constant magnetic scalar potential, so the iron surface is an equipotential, and section 3 shapes poles on that basis. When the iron saturates, \(\mu_r\) falls and the rule fails: at \(\mu_r = 50\), a field at 80° inside leaves at 6.5°, and one at 89° leaves at 49°. A saturated pole no longer fixes the shape of the field in the gap.

The hysteresis loop. Wind a coil on a closed ring of iron, so that \(H = NI/l\) is set by the current, and plot \(B\) against \(H\).

  1. Start from demagnetised iron, \(B = H = 0\). As \(H\) rises, \(B\) rises steeply (domain walls move easily), then bends over and approaches saturation, \(B_s\).
  2. Bring \(H\) back to zero. \(B\) does not retrace its path: walls that jumped past pinning sites stay there. At \(H = 0\) the ring keeps a flux density \(B_R\), the remanence.
  3. Reverse \(H\). \(B\) falls to zero only when \(H = -H_c\), the coercive field (A/m). Beyond, the iron saturates the other way.
  4. Bring \(H\) back up through zero to positive saturation. The path is the mirror image of the way down. The closed curve is the hysteresis loop.

Materials are called soft or hard by the width of the loop. Magnet steels are soft: the Handbook’s table gives coercive fields of 40 to 80 A/m for low-carbon and silicon steels. Permanent-magnet materials are hard: section 14 quotes about 750 to 2000 kA/m for neodymium-iron-boron, some twenty thousand times wider.

The energy lost per cycle. Let the coil have \(N\) turns on a ring of cross-section \(A\) and path length \(l\). Faraday’s law gives a voltage \(NA\,dB/dt\) across the coil, and Ampère’s law gives the current \(I = Hl/N\). The power the supply delivers to the iron is

\[ VI = NA\frac{dB}{dt}\cdot\frac{Hl}{N} = (Al)\,H\frac{dB}{dt}, \]

so per unit volume the work done while \(B\) changes by \(dB\) is \(H\,dB\). Round a closed loop the stored energy returns to its start, and what remains, \(\oint H\,dB\), the area of the loop (J/m³), has been turned into heat. A soft loop driven to saturation is roughly a parallelogram of width \(2H_c\) and height \(2B_s\), so its area is about \(4H_cB_s\). With \(H_c = 50\) A/m and \(B_s = 1.5\) T this is 300 J/m³, or 0.038 J/kg of steel, per cycle. A synchrotron cycled every 2 s loses 0.019 W/kg this way; a transformer at 50 Hz would lose 1.9 W/kg. The loss grows in proportion to the frequency, against the square of the frequency for eddy currents (chapter 11).

Where the iron sits with the current off: the load line. Take a ring with an iron path \(l_{Fe}\) and a gap \(G\), and no current in the coil. Ampère’s law round the ring gives \(H_{Fe}l_{Fe} + H_gG = 0\). If the flux does not spread, \(B\) is the same in iron and gap, and in the gap \(B = \mu_0H_g\). Eliminating \(H_g\),

\[ B = -\mu_0\frac{l_{Fe}}{G}\,H_{Fe}. \]

This straight line through the origin, in the quarter of the \(B\)-\(H\) plane where \(B > 0\) and \(H < 0\), is the load line. The iron must be on its loop and on this line at once, so it sits where they cross. A short gap gives a steep line and the iron stays near its remanence; a long gap gives a shallow line, and the iron is pushed back towards \(-H_c\). For a soft steel the loop’s descending branch crosses \(H = 0\) high up at \(B_R\) and drops to \(B = 0\) within a few tens of A/m, while the line with \(l_{Fe} = 1\) m and \(G = 50\) mm rises by only \(2.5\times10^{-5}\) T for each A/m: over 50 A/m, 1.3 mT. So the crossing is close to \(H = -H_c\), at a small \(B\). Section 4 turns this into the remanent field of a magnet.

Figure 17.1. The hysteresis loop, drawn far wider than a real soft steel’s so that its parts show (not to scale). From demagnetised iron the first magnetisation (dashed) climbs towards saturation \(B_s\); coming back, the iron keeps \(B_R\) at \(H = 0\) and reaches \(B = 0\) only at \(-H_c\); the arrows give the direction of travel. With the current off, the iron of a magnet with a gap sits where its loop meets the load line \(B = -\mu_0(l_{Fe}/G)H\): high on the loop for a short gap (steep line), low and near \(-H_c\) for a long gap (shallow line).

For a hard magnet the part of the loop between \(B_R\) and \(-H_c\) is nearly a straight line, \(B = B_R + \mu_0\mu_{rec}H\), with a recoil permeability \(\mu_{rec}\) close to 1 (about 1.05 is typical for neodymium-iron-boron). Put a block of length \(l_m\) into an iron yoke of very high permeability with a gap \(G\) of the same cross-section. Then \(H_ml_m + H_gG = 0\) and \(B = \mu_0H_g\) give \(H_m = -BG/(\mu_0l_m)\), and on the recoil line

\[ B = \frac{B_R}{1 + \mu_{rec}G/l_m}. \]

With \(B_R = 1.2\) T and a block as long as the gap, \(B = 0.585\) T; with a block twice as long as the gap, 0.787 T. A permanent magnet gives a good fraction of its remanence only if it is long compared with the gap, or (by making its area larger than the gap’s and concentrating the flux) wide.

1 The magnetic circuit

The picture. A dipole magnet is a C or an H of iron with a gap where the beam passes and copper coils wound around the iron. The field lines leave one pole, cross the gap, and return through the iron. Since the iron’s permeability is large, the field \(H = B/\mu\) inside it is small for the same \(B\), and almost all the coil’s ampere-turns are spent driving flux across the gap.

Figure 17.2. A C-shaped dipole seen along the beam, which comes out of the page (not to scale). The two coils wrap the poles; for the upward field drawn, their current runs into the page on the open side and out of it on the yoke side. Ampère’s law is taken round the dashed loop: up across the gap \(G\), where nearly all the ampere-turns are spent, and back through the iron path \(l_{Fe}\), where \(H\) is tiny.

The derivation. Ampère’s law for static fields in matter (Tong, Electromagnetism, section 8.2.2) states that the line integral of \(\mathbf{H}\) (A/m) around a closed loop equals the free current through it:

\[ \oint \mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = N I, \]

with \(N\) the number of turns linked and \(I\) the current in each (A). Take the loop along a field line: across the gap of height \(G\) (m), where \(H = B/\mu_0\), and back through a path of length \(l_{Fe}\) (m) in iron, where \(H = B/(\mu_0\mu_r)\) with \(\mu_r\) the relative permeability. The normal component of \(\mathbf{B}\) is continuous at the pole face, and if the flux does not spread, \(B\) is the same in both. Then

\[ \frac{B G}{\mu_0} + \frac{B l_{Fe}}{\mu_0 \mu_r} = N I \quad\Longrightarrow\quad B = \frac{\mu_0 N I}{G + l_{Fe}/\mu_r} = \eta_m\,\frac{\mu_0 N I}{G}, \qquad \eta_m = \frac{1}{1 + l_{Fe}/(\mu_r G)}. \]

When \(\mu_r \gg l_{Fe}/G\) the efficiency \(\eta_m\) (subscript \(m\) for magnet, to keep it apart from the phase-slip factor \(\eta\)) is close to 1 and \(B = \mu_0 N I/G\): the field depends only on the gap and the ampere-turns, not on the iron.

Worked number. A dipole with a 50 mm gap at 1.5 T needs \(NI = BG/\mu_0 = 59.7\) kA turns, shared between the upper and lower coils. With \(\mu_r = 1000\) and 1 m of iron path, \(\eta_m = 0.980\) and \(NI = 60.9\) kA turns; with \(\mu_r = 5000\), 0.996 and 59.9 kA turns. These are illustrative values, not a particular machine.

Where it fails. The approximation \(\mu_r \gg l_{Fe}/G\) fails when the iron saturates: \(\mu_r\) falls towards 1, \(l_{Fe}/\mu_r\) is no longer small, and more current buys less field. It also ignores the flux that leaks around the coils and the fringe field that bulges out of the gap edges; a designer widens the pole by roughly a gap on each side, or shims its edges, to keep the field flat where the beam is. The full fields need a computer (How a magnet is designed today, under How it was researched and built).

Pre-requisite (continued): Complex numbers and complex functions

Pre-requisite. Chapter 7 taught complex numbers in polar form, their powers, analytic functions and the Cauchy-Riemann equations, and chapter 9 Euler’s formula. This section adds power series: why an analytic function equals its Taylor series inside a disc, how far that disc reaches, and the geometric series that expands \(1/(z - z_0)\), the field of a single wire, in powers of \(z\). Section 2 uses them for the multipole expansion, and section 9 for the field of a coil built from many wires. Taught from Penrose, The Road to Reality, sections 4.3 (convergence of power series), 4.4 (the circle of convergence in the complex plane) and 7.3 (power series from complex smoothness).

Notation. Chapter 7 wrote the complex variable as \(w = z + iy\) because there \(z\) ran along the beam. In this chapter \(z = x + iy\), with \(x\) horizontal and \(y\) vertical in the magnet’s cross-section, and in polar form \(z = re^{i\theta}\). Powers follow chapter 7’s rule, \(z^n = r^ne^{in\theta}\), so

\[ \mathrm{Re}\,z^n = r^n\cos n\theta, \qquad \mathrm{Im}\,z^n = r^n\sin n\theta . \]

Multiplying out gives the same in \(x\) and \(y\): \(z^2 = x^2 - y^2 + 2ixy\) and \(z^3 = x^3 - 3xy^2 + i(3x^2y - y^3)\). These are the shapes of quadrupole and sextupole fields and poles.

Power series. A power series is an endless sum \(c_0 + c_1z + c_2z^2 + \dots\) with complex constants \(c_n\). Penrose (section 7.3) explains the central fact. If \(f\) is analytic in a disc about the origin, then it has derivatives of every order there, and it equals its Taylor series

\[ f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!}\,z^n \]

everywhere inside the largest disc about the origin in which it stays analytic. The radius of that disc, the radius of convergence, is the distance from the origin to the nearest point where \(f\) fails to be analytic, its nearest singularity (Penrose, section 4.4). Outside that circle the series diverges. For real functions this is puzzling: Penrose’s example (section 4.3), \(1/(1 + x^2)\), is smooth for every real \(x\), yet its series \(1 - x^2 + x^4 - \dots\) diverges for \(|x| > 1\). The reason only appears in the complex plane: \(1/(1 + z^2)\) is infinite at \(z = \pm i\), a distance 1 from the origin.

The rule is this chapter’s reason that a multipole expansion holds inside a magnet’s aperture and fails at the coil: the field of a wire is infinite at the wire.

The geometric series. Let \(S_N = 1 + q + q^2 + \dots + q^N\), with \(q\) real or complex. Multiplying by \(q\) shifts every term up by one, so \(qS_N - S_N = q^{N+1} - 1\) and

\[ S_N = \frac{1 - q^{N+1}}{1 - q}. \]

If \(|q| < 1\), then \(|q^{N+1}| = |q|^{N+1}\) shrinks to zero as \(N\) grows, and \(S_N\) approaches

\[ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^n = \frac{1}{1 - q}, \qquad |q| < 1 . \]

If \(|q| \ge 1\) the terms do not shrink and the sum has no limit. For \(q = 1/2\), eleven terms give 1.9990 against the limit 2. Each extra term multiplies the error by \(|q|\).

Expanding \(1/(z - z_0)\). Let \(z_0\) be a fixed complex number. For \(|z| < |z_0|\), take out \(-z_0\) and use the geometric series with \(q = z/z_0\):

\[ \frac{1}{z - z_0} = -\frac{1}{z_0}\cdot\frac{1}{1 - z/z_0} = -\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n-1}}{z_0^{\,n}} . \]

For \(|z| > |z_0|\), take out \(z\) instead, with \(q = z_0/z\):

\[ \frac{1}{z - z_0} = \frac{1}{z}\cdot\frac{1}{1 - z_0/z} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z_0^{\,n}}{z^{\,n+1}} . \]

One function, two series: rising powers of \(z\) inside the circle through \(z_0\), falling powers outside it. The first is the Taylor series of \(1/(z - z_0)\) about the origin, and its radius of convergence is \(|z_0|\), the distance to the singularity, as the rule says.

The field of a wire as a complex function. A line current \(I\) runs along the axis direction at the point \(z_0\) (with \(x\), \(y\) and the axis forming a right-handed set). Its field has size \(\mu_0I/(2\pi d)\) at distance \(d\) and circles the wire anticlockwise. Write \(\delta = z - z_0 = \delta_x + i\delta_y\) for the position relative to the wire. The field is the vector \((\delta_x, \delta_y)\) turned anticlockwise by a right angle, \((-\delta_y, \delta_x)\), scaled by \(\mu_0I/(2\pi|\delta|^2)\):

\[ B_x = -\frac{\mu_0I\,\delta_y}{2\pi|\delta|^2}, \qquad B_y = \frac{\mu_0I\,\delta_x}{2\pi|\delta|^2} . \]

So \(B_y + iB_x = \mu_0I(\delta_x - i\delta_y)/(2\pi|\delta|^2)\). The numerator is the conjugate \(\delta^*\), and \(\delta^*/|\delta|^2 = 1/\delta\), so

\[ B_y + iB_x = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi(z - z_0)} . \]

This is an analytic function everywhere except at the wire. (The combination \(B_x + iB_y\) would have given \(i\) times the conjugate, \(i\mu_0I/(2\pi\delta^*)\), which is not analytic: that is why magnet builders pair the components this way round.) Inside the circle through the wire, its Taylor series is

\[ B_y + iB_x = -\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n-1}}{z_0^{\,n}}, \]

a sum of a uniform field (\(n = 1\)), a field growing as \(z\) (\(n = 2\)), as \(z^2\) (\(n = 3\)), and so on: the dipole, quadrupole, sextupole and higher terms of section 2, all at once, from one wire.

At work. Put one conductor carrying 11 850 A at radius 28 mm and angle 60°, as one turn of a coil, and look at the point 10 mm out on the \(x\) axis. The exact formula gives \(B_y = -15.7\) mT and \(B_x = 95.1\) mT, a field of 96.4 mT. In the series, each term is \(10/28 = 0.357\) times the size of the one before. One term is 36 per cent wrong; three terms 4.6 per cent; five terms 0.58 per cent; ten terms 0.003 per cent. At the LHC’s reference radius of 17 mm the ratio is \(17/28 = 0.61\), and a single wire would need 19 terms to reach one part in \(10^4\). A real coil places its wires symmetrically, and most terms then cancel between wires: section 9 shows how.

Exercise X1. Expand \(1/(z - 2i)\) in powers of \(z\). Give the first four coefficients and the radius of convergence, and compare three terms with the exact value at \(z = 1\).

Answer

With \(z_0 = 2i\) the coefficients \(-1/z_0^n\) are \(-1/(2i) = 0.5i\), \(-1/(2i)^2 = 0.25\), \(-1/(2i)^3 = -0.125i\), \(-1/(2i)^4 = -0.0625\). Exercise X2. Two wires carry \(+I\) at \(z_0\) and \(-I\) at \(-z_0\). Show that inside the circle \(|z| < |z_0|\) their field contains only the terms \(n = 1, 3, 5, \dots\) (dipole, sextupole, decapole).

Pre-requisite (continued): Two-dimensional magnetic potentials and multipole expansion

Pre-requisite. Chapter 7 showed that in a region without current \(B_y + iB_x\) is an analytic function of \(x + iy\), and expanded it into multipoles, normal and skew; chapter 14 took the quadrupole’s potential and its focusing. This section adds the two potentials that belong to such a field: the magnetic scalar potential, whose level curves are the shapes of iron poles, and the vector potential \(A_z\) of the pre-requisite before section 1, whose level curves are the field lines. Both are parts of one analytic function. It then draws the field lines and equipotentials of the dipole, quadrupole and sextupole, and shows that a skew multipole is a normal one turned about the axis, so that a small roll of a magnet makes a small skew error. Section 2 uses the multipoles, section 3 the equipotentials for pole shapes and the ampere-turns per pole, and section 7 and the bench the roll. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, section 3.2 (the magnetic scalar potential, usable only where no current flows), Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, section I.2 (the multipole expansion), and Wilson, Superconducting Magnets, section 3.2 (complex-variable methods for transverse fields).

Counting. Chapter 7 numbered the terms from \(n = 0\) for the dipole. This chapter, like CERN, numbers from \(n = 1\): the \(n\)-th term \(C_n(z/R_r)^{n-1}\) is the field of a \(2n\)-pole, with \(R_r\) a reference radius. The physics is the same; only the label shifts by one.

A scalar potential where no current flows. Choose a reference point \(O\) in the aperture and define, for any point \(P\),

\[ \Phi(P) = -\int_O^P\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{l} \]

(T m). This is a proper function of \(P\) only if the integral does not depend on the path. Two paths from \(O\) to \(P\) together make a closed loop, and round a closed loop \(\oint\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \mu_0I_{enc}\). So if no current threads any loop we can draw, the integral is independent of path, \(\Phi\) exists, and \(\mathbf{B} = -\nabla\Phi\). Since \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0\), the potential obeys Laplace’s equation, \(\nabla^2\Phi = 0\), like the electric potential in empty space.

There is a catch in a magnet. A loop that crosses the gap and returns through the yoke links the coil, and round it the integral is not zero: it is \(\mu_0NI\) (counting with \(\mathbf{H}\) in the iron, as in section 1). Going once round, \(\Phi\) comes back changed by \(\mu_0NI\). The cure is to forbid paths that cross the coil, which makes \(\Phi\) single-valued in what remains, the gap and the iron. Then the difference of potential between two poles, measured across the gap, is \(\mu_0\) times the ampere-turns that drive it, because the iron contributes almost nothing (\(H\) is tiny there). That is the rule section 3 uses for the ampere-turns per pole.

Two potentials, one function. In two dimensions the field can be written both ways:

\[ B_x = -\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial y}, \qquad B_y = -\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial A_z}{\partial x}. \]

Read these as relations between \(A_z\) and \(\Phi\): \(\partial A_z/\partial x = \partial\Phi/\partial y\) and \(\partial A_z/\partial y = -\partial\Phi/\partial x\). They are the Cauchy-Riemann equations for \(u = A_z\), \(v = \Phi\). So \(A_z + i\Phi\) is an analytic function of \(z = x + iy\). Define the complex potential with a minus sign,

\[ \mathcal{F}(z) = -(A_z + i\Phi), \]

because then its derivative is the field. For an analytic function the derivative can be taken along \(x\), so \(d\mathcal{F}/dz = -\partial A_z/\partial x - i\,\partial\Phi/\partial x = B_y + iB_x\). In words: \(A_z = -\mathrm{Re}\,\mathcal{F}\) and \(\Phi = -\mathrm{Im}\,\mathcal{F}\), and the field is \(d\mathcal{F}/dz\). Section 3 uses exactly this. (The letter \(\mathcal{F}\) is calligraphic because \(W\) is kinetic energy in this course.)

The contours of \(A_z\) are the field lines; the contours of \(\Phi\) are the equipotentials. The field is the downhill slope of \(\Phi\), so it crosses the equipotentials at right angles: the two families of curves meet everywhere at right angles. And the surface of good iron, which the field leaves at right angles (the pre-requisite before section 1), is an equipotential.

The multipoles drawn. A normal \(2n\)-pole has \(B_y + iB_x = B_n(z/R_r)^{n-1}\) with \(B_n\) real. Integrating,

\[ \mathcal{F} = \frac{B_nR_r}{n}\left(\frac{z}{R_r}\right)^n, \qquad A_z = -\frac{B_nR_r}{n}\left(\frac{r}{R_r}\right)^n\cos n\theta, \qquad \Phi = -\frac{B_nR_r}{n}\left(\frac{r}{R_r}\right)^n\sin n\theta, \]

and the size of the field, \(|B| = |B_n|(r/R_r)^{n-1}\), is the same at every angle round the axis.

  • Dipole, \(n = 1\). \(A_z = -B_1x\), so the field lines are the vertical lines \(x = \text{constant}\). \(\Phi = -B_1y\), so the equipotentials are horizontal lines: flat, parallel poles.
  • Quadrupole, \(n = 2\), with gradient \(g = B_2/R_r\) (T/m). \(A_z = -g(x^2 - y^2)/2\) and \(\Phi = -gxy\). The field lines are the hyperbolas \(x^2 - y^2 = \text{constant}\), opening along the axes; the equipotentials are the hyperbolas \(xy = \text{constant}\), which have their tips on the diagonals. The four poles sit on the diagonals, and field lines run from each pole to its two neighbours. On the \(x\) axis the field is vertical, on the \(y\) axis horizontal.
  • Sextupole, \(n = 3\), with \(B'' = 2B_3/R_r^2\) (T/m²). \(A_z = -(B''/6)(x^3 - 3xy^2)\) and \(\Phi = -(B''/6)(3x^2y - y^3)\). The equipotentials reach closest to the axis where \(\sin 3\theta = \pm1\), at \(\theta = 30°, 90°, 150°\) and so on: six poles, 60° apart.

Figure 17.3. The three normal multipoles in cross-section, computed from the formulas above. The blue field lines are contours of \(A_z \propto r^n\cos n\theta\); the grey pole faces are contours of \(\Phi \propto r^n\sin n\theta\), all with their tips at the same radius, and they meet the field lines at right angles. N marks a pole the field leaves, S one it enters, for \(B_n > 0\): the dipole’s field points up, the quadrupole’s is vertical on the \(x\) axis and horizontal on the \(y\) axis.

Normal and skew are the same magnet turned. Turn a magnet anticlockwise by an angle \(\varphi\) about its axis. Writing the field as the complex number \(b = B_x + iB_y\), the new field at \(z\) is the old field found at the point that the turn carried to \(z\), namely \(ze^{-i\varphi}\), itself turned by \(\varphi\): \(b_{new}(z) = e^{i\varphi}b_{old}(ze^{-i\varphi})\). (Multiplying a complex number by \(e^{i\varphi}\) turns it anticlockwise by \(\varphi\) about the origin, F5, section 2. So the point that the turn carries to \(z\) started at \(ze^{-i\varphi}\), and the field arrow carried with the magnet is turned as well, which multiplies \(b\) by \(e^{i\varphi}\).) Our combination is \(B_y + iB_x = i\,b^*\), so taking conjugates and multiplying by \(i\),

\[ (B_y + iB_x)_{new}(z) = e^{-i\varphi}\,(B_y + iB_x)_{old}(ze^{-i\varphi}). \]

For the \(2n\)-pole \(C_n(z/R_r)^{n-1}\) this gives \(e^{-i\varphi}C_n(ze^{-i\varphi}/R_r)^{n-1} = C_ne^{-in\varphi}(z/R_r)^{n-1}\). Turning multiplies the coefficient by \(e^{-in\varphi}\). Turn a normal magnet (\(C_n = B_n\), real) clockwise by \(\pi/(2n)\), that is \(\varphi = -\pi/(2n)\): the coefficient becomes \(B_ne^{i\pi/2} = iB_n\), purely imaginary, a pure skew magnet. A skew dipole is a normal one turned by 90° (a horizontal field); a skew quadrupole is turned by 45°; a skew sextupole by 30°.

A small roll \(\varphi\) gives \(C_n \approx B_n(1 - in\varphi)\), since \(e^{-in\varphi} \approx 1 - in\varphi\) for a small angle (the first two terms of its Taylor series, F1, section 8): a skew component \(A_n = -n\varphi B_n\). In units of \(10^{-4}\) of the main term, \(a_n = -n\varphi\times10^4\). A quadrupole rolled by 1 mrad has \(a_2 = -20\) units, a skew quadrupole error that couples horizontal and vertical motion (chapter 18). Turned round, this is a measurement: the phase of the main harmonic seen by a rotating coil gives the magnet’s roll (section 7 and the bench).

2 Two-dimensional fields and the multipole expansion

The picture. Inside a long magnet, away from its ends, the field does not depend on \(s\) and has only \(x\) and \(y\) components. A beam sees the field integrated along its path, so the two-dimensional field of the straight body is what matters most. In a region with no current, such a field is both divergence-free and curl-free, and that makes it the derivative of an analytic function of \(z = x + iy\).

The derivation. In the current-free aperture \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0\), and \(\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{J} = 0\) (Ampère’s law at a point, chapter 10; the curl is F2, section 11). With the field in the \((x, y)\) plane and independent of \(s\), only the \(z\) component of the curl survives, and the two laws read

\[ \frac{\partial B_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial B_y}{\partial y} = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial B_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial B_x}{\partial y} = 0. \]

Let \(F = u + iv\) with \(u = B_y\) and \(v = B_x\). The two equations read \(u_x = v_y\) and \(u_y = -v_x\), the Cauchy-Riemann equations of chapter 7. So \(F(z) = B_y + iB_x\) is an analytic function of \(z\) in the aperture, and it has a Taylor series about the centre. Writing the coefficients so that each has the unit of tesla,

\[ B_y + iB_x = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} C_n\left(\frac{z}{R_r}\right)^{n-1}, \qquad C_n = B_n + iA_n, \]

with \(R_r\) a chosen reference radius (m). This is the convention used at CERN and in Walckiers’ lectures (in this chapter \(n\) counts multipoles; it is not the weak-focusing field index): \(n = 1\) is the dipole, \(n = 2\) the quadrupole, \(n = 3\) the sextupole. American texts often count from \(n = 0\) for the dipole; check before comparing numbers. The \(B_n\) are the normal terms, which give a vertical field on the horizontal midplane, and the \(A_n\) the skew terms, which are the normal ones rotated by \(\pi/(2n)\).

Two cases make the notation concrete. A pure normal quadrupole has \(C_2 = gR_r\) with \(g\) the gradient (T/m); then \(B_y + iB_x = g(x + iy)\), so \(B_y = gx\) and \(B_x = gy\), the field of chapter 16. A pure normal sextupole has \(B_y + iB_x = (B''/2)(x + iy)^2\), so \(B_y = (B''/2)(x^2 - y^2)\) and \(B_x = B''xy\), with \(B'' = \partial^2 B_y/\partial x^2\) (T/m\(^2\)).

Field quality. A real dipole has small higher terms. They are quoted relative to the main term, in units of \(10^{-4}\), at the reference radius:

\[ b_n + i a_n = 10^4\,\frac{C_n}{B_1}. \]

The reference radius is usually about two-thirds of the iron or coil aperture, near the edge of the region the beam uses (Walckiers). The choice matters: the \(n\)-th term grows as \((r/R_r)^{n-1}\), so a sextupole error of one unit is one part in \(10^4\) at \(R_r\) and a quarter of that at half the radius.

Worked number. A dipole at 8.33 T with \(b_3 = 1\) unit at \(R_r = 17\) mm (the LHC’s reference radius; the Tevatron and HERA used 25 mm, Schmüser in the Handbook, section 7.2.4) has a field error of 0.83 mT at 17 mm and 0.29 mT at 10 mm. What such errors do to a beam is chapter 18.

3 Pole shapes from the scalar potential

The picture. Where there is no current, \(\mathbf{B} = -\nabla\Phi\) for a scalar potential \(\Phi\) (T m). Inside iron of very high permeability \(H\) is nearly zero, so the tangential \(H\) just outside the iron is nearly zero too (it is continuous across the surface). The field leaves the iron at right angles, which means the iron surface is an equipotential of \(\Phi\). So to make a field, shape the iron along that field’s equipotentials.

The derivation. Let \(\mathcal{F}(z) = u + iv\) be analytic with \(d\mathcal{F}/dz = B_y + iB_x\) (a calligraphic letter, because \(W\) is kinetic energy). Then \(u_x + iv_x = B_y + iB_x\), so \(v_x = B_x\), and by Cauchy-Riemann \(v_y = u_x = B_y\). Hence \(\mathbf{B} = \nabla v\), and the scalar potential is \(\Phi = -v = -\mathrm{Im}\,\mathcal{F}\). For a pure normal \(2n\)-pole,

\[ \frac{d\mathcal{F}}{dz} = B_n\left(\frac{z}{R_r}\right)^{n-1} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Phi = -\frac{B_n R_r}{n}\left(\frac{r}{R_r}\right)^{n}\sin n\theta, \]

with \(z = re^{i\theta}\). The equipotentials are the curves \(r^n\sin n\theta = \text{constant}\):

  • \(n = 1\), dipole: \(r\sin\theta = y = \pm G/2\). Two flat, parallel poles.
  • \(n = 2\), quadrupole: \(r^2\sin 2\theta = 2xy\), so \(xy = \pm r_0^2/2\). Four rectangular hyperbolas, with their tips at \(r_0\) on the diagonals.
  • \(n = 3\), sextupole: \(\mathrm{Im}\,z^3 = 3x^2y - y^3 = \pm r_0^3\). Six poles, tips at \(\theta = 30^\circ, 90^\circ, 150^\circ\) and so on.

A combined-function magnet, with \(B_y = B_0 + gx\) on the midplane, has \(\mathcal{F} = B_0 z + gz^2/2\) and \(\Phi = -y(B_0 + gx)\), so its poles follow \(y = \text{constant}/(B_0 + gx)\): a gap that narrows towards the side where the field is higher.

Ampere-turns per pole. Run Ampère’s law from the centre out along a pole’s axis to its tip at radius \(r_0\), through the iron (where \(H \approx 0\)), and back to the centre along a symmetry line between poles (where the field is perpendicular to the path, so it adds nothing). The loop encloses one pole’s coil, and

\[ N I = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\left|\Phi(r_0) - \Phi(0)\right| = \frac{B_n R_r}{n\mu_0}\left(\frac{r_0}{R_r}\right)^{n} = \frac{B_{tip}\, r_0}{n\,\mu_0}, \]

with \(B_{tip}\) the field at the pole tip. For a dipole (\(n = 1\), \(r_0 = G/2\)) this is half the total of section 1, as it must be. For a quadrupole, \(NI = g r_0^2/(2\mu_0)\): a 20 T/m quadrupole with \(r_0 = 40\) mm has a pole-tip field of 0.8 T and needs 12.7 kA turns per pole.

Real poles. A pole must stop somewhere. Cutting off the ideal hyperbola, and its finite width, adds the terms that the symmetry allows: for a dipole \(b_3, b_5, b_7, \ldots\), for a quadrupole \(b_6, b_{10}, \ldots\), for a sextupole \(b_9, b_{15}, \ldots\), the \(n(2k+1)\)-th harmonics in general (Zickler). The designer adds small shims at the pole edges, thin strips of steel that build the pole out a little where the field would sag, to cancel the lowest of them.

Pre-requisite (continued): Faraday induction, inductance and magnetic energy

Pre-requisite. Chapter 1 gave Faraday’s law and magnetic flux; chapter 9 inductance, \(V = L\,dI/dt\) and the stored energy \(\tfrac12LI^2\); chapter 11 Lenz’s law and the eddy currents that oppose a change of flux in iron. This section adds a wire or coil that moves through a steady field, which is how magnets are measured; the integrator, which turns the induced voltage into a change of flux; and the stored energy as the integral of \(B^2/2\mu_0\) over all space, which gives a magnet’s inductance from its field. Section 4 uses Faraday’s law for eddy currents, section 7 the moving coil and wire, and section 11 the energy and inductance. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, sections 4.1 (Faraday’s law), 4.1.1 (Faraday’s law for moving wires) and 4.1.2 (inductance and magnetostatic energy), and Wilson, Superconducting Magnets, section 10.7 (search coils and integrators).

A wire moving through a steady field. A straight bar of length \(\ell\) (m) slides at speed \(v\) along two rails, closing a circuit, in a uniform field \(B\) at right angles to the plane of the rails. Nothing changes in time except the position of the bar, so there is no induced electric field. But every charge in the bar moves with it and feels the force \(q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\), of size \(qvB\), along the bar. The work per unit charge carried along the bar is the emf (electromotive force, F4, section 14), \(vB\ell\). Meanwhile the area of the circuit grows at \(\ell v\) per second, so the flux through it grows at \(B\ell v\). The emf equals the rate of change of flux:

\[ \text{emf} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \]

again, now for a moving circuit in a steady field. Tong (section 4.1.1) shows that this holds for any shape and any motion: whether the field changes, or the circuit moves, or both, the emf round the circuit is minus the rate of change of the flux through it. So a coil carried through a magnet reports the change of flux it links, whatever caused the change.

The integrator. For a winding of \(N\) turns, \(V(t) = -N\,d\Phi_B/dt\). Integrate over time:

\[ \int_{t_1}^{t_2}V\,dt = -N\left[\Phi_B(t_2) - \Phi_B(t_1)\right]. \]

The result depends only on the flux at the start and at the end, not on how fast or how steadily the coil moved. An electronic integrator (an amplifier charging a capacitor, or today a fast digitiser summing samples) gives the integral directly, in volt seconds, which are webers. Speed drops out, which is why moving-coil and moving-wire measurements are accurate: Wilson (section 10.7) describes search coils with integrators, and a pair wired in series opposition to measure small differences. The enemy is any steady offset voltage at the input, which the integrator adds up along with the signal: an offset of 1 µV over a 3 s measurement adds 3 µV s, so integrators are zeroed before each measurement and measurements are made both ways and averaged.

At work: the stretched wire. A wire stretched along an iron dipole of 1.5 T and 2 m, \(\int B\,ds = 3\) T m, moved sideways by 1 mm, sweeps through a flux of \(3\times10^{-3}\) Wb. The integrator reads 3 mV s. To measure the field integral to \(10^{-4}\), the integrator must be good to 0.3 µV s, and the stages that move the wire to 0.1 µm in 1 mm.

A rotating coil. A long, narrow coil of \(N\) turns and length \(L\) has its two long sides parallel to the magnet’s axis, at radii \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) on the same radial line at angle \(\theta\). By the flux rule for \(A_z\), the flux it links is \(N L[A_z(R_2, \theta) - A_z(R_1, \theta)]\), up to a sign set by the sense of winding. In a normal \(2n\)-pole, \(A_z = -(B_nR_r/n)(r/R_r)^n\cos n\theta\) (the potentials above), so

\[ \Psi(\theta) = -\frac{NLB_n}{n}\,\frac{R_2^{\,n} - R_1^{\,n}}{R_r^{\,n-1}}\cos n\theta . \]

Turn the coil at a steady angular speed \(\omega\), \(\theta = \omega t\). The voltage \(-d\Psi/dt\) is a sine at \(n\omega\), of amplitude

\[ \hat{V}_n = NL\omega B_n\,\frac{R_2^{\,n} - R_1^{\,n}}{R_r^{\,n-1}} . \]

A \(2n\)-pole shows up at \(n\) times the rotation frequency. A real magnet holds all its multipoles at once, so the coil’s signal is a sum of harmonics in \(\theta\), a Fourier series, and each harmonic gives one multipole. Section 7 writes this as \(\Psi = \mathrm{Re}\sum K_nC_ne^{in\theta}\). With \(N = 20\), \(L = 1\) m, \(R_1 = 0\) and \(R_2 = 12\) mm, one turn per second in a 1 T dipole gives 1.51 V at 1 Hz; the same coil in a 20 T/m quadrupole (\(B_2 = gR_r\)) gives \(NL\omega gR_2^2 = 0.362\) V at 2 Hz.

The energy is in the field. Chapter 9 showed for a long solenoid that \(\tfrac12LI^2\) equals \(B^2/2\mu_0\) times the volume inside. Tong (section 4.1.2) shows that this holds for any arrangement of currents: the energy stored is

\[ U = \int\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}\,dV \]

over all space (in iron of constant permeability, \(B^2/(2\mu_0\mu_r)\), which is why the iron of an unsaturated magnet holds almost none of it). Turned round, a field map gives the inductance, \(L = 2U/I^2\), without any thought about which turns link which flux. At 8.33 T the energy density is 27.6 MJ/m³, the same number as the magnetic pressure, because both are \(B^2/2\mu_0\).

Pre-requisite (continued): Fields in conductors, skin depth, surface resistance

Pre-requisite. Chapter 13 taught Ohm’s law in a conductor, the surface current that carries a field at a metal wall, and the skin depth into which an alternating field penetrates. This section derives the equation behind the skin depth, the diffusion equation for a magnetic field in a conductor, and solves it for a sheet whose outside field is switched on or ramped: the field soaks in with a time constant \(\mu_0\mu_rd^2/(\pi^2\rho_e)\), and the middle of the sheet lags a ramp by a calculable amount. Section 4 uses it for laminations, solid yokes and the vacuum chamber; the same time appears for copper in a quench. Taught from Wilson, Superconducting Magnets, section 7.4 (thermal and magnetic diffusion: characteristic times), and Tong, Electromagnetism, section 8.6 (conductors and the skin depth), with the eddy currents of the pre-requisite above.

The idea. A conductor resists a change of the field inside it. Any change induces currents that oppose it (Lenz’s law), so at first the field is held out. But those currents flow through a resistance and die away, and as they die the field creeps in. The process is the same as heat soaking into a cold slab, and it obeys the same equation.

The diffusion equation. Three laws, for changes slow enough that the displacement current can be dropped (true below optical frequencies in any metal):

  • Ampère: \(\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}\);
  • Ohm: \(\mathbf{J} = \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}/\rho_e\), with \(\rho_e\) the resistivity (\(\Omega\) m);
  • Faraday: \(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t\).

Let the material be uniform with \(\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}\), \(\mu = \mu_0\mu_r\) constant (iron below saturation). Take the curl of Ampère’s law and use the other two:

\[ \nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{B}) = \mu\,\nabla\times\mathbf{J} = \frac{\mu}{\rho_e}\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\frac{\mu}{\rho_e}\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}. \]

The vector identity \(\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{B}) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}) - \nabla^2\mathbf{B}\), checked component by component in chapter 13, with \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0\), turns the left side into \(-\nabla^2\mathbf{B}\). So

\[ \frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t} = D_m\nabla^2\mathbf{B}, \qquad D_m = \frac{\rho_e}{\mu_0\mu_r}\quad(\text{m}^2/\text{s}). \]

This is the heat equation, the same as the diffusion equation of chapter 3, with \(\mathbf{B}\) in place of temperature and the magnetic diffusivity \(D_m\) in place of the thermal one (Wilson, section 7.4, sets the two side by side). For a sheet of thickness \(d\) with the field along its faces, varying only across the thickness \(x\), it reads \(\partial B/\partial t = D_m\,\partial^2B/\partial x^2\).

A field switched on. At \(t = 0\) the field outside the sheet (\(0 < x < d\)) jumps from zero to \(B_a\) and stays there. The solution is

\[ B(x, t) = B_a - \sum_{k = 1, 3, 5, \dots}\frac{4B_a}{k\pi}\sin\frac{k\pi x}{d}\,e^{-k^2t/\tau_1}, \qquad \tau_1 = \frac{d^2}{\pi^2D_m} = \frac{\mu_0\mu_rd^2}{\pi^2\rho_e}. \]

Check it in three steps. Each term satisfies the equation: the second derivative in \(x\) brings down \(-(k\pi/d)^2\), the time derivative \(-k^2/\tau_1 = -D_m(k\pi/d)^2\), the same. At the faces, \(x = 0\) and \(x = d\), every sine is zero and \(B = B_a\). At \(t = 0\) the sum is the Fourier sine series of a constant on \(0 < x < d\), which equals \(B_a\) inside, so \(B = 0\) inside. The coefficients come out as in chapter 12: multiply the series for 1 by \(\sin(k\pi x/d)\) and integrate across the sheet, and orthogonality leaves the coefficient \(s_k = (2/d)\int_0^d\sin(k\pi x/d)\,dx = (2/k\pi)(1 - \cos k\pi)\), which is \(4/k\pi\) for odd \(k\) and zero for even \(k\). The higher terms die \(k^2\) times faster, so after about \(\tau_1\) only the first is left, and at the mid-plane

\[ B\left(\tfrac{d}{2}, t\right) \approx B_a\left(1 - \frac{4}{\pi}e^{-t/\tau_1}\right). \]

The mid-plane reaches 53 per cent of the outside field at \(\tau_1\), 83 per cent at \(2\tau_1\) and 94 per cent at \(3\tau_1\) (the full series gives the same to three figures). A rough rule: in a time \(t\) the field reaches a depth of order \(\sqrt{D_mt}\), so the time grows as the square of the thickness.

Figure 17.4. A field switched on outside a sheet soaks in, computed from the series above (eight terms). At \(0.05\,\tau_1\) it has entered only a thin skin at each face; at \(\tau_1\) the mid-plane has 53 per cent of the outside field, at \(2\tau_1\) 83 per cent and at \(3\tau_1\) 94 per cent. The profiles have the shape of heat soaking into a cold slab.

A field ramped steadily. Now let the outside field rise at a steady rate \(\dot{B}\). After a few \(\tau_1\) the profile stops changing shape and simply rises at \(\dot{B}\) everywhere. Then \(D_m\,\partial^2B/\partial x^2 = \dot{B}\), a parabola that equals the outside field at both faces:

\[ B(x, t) = B_a(t) - \frac{\dot{B}}{2D_m}\,x(d - x). \]

The mid-plane lags behind the outside by \(\Delta B = \dot{B}d^2/(8D_m) = \dot{B}\mu_0\mu_rd^2/(8\rho_e)\), which is a delay in time of \(\mu_0\mu_rd^2/(8\rho_e) = (\pi^2/8)\tau_1\). The eddy-current loss of section 4 assumes this lag is negligible, that is, a sheet thin enough that \(\tau_1\) is short beside the ramp.

The link to the skin depth. For a field oscillating at angular frequency \(\omega\), the same equation gives the skin depth of chapter 13, \(\delta = \sqrt{2D_m/\omega} = \sqrt{2\rho_e/(\omega\mu_0\mu_r)}\). Since \(\tau_1 = d^2/(\pi^2D_m)\), \(\omega\tau_1 = 2d^2/(\pi^2\delta^2)\): a sheet is thin, with the field nearly uniform through it, when \(d \ll \delta\), which is the same as \(\omega\tau_1 \ll 1\). For steel at 50 Hz with \(\mu_r = 1000\) and \(\rho_e = 2.5\times10^{-7}\) \(\Omega\) m, \(\delta = 1.1\) mm and a 1 mm sheet has \(\omega\tau_1 = 0.16\).

A stainless-steel chamber lets the field through almost at once, but the eddy currents it carries during the ramp still make a field error (section 4). Answer

\(\tau_1\) is inversely proportional to \(\rho_e\): 82 ms at zero field, 27 ms at three times the resistivity. A change over 0.1 s is a few \(\tau_1\) long in the high-field case and about one \(\tau_1\) at zero field, so the plate holds back a large part of the change and carries eddy currents, and their heat, for the whole time.

4 Iron: saturation, hysteresis and eddy currents

Saturation. The relative permeability of soft steel is in the thousands at low field and falls steeply as \(B\) approaches the saturation of 1.6 to 2 T. The pole tips saturate first, because the flux crowds there, and their effective shape changes: a quadrupole’s gradient falls and its \(b_6\) shifts as the current rises; a C-shaped dipole develops a quadrupole term. The practical rule is a pole-tip field of about 1 T in quadrupoles and dipole fields below about 1.8 T: S. Y. Lee puts the attainable field of an iron dipole at about 1.8 T, with the iron saturating near 1.7 T, and the usual quadrupole pole-tip field below 0.9 T (Accelerator Physics, chapter 1, section II.2 and exercise 1.12). Zickler advises keeping the flux in the yoke below about 1.5 T, and Lebrun puts the limit for iron-dominated magnets at below 2 T.

Hysteresis and remanence. Iron remembers. Its \(B\)-\(H\) curve is a loop, and the flux at a given current depends on where the current has been. When the coil current returns to zero, Ampère’s law gives \(H_{gap}\,G + H_{Fe}\,l_{Fe} = 0\). The iron sits near its coercive field \(-H_c\), so the gap keeps a remanent field

\[ B_{rem} \approx \frac{\mu_0 H_c\, l_{Fe}}{G}. \]

The Handbook’s table of magnet steels (Tigner, Handbook of Accelerator Physics and Engineering, section 6.3) lists coercive forces of 40 to 80 A/m for low-carbon and silicon steels. With \(H_c\) of 50 A/m, \(l_{Fe} = 1\) m and \(G = 50\) mm, \(B_{rem} = 1.3\) mT; with 100 A/m, 2.5 mT. That is 1.3 to 2.5 per cent of a 0.1 T injection field. The remedy is the standard cycle: before use, and after any change, every magnet in a ring is ramped to its maximum and back several times, and set points are always approached from the same side, so that each magnet sits on the same branch of its loop and the field is reproducible. Where the current is pulsed, the cycle repeats itself every pulse.

Eddy currents and laminations. A changing field induces currents in the iron that oppose the change, waste power and delay the field. Take a sheet of thickness \(d\) (m) and resistivity \(\rho_e\) (\(\Omega\) m) in a uniform field rising at \(\dot{B}\) (T/s) parallel to its faces, and assume the sheet is thin enough that the eddy currents do not change the field inside it (the diffusion time below is short compared with the ramp). Faraday’s law gives an induced electric field growing linearly from the mid-plane, and averaging the dissipation over the thickness gives the power per unit volume \(P_e\) (W/m\(^3\)), derived in chapter 15, section 12 (written there with the conductivity \(1/\rho_e\)):

\[ P_e = \frac{d^2\dot{B}^2}{12\rho_e}. \]

The loss goes as the square of the thickness. With an assumed \(\rho_e = 2.5\times10^{-7}\) \(\Omega\) m, 1 mm sheet at 10 T/s dissipates 33 W/m\(^3\); a solid 100 mm block would dissipate \(3.3\times10^5\) W/m\(^3\). The field also takes a time of order \(\mu_0\mu_r d^2/(\pi^2\rho_e)\) to diffuse into a block: 0.5 ms for 1 mm with \(\mu_r = 1000\), but 1.3 s for 50 mm. So the yokes of all cycling magnets are stacked from insulated steel sheets about a millimetre thick, and the vacuum chamber is made thin, because the eddy currents in its wall make a sextupole field during the ramp (that error is in chapter 18). Hysteresis adds a loss per cycle proportional to the area of the \(B\)-\(H\) loop.

5 Yokes and lattices

Shapes. A C-shaped dipole has one return leg, so the gap is open on one side: the vacuum chamber can be slid in, and in an electron ring the synchrotron light can leave. It is asymmetric, and the iron leg bends a little under the magnetic pull. An H-shaped dipole has two return legs, is symmetric and stiffer, but closed. A window-frame dipole puts the coils inside the rectangular window of the yoke, filling the gap’s width: the field is very uniform from wall to wall, but the coils sit beside the beam and must be accurately placed. Quadrupoles and sextupoles are four- and six-legged versions of the H, often split in halves to admit the vacuum chamber. Figure 17.5 draws the three dipoles side by side.

Figure 17.5. The three shapes of iron dipole, seen along the beam, which comes out of the page (schematic, not to scale). In each the gap field points up and the coil current runs into the page on the right and out of it on the left, as in figure 17.2. The C returns its flux through one leg and is open on the other side; the H returns it through two legs, symmetric and closed; the window frame puts its coils in the window beside the gap, so the field runs evenly from one coil to the other.

Combined against separated function. In a combined-function magnet the pole is tilted so that one magnet both bends and focuses (\(B_y = B_0 + gx\)). The CERN PS of 1959 was built this way: 100 magnet units of 4.4 m nominal length, about 4000 tonnes of them, each a sequence of focusing and defocusing halves. Its bending radius is about \(100 \times 4.4\ \text{m}/2\pi = 70\) m, so at 25 GeV kinetic energy, \(p = 25.9\) GeV/\(c\), the field on the orbit is about 1.23 T. In a separated-function lattice, dipoles bend and quadrupoles focus. G. T. Danby and colleagues at Brookhaven argued in 1967 that this was the better choice for a very high energy synchrotron. The dipoles then have flat poles that can run at full field everywhere across the gap, the quadrupoles can be set independently, and saturation in one family does not upset the other. The Fermilab Main Ring and the CERN SPS were built separated-function, and so has every large ring since. The optics that the two kinds of lattice give are chapter 16.

6 Copper coils, power and cooling

The picture. The coil must carry the ampere-turns of section 1. Its copper can be thick, carrying a low current density with little loss but costing more metal, or thin, carrying a high current density, costing less metal but more electricity and more cooling water.

The power. A coil of total ampere-turns \(NI\), conductor current density \(J\) (A/m\(^2\)), mean turn length \(l_t\) (m) and resistivity \(\rho_e\) has copper cross-section \(NI/J\) and volume \((NI/J)\,l_t\). The power is \(\rho_e J^2\) times the volume:

\[ P = \rho_e\, J\,(NI)\, l_t. \]

Neither \(N\) nor \(I\) appears separately. Once the field (and so \(NI\)) and the coil’s size are fixed, the only choice that changes the power is the current density. The number of turns sets only how the power is split between current and voltage, \(P = VI\), which is a choice made to suit the power supply.

Worked numbers. Take the 1.5 T, 50 mm dipole of section 1 with \(NI = 60.9\) kA turns, a mean turn length of 4.6 m (a 2 m magnet) and copper at \(1.72\times10^{-8}\) \(\Omega\) m:

\(J\) (A/mm\(^2\)) copper area (mm\(^2\)) copper mass (kg) power (kW) water at \(\Delta T = 20\) K (l/min)
2 30 400 1250 9.6 6.9
5 12 200 500 24.1 17.3
10 6100 250 48.2 34.5

The copper mass takes a density of 8960 kg/m\(^3\). The water flow is \(\dot{m} = P/(c_p\Delta T)\) with \(c_p = 4186\) J/(kg K), and 1 kg of water taken as 1 l. At 5 A/mm\(^2\) the coil could be 61 turns at 1000 A and 24 V, or 122 turns at 500 A and 48 V. Such coils are wound from hollow copper with water flowing in the hole, the circuit divided into parallel branches so that the water does not warm too much along one path. Multiply by a thousand magnets running all year and the electricity bill, and the cooling plant, become a large part of a laboratory’s budget. This is the second reason, beside size, that large rings went superconducting.

Pre-requisite (continued): Electrons in metals, Fermi-Dirac statistics, work function

Pre-requisite. Chapter 2 pictured the conduction electrons of a metal as a gas that fills the available states up to the Fermi level, with the Fermi-Dirac distribution. This section uses that picture for three properties this chapter needs: the Hall voltage across a plate that carries a current in a field, which makes a field probe; the resistivity of copper, which falls on cooling to a floor set by impurities and measured by the residual resistivity ratio; and the heat capacity near absolute zero, where the electrons give a part proportional to \(T\) and the vibrating lattice a part proportional to \(T^3\). Section 7 uses the Hall probe, sections 6 and 11 the resistivity, and sections 11 and 13 the heat capacity. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 5, §57 and §58 (the degenerate electron gas and its specific heat) and §64 and §66 (solids at low temperatures, Debye’s interpolation formula); Tong, Electromagnetism, section 8.6 (the Drude model of conduction); Wilson, Superconducting Magnets, sections 5.2 (distributed disturbances, with the specific heats of magnet materials at low temperature) and 6.3 (resistivity of the normal metal); and Livingston and Blewett, section 8-7 (the Hall effect in field measurement).

Drift. A current in a metal is a slow drift of the conduction electrons on top of their fast, random motion near the Fermi speed. With \(n_c\) carriers per cubic metre, each of charge \(q\), drifting at \(v_d\), the current density is \(J = n_cqv_d\). Copper has one conduction electron per atom, \(n_c = 8.49\times10^{28}\) m⁻³. At the 5 A/mm² of a water-cooled coil the drift is \(v_d = 0.37\) mm/s, while the electrons’ random speed is about \(1.6\times10^6\) m/s.

The Hall effect. Take a thin plate of thickness \(t_H\) (along the field) and width \(w\), carrying a current \(I_H\) along its length, with a field \(B\) through it at right angles. Each drifting carrier feels a sideways force \(qv_dB\). Carriers pile up on one edge, and the opposite charge is left on the other, until the electric field \(\mathcal{E}_H\) between the edges pushes back just as hard: \(q\mathcal{E}_H = qv_dB\). The voltage across the width is \(V_H = \mathcal{E}_Hw = v_dBw\). Since \(I_H = n_cqv_d\,wt_H\), the width cancels:

\[ V_H = \frac{I_HB}{n_cqt_H} . \]

E. H. Hall found it in 1879; the sign of \(V_H\) tells the sign of the carriers. The voltage is proportional to \(B\), which makes a field probe, and inversely proportional to the carrier density, which rules out metals: a copper plate 0.1 mm thick carrying 0.1 A in 1 T gives only 74 nV. Semiconductors such as indium arsenide and indium antimonide have far fewer carriers. A sensitivity of 1 V per ampere per tesla needs \(n_ct_H = 1/(q\times1\ \text{V/(A T)}) = 6.2\times10^{18}\) m⁻². The indium arsenide plates that Livingston and Blewett describe in 1961 gave 20 mV per 0.1 T at 0.2 A, which is just that; Wilson quotes commercial probes giving 50 mV at 20 mA in 10 T, 0.25 V/(A T). In a semiconductor the number of carriers and the ease with which they move both change with temperature, so the calibration drifts with temperature; that is why section 7 calibrates Hall probes against NMR.

Resistivity: why it falls and where it stops. In the Drude picture (Tong, section 8.6) an electron is accelerated by the field between collisions, a mean time \(\tau_c\) apart, and the resistivity is

\[ \rho_e = \frac{m_e}{n_ce^2\tau_c} . \]

For copper at 293 K, \(\rho_e = 1.72\times10^{-8}\) \(\Omega\) m gives \(\tau_c = 2.4\times10^{-14}\) s and, at the Fermi speed, a mean free path of 38 nm. Two kinds of obstacle scatter the electrons: vibrations of the lattice, which die away on cooling, and fixed imperfections (impurity atoms, dislocations, grain boundaries), which do not. Collision rates from independent causes add, and so do the resistivities they produce. This is Matthiessen’s rule (Wilson, section 6.3):

\[ \rho_e(T) = \rho_i + \rho_{ph}(T), \]

with \(\rho_i\) the residual part from imperfections and \(\rho_{ph}\) the part from lattice vibrations. The lattice part is proportional to \(T\) near room temperature and falls as \(T^5\) far below it. The Bloch-Grüneisen formula joins the two:

\[ \rho_{ph}(T) = A\left(\frac{T}{\Theta_R}\right)^5\int_0^{\Theta_R/T}\frac{x^5\,dx}{(e^x - 1)(1 - e^{-x})}, \]

with \(\Theta_R \approx 343\) K for copper and \(A\) fixed by \(\rho_e(273\ \text{K}) = 1.54\times10^{-8}\) \(\Omega\) m. It gives \(1.72\times10^{-8}\) \(\Omega\) m at 300 K, \(1.9\times10^{-9}\) at 77 K, \(7\times10^{-12}\) at 20 K and \(3\times10^{-15}\) at 4.2 K. Below about 20 K the lattice part is negligible, and what is left is \(\rho_i\). The residual resistivity ratio, \(\mathrm{RRR} = \rho_e(273\ \text{K})/\rho_e(4.2\ \text{K})\) (some authors use room temperature in the numerator, which changes it by about 10 per cent), measures the purity and the annealing of the copper. With RRR = 100, \(\rho_i = 1.54\times10^{-10}\) \(\Omega\) m, and the two parts are equal near 38 K. In a magnetic field the resistivity rises again (magnetoresistance); Wilson shows that in 6 T the advantage of ultra-pure copper over ordinary oxygen-free copper shrinks from a factor of 20 to less than 2, which is why magnet conductors use ordinary oxygen-free copper.

Heat capacity near absolute zero. Two things in a metal can take up heat: the vibrations of the lattice and the conduction electrons. Both freeze out on cooling, in different ways.

The lattice. Well below the Debye temperature \(\Theta_D\) only long sound waves are excited. Counting them as one counts the photons of black-body radiation, with three polarisations instead of two, gives Debye’s \(T^3\) law (Landau and Lifshitz, §64 and §66):

\[ C_{lat} = \frac{12\pi^4}{5}\,n_ak_B\left(\frac{T}{\Theta_D}\right)^3 = c_{lat}T^3, \]

per unit volume, with \(n_a\) atoms per cubic metre. For copper, \(n_a = 8.49\times10^{28}\) m⁻³ and \(\Theta_D = 343\) K give \(c_{lat} = 6.79\) J m⁻³ K⁻⁴. (The coefficients are written \(c_{lat}\) and \(c_e\) here, not the \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) of many solid-state books, because this course keeps \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) for the relativistic factors.)

The electrons. The Pauli principle forbids most of them from taking up a little energy, because the states just above them are full. Only those within about \(k_BT\) of the Fermi level can be excited. There are about \(n_c\,k_BT/E_F\) of them, each taking about \(k_BT\), so the thermal energy is about \(n_c(k_BT)^2/E_F\) and its slope is proportional to \(T\). The exact calculation (Landau and Lifshitz, §58) gives

\[ C_e = \frac{\pi^2}{2}\,n_ck_B\,\frac{k_BT}{E_F} = c_eT . \]

With \(E_F = 7.0\) eV this is 71 J m⁻³ K⁻² for copper; the measured value, 0.695 mJ mol⁻¹ K⁻², is 98 J m⁻³ K⁻², the difference coming from the electrons’ interactions with the lattice and each other, which the free-gas picture leaves out. The numbers below use the measured \(c_e = 98\) J m⁻³ K⁻².

The two parts are equal where \(c_eT = c_{lat}T^3\), at \(T = \sqrt{c_e/c_{lat}} = 3.8\) K for copper: below that the electrons dominate, above it the lattice. At 1.9 K they give 186 and 47 J m⁻³ K⁻¹; at 4.2 K, 412 and 503. At room temperature copper holds \(3n_ak_B = 3.5\times10^6\) J m⁻³ K⁻¹ (the law of Dulong and Petit, F6, section 15), so at 4.2 K its heat capacity is 1/4000 of the room-temperature value. Wilson (section 5.2) makes the same point for all the materials of a magnet winding: at low temperature a tiny energy makes a large rise in temperature, which is why a quench needs so little to start (section 11).

Pre-requisite (continued): Spin and magnetic moments

Pre-requisite. Chapter 7 taught quantum angular momentum, spin, the electron’s magnetic moment and its energy \(-\boldsymbol{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{B}\) in a field. This section adds the proton’s moment and its gyromagnetic ratio; the Larmor precession of a moment about a field, at a frequency proportional to the field, derived classically and checked quantum mechanically; and nuclear magnetic resonance, in which a weak radio-frequency field at exactly that frequency tips the protons over, so that counting a frequency measures a field. Section 7 uses it for the NMR probe, the absolute standard of magnet measurement. Taught from Tong, Quantum Mechanics, sections 4.3 (time evolution of a spin), 9.3 and 9.3.1 (spin in a magnetic field, spin precession) and 13.3.1 (Rabi oscillations and the rotating-wave approximation); Livingston and Blewett, sections 8-6 and 8-7 (methods of magnetic measurement, and measurement using nuclear resonance); and Wilson, Superconducting Magnets, section 10.7 (measurement of magnetic field).

Moment and angular momentum. A charge \(q\) of mass \(m\) going round a circle of radius \(r\) at speed \(v\) is a current \(qv/(2\pi r)\) round an area \(\pi r^2\), so its magnetic moment is \(\mu = qvr/2\) (A m², or J/T). Its angular momentum is \(L = mvr\). The ratio

\[ \frac{\mu}{L} = \frac{q}{2m} \]

does not depend on the size or speed of the orbit. For spin the same proportion holds with an extra number, the \(g\)-factor: \(\boldsymbol{\mu} = g(q/2m)\mathbf{S}\). The ratio \(\gamma = \mu/S\) (rad s⁻¹ T⁻¹) is the gyromagnetic ratio. The electron has \(g = 2.0023\); the proton, which is built of quarks, has \(g_p = 5.586\) (Tong, section 9.3). So for the proton

\[ \gamma_p = g_p\frac{e}{2m_p} = 2.675\times10^8\ \text{rad s}^{-1}\,\text{T}^{-1}, \]

and the component of its moment along a field, for spin \(\pm\hbar/2\), is \(\mu_p = \gamma_p\hbar/2 = 1.41\times10^{-26}\) J/T.

Larmor precession, classically. A moment in a field feels a torque \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\times\mathbf{B}\) (the current loop of F4, section 10, whose torque has the size \(\mu B\sin\alpha\) at an angle \(\alpha\) to the field), and a torque is the rate of change of angular momentum (F3, section 7):

\[ \frac{d\mathbf{S}}{dt} = \boldsymbol{\mu}\times\mathbf{B} = \gamma\,\mathbf{S}\times\mathbf{B} . \]

The change is always at right angles to \(\mathbf{S}\), so the length of \(\mathbf{S}\) stays fixed; and at right angles to \(\mathbf{B}\), so the component along \(\mathbf{B}\) stays fixed. What is left is a turning of the part across the field. With \(\mathbf{B} = B\hat{\mathbf{z}}\) the equation reads \(dS_x/dt = \gamma BS_y\) and \(dS_y/dt = -\gamma BS_x\), so the complex number \(S_x + iS_y\) obeys \(d(S_x + iS_y)/dt = -i\gamma B(S_x + iS_y)\) and turns as \(e^{-i\gamma Bt}\). The spin precesses about the field, like a top about the vertical, at the Larmor angular frequency

\[ \omega_L = \gamma B, \]

whatever its angle to the field. (For \(\gamma > 0\) it turns clockwise seen from the tip of \(\mathbf{B}\).)

The same, quantum mechanically. A spin \(\tfrac12\) in the field has energy \(-\mu_zB = \mp\tfrac12\hbar\gamma B\) for spin along or against the field, two levels split by

\[ \Delta E = \hbar\gamma B = \hbar\omega_L . \]

A spin pointing at an angle to the field is a superposition of the two states, and their relative phase advances at \(\Delta E/\hbar = \omega_L\): the direction of the spin turns about the field at the Larmor frequency (Tong, sections 4.3 and 9.3.1). The two pictures agree, and the second adds that a photon of energy \(\hbar\omega_L\) can flip a spin from one level to the other.

Numbers. For a free proton \(\gamma_p/2\pi = 42.578\) MHz/T. In a water sample the surrounding electrons shield the protons slightly, and the value used for probes is the shielded one, \(\gamma_p'/2\pi = 42.576\) MHz/T, 26 parts per million lower. At 1 T the splitting is \(1.76\times10^{-7}\) eV, so tiny beside \(k_BT = 0.026\) eV at room temperature that the two levels are almost equally filled. The Boltzmann factor (F6, section 13) makes the numbers in the lower and upper levels proportional to \(e^{+x}\) and \(e^{-x}\), with \(x = \Delta E/2k_BT\) (the levels sit at \(\mp\tfrac12\Delta E\)). The excess along the field, as a fraction of all the protons, is \((e^x - e^{-x})/(e^x + e^{-x})\), a ratio written \(\tanh x\), which is close to \(x\) itself when \(x\) is small (expand both exponentials to first order, F1, section 8): here \(x = 3.4\times10^{-6}\), an excess of 3.4 parts per million. The signal comes from that small excess among the \(6.7\times10^{28}\) protons in each cubic metre of water.

Resonance. Add to the steady field \(B_0\hat{\mathbf{z}}\) a weak field \(B_1\) that rotates in the \((x, y)\) plane at angular frequency \(\omega\), in the same sense as the precession. (A coil fed with radio frequency makes a field that oscillates along one line; that is the sum of two fields rotating in opposite senses, and only the one turning with the spins matters. Dropping the other is the rotating-wave approximation, which Tong, section 13.3.1, uses for atoms in light, noting that the name comes from magnetic resonance.) Look at the spins from a frame that turns with \(B_1\). In a frame turning at angular velocity \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\), the rate of change of any vector is the rate seen in the laboratory minus \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf{S}\). The reason: a vector that stands still in the turning frame is, seen from the laboratory, carried round on a circle, and its rate of change is then \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf{S}\), as the velocity of a point on a turning wheel is \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf{r}\) (F2, section 4); the turning frame does not count that part as change. So

\[ \left(\frac{d\mathbf{S}}{dt}\right)_{turning} = \gamma\,\mathbf{S}\times\mathbf{B} - \boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf{S} = \gamma\,\mathbf{S}\times\left(\mathbf{B} + \frac{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}{\gamma}\right). \]

The turning frame sees an effective field \(\mathbf{B} + \boldsymbol{\Omega}/\gamma\). Turning with the precession, \(\boldsymbol{\Omega} = -\omega\hat{\mathbf{z}}\), the effective field is \((B_0 - \omega/\gamma)\hat{\mathbf{z}}\) plus the now stationary \(B_1\). At resonance, \(\omega = \gamma B_0\), the large field disappears from this frame entirely, and the spins precess about \(B_1\) alone, at \(\gamma B_1\): they tip from along \(\hat{\mathbf{z}}\) down through the plane and over, taking energy from the radio-frequency field as they go. Away from resonance the effective field stays nearly along \(\hat{\mathbf{z}}\) and the spins barely tip. Absorption happens only in a narrow band of frequency around \(\gamma B_0/2\pi\).

The probe. A small water sample sits in a coil that belongs to a tuned radio-frequency oscillator. The frequency is swept, or the field modulated a little, until the absorption shows as a change in the coil’s impedance and so in the oscillator’s level (Wilson, section 10.7; Livingston and Blewett, section 8-7); then the frequency is counted. Frequencies are easily counted to \(10^{-7}\), and \(\gamma_p'\) is known better than that, so NMR is the primary standard against which Hall probes and coils are calibrated. Two limits follow from the physics. First, every proton in the sample must see nearly the same field, or the line is smeared over \(\gamma\,\Delta B/2\pi\) and becomes too weak to find: a gradient across the sample is the enemy. Second, the tuned circuit covers a limited range of frequency, so each probe covers a limited range of field, and at low field both the frequency and the polarisation are small.

7 Measuring the field

A magnet is accepted on measurements, not on calculations. Four instruments do most of the work (Walckiers; the CAS Bruges proceedings).

Rotating coil. A long, narrow coil of \(N_t\) turns and length \(L\) (m) is turned about the magnet’s axis inside the aperture. The flux through it changes with angle, and an integrator triggered by an angle encoder records it. For a coil with conductors at radii \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) the flux at angle \(\theta\) is

\[ \Psi(\theta) = \mathrm{Re}\sum_{n} K_n C_n e^{in\theta}, \qquad K_n = N_t L\,\frac{R_2^{\,n} - R_1^{\,n}}{n R_r^{\,n-1}}, \]

so a Fourier analysis of one turn gives every multipole \(C_n\) at once, integrated over the coil’s length. Extra windings wired against the main one (“bucking”) cancel the dipole or quadrupole signal so the small terms can be amplified. Dipole strengths are measured to \(10^{-4}\), and harmonics repeated to 0.01 unit in the best cases. Modern systems take several hundred readings per turn at up to 10 turns per second, fast enough to follow the field during a ramp. The LHC magnets were measured with shafts 15 m long, made of twelve segments of 1.15 m. Figure 17.6 draws the arrangement.

Hall probe. A thin semiconductor plate carrying a current \(I_H\) develops a voltage across it \(V_H = I_H B/(n_c q t_H)\), with \(n_c\) the carrier density and \(t_H\) the plate thickness. It is small, measures the local field, and can be moved through the magnet on a stage to map the field and the fringe field at the ends. Its calibration drifts with temperature, so it is calibrated against NMR.

NMR probe. Protons in a sample of water precess in a field \(B\) at the frequency

\[ f = \frac{\gamma_p'}{2\pi}B = 42.576\ \text{MHz/T}\times B, \]

with \(\gamma_p'\) the shielded proton gyromagnetic ratio (CODATA 2018). A probe sweeps a radio frequency until it sees the resonance absorption. Frequency is measured very accurately, so NMR is the absolute standard, but it needs a uniform field (a gradient smears the line) and works over a limited range per probe. At the LHC injection field of about 0.535 T the frequency is 22.8 MHz; at 8.33 T it is 354.7 MHz.

Stretched wire. A thin wire, often 0.1 mm copper-beryllium, is stretched through the aperture and moved sideways by precision stages at both ends. The voltage integrated as it moves is the flux it cuts, which is \(\int B_y\,ds\) times its displacement. It gives the integrated field and, in a quadrupole, the magnetic axis, with few calibration worries: the accuracy of a dipole strength is set by the integrator gain (\(10^{-4}\) to \(10^{-5}\)) and the stages (better than 1 µm in 10 mm).

The integrated field divided by the current, \(\int B\,ds/I\) in T m/A, is the transfer function. Measured against current, up and down, it shows saturation (it falls at high current) and hysteresis (the up and down branches differ).

8 Superconductors

The phenomenon. Below a critical temperature \(T_c\) some metals carry current with no resistance, and they expel weak magnetic fields (the Meissner effect). In a type I superconductor, such as mercury or lead, a field above a small critical field destroys the state everywhere, so these are useless for magnets. In a type II superconductor, above a lower critical field \(B_{c1}\) the field enters in quantised flux tubes, vortices, each carrying the flux quantum \(h/2e\), and the material stays superconducting between them up to an upper critical field \(B_{c2}\) of tens of tesla. L. V. Shubnikov’s group established the two critical fields in 1936; A. A. Abrikosov explained the vortex lattice in 1957 (Padamsee, RF Superconductivity: Science, Technology and Applications, section 3.5.1, cites his 1957 paper; Tong, Electromagnetism, sections 6.5.2 and 6.5.3, gives the field theory).

A current crossing the vortices pushes them sideways with a Lorentz force. If they move, they dissipate energy, and the material has resistance. So a useful conductor must pin its vortices on defects: grain boundaries and fine precipitates. The current density at which pinning gives way is the critical current density \(J_c\).

The critical surface. \(J_c\) depends on field and temperature. The surface \(J_c(B, T)\) bounds the region in which the conductor is superconducting; it falls to zero at \(B_{c2}(T)\) and at \(T_c(B)\). A commonly used empirical fit (Lubell’s) for niobium-titanium is

\[ B_{c2}(T) = B_{c20}\left[1 - \left(\frac{T}{T_{c0}}\right)^{1.7}\right], \]

with \(B_{c20} \approx 14.5\) T and \(T_{c0} \approx 9.2\) K (de Rijk). It gives \(B_{c2} = 10.7\) T at 4.2 K and 13.5 T at 1.9 K (the Handbook’s table of superconductors, section 6.2, gives 10.5 to 11.0 T at 4.2 K for Nb-45-50Ti, and Wilson, section 12.2, a peak of about 11 T at 4.2 K and 15 T at zero temperature, with \(T_c = 9.3\) K): cooling from 4.2 K to 1.9 K moves the whole curve up by about 3 T. The same shift appears in de Rijk’s numbers: niobium-titanium carries 2500 A/mm\(^2\) at 6 T and 4.2 K, and the same at 9 T and 1.9 K. Turned round, \(T_c(B) = T_{c0}(1 - B/B_{c20})^{1/1.7}\) is 7.4 K at 4.4 T and 5.6 K at 8.33 T. A magnet is run at about 80 per cent of the current at which its peak field point would reach the critical surface, leaving a margin in current, field and temperature.

Pre-requisite (continued): Laplace and Poisson equations, uniqueness, boundary-value problems

Pre-requisite. Chapter 2 and its continuations in chapters 4, 6, 7, 9 and 15 taught Laplace’s and Poisson’s equations for the electric potential, the boundary conditions at electrodes and the uniqueness of the solution. This section writes the same equations for the vector potential \(A_z\) of a long magnet, finds their solutions in polar coordinates, \(r^{\pm n}\cos n\theta\), says which belong inside and which outside, and matches the two across a sheet of current. Section 9 uses all of it for the \(\cos\theta\) dipole. (How these solutions come out of separating the variables is in chapter 14; here they are checked directly.) Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, sections 2.4.2 (boundary-value problems), 3.1.2 (surface currents) and 3.2 (the vector potential); Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 2, §43; and Wilson, Superconducting Magnets, section 3.2 (ideal current distributions for transverse fields).

Poisson’s equation for \(A_z\). Ampère’s law at a point, \(\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{J}\) (chapter 10 derived one component of it from a small loop), has for a long magnet only a \(z\) component:

\[ \frac{\partial B_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial B_x}{\partial y} = \mu_0J_z . \]

Put in \(B_x = \partial A_z/\partial y\) and \(B_y = -\partial A_z/\partial x\) from the pre-requisite before section 1:

\[ \nabla^2A_z = \frac{\partial^2A_z}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2A_z}{\partial y^2} = -\mu_0J_z . \]

This is Poisson’s equation, the same as the electrostatic \(\nabla^2V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0\) with \(\mu_0J_z\) in place of \(\rho/\varepsilon_0\). Everything learnt for the electric potential carries over: uniqueness, images, numerical solution on a grid. Where there is no current, in the aperture and outside the coil, \(A_z\) obeys Laplace’s equation.

Solutions in polar coordinates. In polar coordinates the Laplacian is

\[ \nabla^2f = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right) + \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial\theta^2}. \]

Try \(f = r^n\cos n\theta\) with \(n\) a positive whole number. The radial part gives \((1/r)\,d(r\cdot nr^{n-1})/dr = n^2r^{n-2}\cos n\theta\); the angular part gives \(-n^2r^{n-2}\cos n\theta\). They cancel. Try \(f = r^{-n}\cos n\theta\): the radial part gives \((1/r)\,d(-nr^{-n})/dr = n^2r^{-n-2}\cos n\theta\), the angular part \(-n^2r^{-n-2}\cos n\theta\); they cancel too. The same holds with \(\sin n\theta\). For \(n = 0\) the two solutions are a constant and \(\ln r\), the potential of a line current. Any sum of these is also a solution. The whole number \(n\) is forced: the potential must come back to itself after a full turn in \(\theta\).

Which belong where.

  • Inside, in a region containing the axis: \(r^{-n}\) is infinite at \(r = 0\), where there is no current to justify it. Only \(r^n\cos n\theta\) and \(r^n\sin n\theta\) are allowed, and \(r^n\cos n\theta = \mathrm{Re}\,z^n\): these are the multipoles.
  • Outside, in a region reaching to infinity: \(r^n\) grows without limit, but the field of a coil with no net current must die away far from it. Only \(r^{-n}\cos n\theta\) and \(r^{-n}\sin n\theta\) are allowed.

Which \(n\) appear is decided by the source. If the current on a sheet varies as \(\cos n\theta\), only the solutions with that same \(\cos n\theta\) can match it, because the matching conditions below must hold at every angle, and different harmonics cannot cancel each other at every angle (they are orthogonal, as in a Fourier series).

Matching across a sheet at \(r = a\). From the pre-requisite before section 1:

  • \(B_r\), the normal component, is continuous. Since \(B_r = (1/r)\,\partial A_z/\partial\theta\), \(A_z\) itself is continuous (a possible constant difference is set to zero).
  • \(B_\theta\) jumps by the surface current: \(B_{\theta,out} - B_{\theta,in} = \mu_0K_z\). Since \(B_\theta = -\partial A_z/\partial r\),

\[ \frac{\partial A_{in}}{\partial r} - \frac{\partial A_{out}}{\partial r} = \mu_0K_z \qquad\text{at } r = a . \]

Why a guess is enough. Suppose a trial \(A_z\) satisfies Laplace’s equation inside and outside, the two matching conditions at the sheet, is finite on the axis and dies away at infinity. Any other solution would differ from it by a field with no source anywhere that vanishes far away, and by the uniqueness theorem of chapter 2 that difference is zero. So the guess, if it fits, is the answer.

At work: a \(\cos 2\theta\) sheet makes a pure quadrupole. Let \(K_z = -K_0\cos 2\theta\) on \(r = a\). Guess, with one unknown constant \(\alpha\),

\[ A_{in} = \alpha\,r^2\cos 2\theta, \qquad A_{out} = \alpha\,\frac{a^4}{r^2}\cos 2\theta, \]

which agree at \(r = a\). At \(r = a\), \(\partial A_{in}/\partial r = 2\alpha a\cos 2\theta\) and \(\partial A_{out}/\partial r = -2\alpha a\cos 2\theta\), so the jump condition reads \(4\alpha a\cos 2\theta = -\mu_0K_0\cos 2\theta\), and \(\alpha = -\mu_0K_0/(4a)\). Inside, \(r^2\cos 2\theta = x^2 - y^2\), so \(A_z = \alpha(x^2 - y^2)\) and

\[ B_x = \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial y} = \frac{\mu_0K_0}{2a}\,y, \qquad B_y = -\frac{\partial A_z}{\partial x} = \frac{\mu_0K_0}{2a}\,x: \]

a pure quadrupole of gradient \(g = \mu_0K_0/(2a)\), with no other multipole at all. Outside, the field falls as \(r^{-3}\). The same steps for \(K_z = -K_0\cos n\theta\) give a pure \(2n\)-pole whose field at the sheet is \(\mu_0K_0/2\) for every \(n\); \(n = 1\) is the dipole of section 9.

Numbers. For illustration take a gradient of 223 T/m, about that of the LHC’s main quadrupoles, with the sheet at \(a = 28\) mm. Then \(K_0 = 2ag/\mu_0 = 9.94\times10^6\) A/m, the field at the sheet is \(ga = 6.24\) T, and each pole, the stretch from \(-45°\) to \(+45°\), carries \(\int K_0\cos 2\theta\,a\,d\theta = K_0a = 2.78\times10^5\) A. A numerical sum over 4000 line currents placed on the sheet gives the same gradient at every interior point to machine precision.

Exercise L1. Check that \(r^3\cos 3\theta\) satisfies Laplace’s equation, and find the field it gives as \(A_z = \alpha r^3\cos 3\theta\).

Answer

Radial part: \((1/r)\,d(3r^3)/dr = 9r\); angular part: \(-9r\). They cancel. With \(r^3\cos 3\theta = \mathrm{Re}\,z^3 = x^3 - 3xy^2\), \(B_x = \partial A_z/\partial y = -6\alpha xy\) and \(B_y = -\partial A_z/\partial x = -3\alpha(x^2 - y^2)\): a sextupole.

Answer

Exercise L3. For the quadrupole sheet above, how strong is the field at \(r = 2a\), outside?

Answer

Outside, \(A_z \propto r^{-2}\), so the field, one derivative, falls as \(r^{-3}\). Just outside the sheet it has the size \(\mu_0K_0/2 = 6.24\) T, the same as just inside (only its \(\theta\) component reverses). At \(2a\) it is \(6.24/8 = 0.78\) T. 9 The \(\cos\theta\) dipole

The picture. Without iron near the beam, the field must come from the arrangement of the current. The question is: what current distribution on a cylinder gives a perfectly uniform field inside? The answer is a current along the axis that varies as the cosine of the angle around it.

The derivation. Use cylindrical coordinates \((r, \theta)\) about the magnet axis, a cylinder of radius \(a\) (m), and a surface current along \(z\) (A/m):

\[ K_z(\theta) = -K_0\cos\theta. \]

In two dimensions \(\mathbf{B} = \nabla\times(A_z\hat{\mathbf{z}})\), so \(B_r = (1/r)\,\partial A_z/\partial\theta\) and \(B_\theta = -\partial A_z/\partial r\), and away from the current \(\nabla^2 A_z = 0\) (Tong, sections 3.1 and 3.2; Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 2, §43). Try the solutions of Laplace’s equation with the same angular dependence as the source:

\[ A_z = \begin{cases} -\alpha\, r\cos\theta, & r < a,\\[2pt] -\alpha\, \dfrac{a^2}{r}\cos\theta, & r > a,\end{cases} \]

which is continuous at \(r = a\) and finite at the centre and at infinity. Inside, \(B_r = \alpha\sin\theta\) and \(B_\theta = \alpha\cos\theta\). Since \(\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf{r}} + \cos\theta\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\), this is a uniform field \(B = \alpha\) in the \(y\) direction. Outside, \(B_r = \alpha(a/r)^2\sin\theta\) and \(B_\theta = -\alpha(a/r)^2\cos\theta\), the field of a two-dimensional dipole. The surface current fixes \(\alpha\) through the jump in the tangential field (Tong, section 3.1.2), \(\hat{\mathbf{r}}\times(\mathbf{B}_{out} - \mathbf{B}_{in}) = \mu_0\mathbf{K}\), which here reads \(B_{\theta,out} - B_{\theta,in} = -\mu_0 K_0\cos\theta\), that is \(-2\alpha\cos\theta = -\mu_0K_0\cos\theta\). So

\[ B_0 = \alpha = \frac{\mu_0 K_0}{2}, \]

uniform everywhere inside, with no higher multipoles at all. A numerical sum over 20 000 line currents confirms it: 8.33 T at every interior point to machine precision.

The same result from the multipole expansion. A line current \(I\) along \(z\) at \(z_0 = re^{i\phi}\) gives, inside its radius,

\[ B_y + iB_x = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi(z - z_0)} = -\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n-1}}{z_0^{\,n}}, \]

so its contribution to the \(n\)-th coefficient is proportional to \(e^{-in\phi}/r^n\). Summing over a current distribution \(I(\phi)\) projects out its \(\cos n\phi\) Fourier component. A pure \(\cos\phi\) distribution has only \(n = 1\): a pure dipole. A \(\cos 2\phi\) distribution gives a pure quadrupole.

Thick coils and sectors. Real coils are made of flat cables of finite size. A shell between \(a_1\) and \(a_2\) with \(J_z = -J_0\cos\theta\) is a stack of thin shells, so \(B_0 = \mu_0J_0(a_2 - a_1)/2\). A practical approximation is a sector of uniform current density \(J\) between \(a_1\) and \(a_2 = a_1 + w\), spanning \(|\phi| < \phi_0\), with the opposite current in the mirror sector. Each element of the sector, at radius \(r\) and angle \(\phi\), has area \(r\,dr\,d\phi\) (F2, section 13) and carries the current \(dI = J\,r\,dr\,d\phi\). At the centre, a distance \(r\) away, it makes a field of size \(\mu_0\,dI/(2\pi r) = \mu_0J\,dr\,d\phi/(2\pi)\), at right angles to the line joining them. That line makes the angle \(\phi\) with the \(x\) axis, so the field makes the angle \(\phi\) with the \(y\) axis: its vertical part is \(\cos\phi\) times its size, and the horizontal parts of elements at \(+\phi\) and \(-\phi\) cancel. The \(r\) has cancelled: an element farther out carries more current, because its area grows as \(r\), but is farther away by the same factor. So each element contributes \(\mu_0J\,dr\cos\phi\,d\phi/(2\pi)\) to \(B_y\) at the centre. The integral over \(r\) from \(a_1\) to \(a_2\) is \(w\), and over \(\phi\) it is \(\int_{-\phi_0}^{\phi_0}\cos\phi\,d\phi = 2\sin\phi_0\), so one sector gives \(\mu_0Jw\sin\phi_0/\pi\), and the mirror sector, with the opposite current on the opposite side, adds the same again (exercise X2 showed that such a pair doubles every odd term). For the higher terms use the series above: an element at \(re^{i\phi}\) adds to the \(n\)-th coefficient in proportion to \(dI\,e^{-in\phi}/r^n = J\,r^{1-n}\,dr\,e^{-in\phi}\,d\phi\). For the sextupole, \(n = 3\), the radial integral is \(\int_{a_1}^{a_2}r^{-2}\,dr = 1/a_1 - 1/a_2\) and the angular one is \(\int_{-\phi_0}^{\phi_0}\cos 3\phi\,d\phi = \tfrac23\sin 3\phi_0\) (the \(\sin 3\phi\) parts cancel between \(+\phi\) and \(-\phi\)). So

\[ B_1 = \frac{2\mu_0 J w}{\pi}\sin\phi_0, \qquad B_3 \propto \sin 3\phi_0\left(\frac{1}{a_1} - \frac{1}{a_2}\right). \]

At \(\phi_0 = 60^\circ\) the sextupole vanishes and \(B_1 = \sqrt{3}\,\mu_0Jw/\pi\). The field depends on the current density and the coil’s width, not on the aperture. Real coils approximate \(\cos\theta\) better by placing copper wedges between blocks of cable, which cancel \(b_3\), \(b_5\) and \(b_7\) together; codes such as ROXIE optimise the block positions.

Worked number: the LHC dipole. Each aperture of an LHC dipole has 80 turns at the design current of 11 850 A, so it carries \(9.48\times10^5\) A on each side, with an average current density in the coil of about 300 A/mm\(^2\) (de Rijk). The coil aperture is 56 mm, so \(a_1 = 28\) mm. Treat each side as two 60° sectors carrying \(9.48\times10^5\) A at 300 A/mm\(^2\): the area is 3160 mm\(^2\), which fixes \(w = 33.7\) mm, and the sector formula gives \(B_1 = 7.0\) T. The yoke of iron around the collared coil adds the rest of the 8.33 T, about 16 per cent of the total, or 19 per cent of the coil’s own field; Schmüser (Handbook, section 7.2.4) gives 22 per cent of the coil’s field for the LHC and HERA, 10 per cent for the Tevatron and 35 per cent for RHIC. The iron here is not the field-maker it is in section 1; it returns the flux, shields the neighbouring aperture, and adds a little.

The LHC dipole is two-in-one: two such coils, for the two counter-rotating beams, sit side by side in one iron yoke and one cryostat, with the fields in opposite directions. This saves space, iron and cryostat, at the cost of coupling the two apertures magnetically.

10 Forces and the magnetic pressure

The picture. Each conductor carries current in the field of all the others, and feels a Lorentz force. In a dipole the two halves of the coil carry opposite currents and repel, and each half is also squeezed towards the midplane. A useful measure is the magnetic pressure, the energy density of the field:

\[ p_B = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}. \]

At 8.33 T this is \(2.76\times10^7\) Pa, 27.6 MPa, 276 bar. At 1.5 T it is 0.9 MPa; at 16 T, 102 MPa. The forces grow as the square of the field.

The thin shell exactly. The force per unit area on a surface current is \(\mathbf{K}\times\mathbf{B}_{avg}\), with \(\mathbf{B}_{avg}\) the mean of the fields on the two sides. For the \(\cos\theta\) shell the radial component \(B_r = B_0\sin\theta\) is the same on both sides, and the tangential one is \(B_0\cos\theta\) inside and \(-B_0\cos\theta\) outside, so its mean is zero. The force is therefore purely azimuthal:

\[ f_\theta = -K_0 B_0\cos\theta\sin\theta, \]

directed towards the midplane \(\theta = 0\) in each quadrant. Integrating over a quadrant, the force per unit length that the coil presses onto its midplane is

\[ F = \int_0^{\pi/2} K_0B_0\cos\theta\sin\theta\, a\, d\theta = \frac{K_0B_0a}{2} = \frac{B_0^2\,a}{\mu_0}. \]

At 8.33 T and \(a = 28\) mm this is 1.55 MN per metre of magnet, about 160 tonnes-force on every metre of each quadrant. Spread over a coil 34 to 40 mm thick it is a stress of 39 to 45 MPa at the midplane, close to de Rijk’s estimate of about 40 MPa for an 8 T dipole and 130 MPa at 13 T. A thick coil also has an outward radial force.

Figure 17.9. The force on the \(\cos\theta\) shell, \(f_\theta = -K_0B_0\cos\theta\sin\theta\), drawn every \(15°\). It runs along the shell, is largest at \(45°\), vanishes at the midplane and at the top, and in every quadrant pushes the conductor towards the midplane, where the coil must bear the summed force of the whole quadrant, \(B_0^2a/\mu_0\) per unit length.

Holding the coil. The coil must not move. A movement of 0.01 mm can release enough frictional heat to quench it (section 11). So the coil is clamped in collars, interlocking laminations of stainless steel or aluminium, that squeeze it with a prestress larger than the magnetic forces will ever relieve: for the LHC dipoles, 70 MPa at room temperature, which becomes 30 MPa when cold because the materials contract differently (de Rijk; Schmüser, in the Handbook, section 7.2.4, gives 100 MPa for the LHC and 60 MPa for HERA’s aluminium collars). Around the collars sit the iron yoke and a welded steel shell. Nb\(_3\)Sn coils cannot take such squeezing at room temperature, and the newer magnets use an aluminium shell, bladders inflated to up to 600 bar, and keys, which load the coil mostly as it cools (de Rijk).

Pre-requisite (continued): RLC circuits and phasors

Pre-requisite. Chapter 4 taught circuits of resistors, capacitors and inductors, and chapter 8 the time constant \(L/R\) of an inductor discharging into a resistor. This section follows the energy through such a discharge: where the stored \(\tfrac12LI^2\) goes, the integral of \(I^2\) over the decay, which sets how hot a conductor carrying that current gets, and the limit that the allowed voltage puts on the resistor, which ties the discharge time to the stored energy, the current and the voltage. Section 11 uses all of it for quench protection. Taught from Wilson, Superconducting Magnets, sections 9.1 (temperature rise), 9.2 (voltage) and 9.6 (protection by an external resistor), and Tong, Electromagnetism, sections 4.1.2 and 4.1.3 (inductance, magnetic energy and resistance).

The discharge. A magnet of inductance \(L\) (H) carries a current \(I_0\) from its supply. At \(t = 0\) the supply is switched out and a resistor \(R\) (\(\Omega\)) is left across the magnet. The current in an inductor cannot stop at once, because that would need an infinite voltage \(L\,dI/dt\), so it carries on through the resistor. Kirchhoff’s voltage law round the loop (F4, section 9) gives

\[ L\frac{dI}{dt} + RI = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I(t) = I_0e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \tau = \frac{L}{R}, \]

the decay of chapter 8 (the equation is the first differential equation of F1, section 10: the rate of change of \(I\) is proportional to \(I\)).

Where the energy goes. The resistor turns \(RI^2\) into heat. Over the whole decay, using \(\int_0^\infty e^{-kt}\,dt = 1/k\) (F1, section 7) with \(k = 2/\tau\),

\[ \int_0^\infty RI_0^2e^{-2t/\tau}\,dt = RI_0^2\,\frac{\tau}{2} = \tfrac12LI_0^2 . \]

All the energy stored in the magnet ends in the resistor, which must be built to absorb it, with enough mass to keep its temperature rise moderate. That is the point of an external dump: the energy is taken out of the cold magnet and deposited somewhere warm.

The integral of \(I^2\). A piece of conductor that keeps carrying the decaying current (the spot inside the magnet where a quench started) is heated in proportion to \(\int I^2\,dt\), and its temperature rise depends on that integral and on nothing else about the current’s history (section 11 shows why). For the exponential decay,

\[ \int_0^\infty I^2\,dt = I_0^2\,\frac{\tau}{2} . \]

If the full current flows for a time \(t_d\) before the switch opens, while the quench is detected, confirmed and acted on, add \(I_0^2t_d\). Divided by the square of the copper’s cross-section, this gives the \(\int J^2\,dt\) of section 11.

The voltage sets the speed. At the moment of switching the resistor’s voltage is \(RI_0\), the largest it will be; the magnet’s terminals see the same voltage, as \(L\,dI/dt = -RI\). If the insulation allows at most \(V_{max}\), then \(R \le V_{max}/I_0\) and

\[ \tau = \frac{L}{R} \ge \frac{LI_0}{V_{max}} = \frac{2U}{V_{max}I_0}, \qquad U = \tfrac12LI_0^2 . \]

This is the key relation. For a given stored energy and a given allowed voltage, the discharge is faster the larger the current. A magnet wound with fewer turns of a cable carrying more current has a smaller inductance for the same field and energy, and can be emptied faster. This is one reason accelerator magnets use cables of around 10 kA rather than wires of a few hundred amperes.

At work. One LHC dipole stores about 7 MJ at 11 850 A, so \(L = 2U/I_0^2 = 0.0997\) H. With \(V_{max} = 1\) kV, \(\tau \ge 1.18\) s and \(\int I^2\,dt \ge 8.3\times10^7\) A² s. The same energy wound for 1000 A would need at least 14 s. Suppose, for illustration, that the cable holds 15 mm² of copper. Then \(\int J^2\,dt = 8.3\times10^7/(1.5\times10^{-5})^2 = 3.7\times10^{17}\) A² s m⁻⁴, nearly three times the \(1.33\times10^{17}\) that section 11 finds will take copper from 1.9 K to 300 K. Even for a single magnet, a dump resistor within 1 kV cannot protect the hot spot; it would need about 2.8 kV. Section 11 shows the remedy: quench heaters spread the energy through the magnet’s own copper.

A dump that holds the voltage. If the magnet discharges instead into an element that holds a constant voltage \(V\) (a stack of diodes, or a resistor whose resistance rises as the current falls), the current falls in a straight line, \(L\,dI/dt = -V\), reaching zero at \(t_f = LI_0/V\). Then \(\int I^2\,dt = I_0^2t_f/3\). With the same peak voltage, \(t_f\) equals the exponential’s \(\tau\), and the integral is two thirds of the exponential’s \(I_0^2\tau/2\): holding the voltage at its limit all the way down empties the magnet faster than a resistor, whose voltage falls with the current.

Into a resistor (blue) the current decays as \(e^{-t/\tau}\) with \(\tau = 1.18\) s and is never quite zero; into a constant 1 kV (red) it falls in a straight line and is gone at 1.18 s. The dashed curves are \((I/I_0)^2\); the shaded areas, 0.59 s and 0.39 s, times \(I_0^2\) are the \(\int I^2\,dt\) that heats the hot spot.

Exercise R1. A solenoid stores 1 MJ at 500 A, and its insulation allows 500 V. Find its inductance, the largest dump resistance, the time constant, and \(\int I^2\,dt\) if detection takes 0.1 s.

Answer

\(L = 2U/I_0^2 = 2\times10^6/500^2 = 8\) H. \(R = 500/500 = 1\) \(\Omega\). \(\tau = L/R = 8\) s. \(\int I^2\,dt = I_0^2(t_d + \tau/2) = 250\,000\times4.1 = 1.03\times10^6\) A² s.

Exercise R2. The 7 MJ of one LHC dipole is dumped into a steel resistor block (specific heat 460 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹). How heavy must it be for a rise of no more than 200 K?

Answer

\(m = U/(c\,\Delta T) = 7\times10^6/(460\times200) = 76\) kg. A whole sector’s 1.08 GJ would need about 12 tonnes for the same rise, which is why the LHC’s dump resistors are large installations.

Exercise R3. For the LHC dipole above at 1 kV, compare \(\int I^2\,dt\) for an exponential dump and for a constant-voltage dump.

Answer

Both have the time \(LI_0/V = 1.18\) s. Exponential: \(I_0^2\times1.18/2 = 8.3\times10^7\) A² s. Constant voltage: \(I_0^2\times1.18/3 = 5.5\times10^7\) A² s, a third less, at the same peak voltage.

11 Stored energy, quench and protection

Stored energy. The field holds energy \(\int B^2/(2\mu_0)\,dV\). For the thin shell the field energy outside equals that inside, so the energy per unit length of one aperture is \(\pi a^2B_0^2/\mu_0\): 136 kJ/m at 8.33 T. A thick \(\cos\theta\) shell from 28 to 62 mm producing 8.33 T, with no iron, stores 280 kJ/m, or 8 MJ in two 14.3 m apertures. The LHC dipoles store about 7 MJ each, and the LHC’s magnets about 10 GJ in all. Since the energy is \(\tfrac12LI^2\), 7 MJ at 11 850 A means an inductance of about 0.1 H per dipole. Seven megajoules is the energy needed to warm and melt about 10 kg of copper.

Quench. If any part of the conductor rises above its critical temperature, it carries the current through its copper, which at 1.9 K has a tiny but finite resistance, and heats. The heat spreads and the normal zone grows. This runaway is a quench. It needs very little to start. The heat capacity of metals falls steeply near absolute zero, the lattice part as \(T^3\) and the electrons’ part as \(T\) (in copper at 2 to 5 K the two are comparable): warming a cubic centimetre of copper from 1.9 K to 5.5 K takes only 2.8 mJ (Debye model with the electronic term; handbook values for copper). Schmüser (Handbook, section 7.2.4) puts the energy that takes a cable past \(T_c\) at a few millijoules per cubic centimetre. On the other side, the conductor at 8.33 T and 11 850 A feels a force of \(BI = 98.7\) kN per metre. If it slips 10 µm against friction, the work done is about 1 J per metre, 10 mJ per centimetre: enough to quench it. Epoxy cracking under stress does the same.

Training. A new magnet usually quenches below its expected limit, then a little higher on the next attempt, and higher again, as each quench lets the coil settle and the weakest spots slip into place. A plot of quench current against quench number, the training curve, rises and levels off near the conductor’s limit. A magnet that has been warmed may need some retraining. When the LHC prepared to run at 6.5 TeV in 2015, its main dipoles had to be trained up to about 11 000 A (CERN gives 11 080 A), 7.7 T; the first sector had been trained by December 2014.

The hot spot. Where the quench starts, the copper carries the full current from the start, and in the first fraction of a second no heat leaves (the adiabatic approximation). For a unit volume of copper with resistivity \(\rho_e(T)\) and volumetric heat capacity \(C_V(T)\) (J m\(^{-3}\) K\(^{-1}\)), starting at temperature \(T_0\), the heat balance is \(J^2\rho_e\,dt = C_V\,dT\), so

\[ \int_0^\infty J^2(t)\,dt = \int_{T_0}^{T_{max}}\frac{C_V(T)}{\rho_e(T)}\,dT. \]

The left side depends only on how fast the current is brought down; the right only on the material. For copper of residual resistivity ratio 100 starting at 1.9 K, a Debye heat capacity and a Bloch-Grüneisen resistivity give \(1.33\times10^{17}\) A\(^2\) s m\(^{-4}\) to reach 300 K. At a copper current density of 1000 A/mm\(^2\) that allows 0.13 s of full current; at 500 A/mm\(^2\), 0.53 s. Wilson (Superconducting Magnets, section 9.1) gives a check: an all-copper conductor at \(4.7\times10^7\) A/m\(^2\) reaches room temperature 54 s after a quench at 4.2 K, which implies about \(1.2\times10^{17}\) A\(^2\) s m\(^{-4}\). De Rijk notes that an unprotected magnet can reach about 3000 K at the hot spot.

Protection. The magnet’s energy must therefore leave in a fraction of a second, or be spread over the whole coil so that no one spot takes it all. The usual sequence is:

  1. Detect: compare the voltages across two halves of the coil; the inductive voltages cancel, the resistive one does not. A threshold of about 100 mV is typical.
  2. Fire the quench heaters: steel strips on the coil, pulsed by a capacitor bank, which drive large parts of the coil normal, so that the magnet’s energy is absorbed by all its copper.
  3. Switch off the supply and open the circuit into a dump resistor, which takes the energy of the rest of the circuit.

A string of magnets cannot be discharged quickly through one resistor. An LHC dipole circuit of one sector has 154 dipoles, about 15.4 H and 1.08 GJ at full current. If the voltage across the dump may not exceed 1000 V (de Rijk’s coil-to-ground figure), the resistor is at most \(1000/11\,850 = 84\) m\(\Omega\) and the time constant at least \(L/R = 182\) s. That is a thousand times too slow for a hot spot. So each LHC dipole has a cold bypass diode in parallel, inside the helium vessel, as in HERA and RHIC; the Tevatron used thyristors outside the cryostat (Schmüser, Handbook, section 7.2.4): when it quenches and its heaters fire, its current switches into the diode and it discharges itself, while the rest of the circuit ramps down through the dump resistors (figure 17.11).

Figure 17.11. One LHC main dipole circuit, schematic, drawn with three of its 154 magnets. The converter drives the current \(I\) round the string. Each dipole has a cold bypass diode in parallel, pointing the way the current flows. The quench detector compares the voltages across the two halves of a magnet’s coil; when they differ by about 100 mV it fires the heater strips from their capacitor bank, switches the converter off and opens the dump switch, which puts the resistor, at most 84 m\(\Omega\) for 1000 V at 11 850 A, in series. The quenching magnet’s resistive voltage turns its diode on, so the string’s current goes round it while it empties itself into its own copper; the rest of the circuit’s energy, up to 1.08 GJ in all, goes into the resistor, with a time constant of at least 182 s.

Ramp voltage. The same inductance limits the ramp rate. Ramping one sector from 761 A (the injection field, scaled linearly) to 11 850 A in 20 minutes means \(dI/dt = 9.2\) A/s and \(L\,dI/dt = 142\) V across the circuit, harmless; ramping in a minute would need 2.8 kV.

12 Filaments, flux jumps and persistent currents

The critical state. Raise the field outside a slab of type II superconductor, along its faces. A field that changes with depth \(x\) into the slab needs a current flowing in it: Ampère’s law round a thin loop straddling depth \(x\) (F4, section 12; the same loop as for the current sheet before section 1) gives \(dB/dx = \mu_0J\). The pinned vortices let the field fall with depth only as steeply as the largest current the material can carry, \(\mu_0J_c\); any steeper, and the force on the vortices would beat the pinning and push them further in. So the field enters as a wedge, falling in a straight line from each face with slope \(\mu_0J_c\), and wherever it has entered the current density is exactly \(J_c\); deeper in, where the field has not yet reached, there is no current. This picture, due to C. P. Bean in 1962, is the critical state, and Wilson’s flux-jump argument (section 7.2) rests on it. Figure 17.12 draws it.

Figure 17.12. Bean’s critical state in a slab of half-width \(a_f\), schematic. The outside field \(B_a\) falls from each face in a straight line of slope \(\mu_0J_c\); the current is \(+J_c\) in one layer and \(-J_c\) in the other, both at right angles to the field, and zero in the field-free middle. A small warming lowers \(J_c\) and the slope (dashed): the field moves in, the shaded flux crossing current as it goes, and that releases heat. This is the start of a flux jump.

Why a superconducting wire is made of filaments. A slab of superconductor carrying \(J_c\) in its critical state is unstable. A small heat pulse lowers \(J_c\), the flux moves in, the moving flux dissipates energy, which heats the slab further. This flux jump runs away if the slab is too thick. For a slab of half-width \(a_f\) (m), a simple energy balance gives the adiabatic stability condition

\[ a_f < \frac{1}{J_c}\sqrt{\frac{3C_V\,(T_c - T_0)}{\mu_0}}, \]

with \(C_V\) the superconductor’s volumetric heat capacity (J m\(^{-3}\) K\(^{-1}\)), \(T_0\) the bath temperature and \(T_c\) the critical temperature at the local field. The balance runs as follows. Take \(J_c\) to fall linearly with temperature to zero at \(T_c\), so a temperature rise \(\Delta T\) lowers it by \(\Delta J_c = J_c\,\Delta T/(T_c - T_0)\). In the slab \(|x| < a_f\) the field profile has slope \(\mu_0 J_c\), so the field at depth \(x\) from the centre changes by \(\Delta B = \mu_0\Delta J_c(a_f - x)\). The flux that crosses the point \(x\) on its way in is \(\int_0^x \Delta B\,dx'\) per unit length, and it crosses against a current density \(J_c\), so the heat released there is \(J_c\int_0^x\Delta B\,dx' = \mu_0J_c\Delta J_c(a_fx - x^2/2)\) per unit volume. Averaged over \(0 < x < a_f\) this is \(\mu_0J_c\Delta J_c a_f^2/3\). The flux motion runs away if this heat exceeds the \(C_V\,\Delta T\) that raised the temperature in the first place; requiring \(\mu_0J_c^2a_f^2/[3(T_c - T_0)] < C_V\) gives the condition above. The adiabatic assumption (no heat leaves during the jump) is pessimistic for thin filaments in copper. With an assumed \(C_V \approx 6\times10^3\) J m\(^{-3}\) K\(^{-1}\) for niobium-titanium near 4 K and de Rijk’s \(J_c = 2500\) A/mm\(^2\) at 6 T and 4.2 K (where \(T_c(B) = 6.7\) K), this gives \(a_f \approx 76\) µm, a filament of order 150 µm across. This is Wilson’s adiabatic stability criterion (Superconducting Magnets, section 7.2, eqn 7.7); for niobium-titanium at 4.2 K and 6 T he takes \(J_c = 1.5\times10^9\) A/m\(^2\), a heat capacity of \(5.5\times10^3\) J m\(^{-3}\) K\(^{-1}\) and \(T_c = 6.5\) K, and finds 115 µm. The copper around the filaments also matters: it conducts the heat away and offers a parallel path for the current, so the filaments are embedded in a copper matrix, a strand of 0.6 to 1 mm with thousands of filaments. For accelerator magnets they are made much finer than flux-jump stability demands, a few micrometres, for the reason below. To stop the filaments coupling through the copper when the field changes, the strand is twisted. Strands are cabled into the flat Rutherford cable, keystoned (slightly thicker at one edge than the other, so that cables laid side by side curve round into the arc of a \(\cos\theta\) coil), which packs densely and transposes every strand through every position (each strand wanders in turn from the top face to the bottom and from one edge to the other along the cable, so that all strands link the same flux and share the current equally); a 12 kA cable has about 30 strands.

Persistent currents. When the field changes, each filament screens it with currents at \(J_c\) that circulate within the filament and do not decay, because there is no resistance. These persistent currents make each filament a small magnet. For a round, fully penetrated filament of diameter \(d_f\) the magnetisation (A/m) is \(M = 2J_c d_f/(3\pi)\) (Wilson, section 8.2.3, eqn 8.17; Schmüser, Handbook, section 7.2.4, who gives 3 µm as a typical filament radius in recent magnets), times the fraction \(\lambda\) of the coil that is superconductor. With an assumed \(J_c = 10^4\) A/mm\(^2\) at low field and \(\lambda = 0.3\), a 6 µm filament gives \(\mu_0M \approx 5\) mT and a 50 µm filament 40 mT. These magnetisations add field errors, mostly \(b_3\) and \(b_5\), of several units at injection, where the main field is lowest and \(J_c\) is highest. Thin filaments make them small. They also make the field depend on the history of the current, a hysteresis like iron’s.

Decay and snapback. At constant current, at injection, the persistent-current errors slowly decay over minutes, as current redistributes in the cable. When the ramp starts they return to their original values within a small change of current, the snapback. Both must be predicted and corrected, by trimming the sextupole correctors as the ramp begins, or the chromaticity and tunes (chapter 16) wander at the moment the beam is most fragile (chapter 18). Bottura and others measured these effects in the LHC dipoles in the 1990s.

13 Cryogenics

Two coolants. Helium boils at 4.2 K at atmospheric pressure. Below 2.17 K, the lambda point, liquid helium becomes superfluid (helium II): part of the liquid flows with no viscosity at all, a quantum effect seen on a large scale. Lebrun gives its virtues: a viscosity about a hundred times lower than water’s, so it seeps through the coil windings; a heat capacity per volume about 2000 times that of the superconductor, so it buffers small heat pulses; and a thermal conductivity, between 1.8 K and 2.0 K, about 1000 times that of high-purity (OFHC) copper. The LHC runs at 1.9 K in pressurised superfluid helium, near atmospheric pressure, both to gain the 3 T of section 8 for niobium-titanium and to use these transport properties. The Tevatron and HERA ran at about 4.5 K with supercritical helium in forced flow: helium pumped above its critical pressure of 2.27 bar, where liquid and gas are no longer distinct, so that no bubbles form in the channels.

The cost of a cold watt. A refrigerator lifts heat \(Q\) from temperature \(T\) to the room at \(T_a\). The second law (F6, section 16) gives the minimum work. The heat \(Q\) taken in at \(T\) carries the entropy \(Q/T\) out of the cold space. The machine delivers to the room both that heat and the work \(W_{el}\) turned into heat, \(Q + W_{el}\), which carries the entropy \((Q + W_{el})/T_a\). Entropy cannot be destroyed, so \((Q + W_{el})/T_a \geq Q/T\), and solving for \(W_{el}\),

\[ W_{el} \geq Q\,\frac{T_a - T}{T}. \]

With \(T_a = 300\) K:

80 2.8 9.8
4.5 65.7 235
1.9 157 560
1.8 166 592

Real plants reach a quarter to under a third of Carnot. Lebrun reports that HERA’s 4.4 K refrigerators reached a coefficient of performance of 280 W per W, about 24 per cent of Carnot, a record when built, and that the LHC’s new plants reached 28 per cent. Every watt that leaks into a 1.9 K magnet string costs more than half a kilowatt of electricity.

Heat loads. The heat comes from conduction through supports and current leads, radiation from warmer surfaces (cut by thermal shields at 50 to 80 K and by layers of reflective insulation in vacuum), synchrotron radiation and image currents from the beam (taken by a beam screen at higher temperature in the LHC), resistive joints, and, while ramping, the losses of persistent and coupling currents. The LHC’s cryogenic distribution line, 25.6 km long, had to leak less than 0.2 W/m on average at its coldest level.

The LHC’s plant. Eight refrigerators, each of 18 kW equivalent at 4.5 K, and eight 1.8 K units of 2.4 kW each (Lebrun); the Handbook rounds the totals to some 150 kW at 4.5 K and 20 kW at 1.9 K (Rode and Ganni, section 5.9.1). At 28 per cent of Carnot the 1.8 K duty alone needs 11 MW of electricity; at the Handbook’s figure for the LHC’s 1.8 K system, 950 W per W, about 17 per cent of Carnot, it needs 18 MW. The 4.5 K equivalent of all eight plants needs about 34 MW at 235 W per W (the Handbook says the large CERN and DESY plants do better than 300 W per W). CERN gives 40 MW for the whole cryogenic system, less than the two figures added, so the 1.8 K duty is presumably carried partly within the 4.5 K plants’ rating rather than on top of it. The LHC’s helium inventory is about 135 tonnes (Lebrun; CERN gives 120 tonnes to keep the magnets at 1.9 K).

Cool-down. The heat that must come out on the way down is almost all above 80 K, because heat capacities fall so steeply. For iron (Debye model, Debye temperature 470 K), cooling from 300 K to 80 K removes 68 kJ/kg, from 80 K to 4.5 K only 2.9 kJ/kg. So the first part is done with liquid nitrogen. CERN puts the LHC’s magnet cold masses at some 36 000 tonnes; treated as iron, 300 to 80 K removes about 2500 GJ, which needs about 6000 to 12 000 tonnes of nitrogen (latent heat 199 kJ/kg, plus up to 230 kJ/kg more if the cold gas is warmed back to room temperature on the way out); CERN gives about 10 000 tonnes. Cooling an LHC sector takes weeks.

14 Permanent magnets

The material. A permanent magnet holds a magnetisation set at the factory. Its remanence \(B_R\) (T; capital \(R\), to keep it apart from the radial field \(B_r\)) is the flux density it gives in a closed iron circuit, and its coercivity measures how strongly it resists demagnetisation. Sintered neodymium-iron-boron, developed independently by General Motors and Sumitomo Special Metals in 1984, has \(B_R\) of 1.0 to 1.4 T and a coercivity of 750 to 2000 kA/m, with a Curie temperature, the temperature above which the material loses its magnetic order and with it its magnetisation, of 310 to 400 °C. Its remanence falls by 0.09 to 0.12 per cent per kelvin, so magnets must be kept at a steady temperature or compensated. The Handbook’s typical values (Schlueter, section 6.4, table 1) fall inside these ranges: \(B_R\) of 1.1 to 1.4 T, \(\mu_0 H_c\) of 1.0 to 1.3 T (800 to 1030 kA/m), \(-0.10\) per cent per kelvin, and a Curie point of 350 °C. Ferrites are weaker (\(B_R\) of 0.2 to 0.44 T) but cheap, and they suffer no magnetic degradation up to 250 °C, though their remanence drifts faster with temperature, by about \(-0.2\) per cent per kelvin.

The Halbach array. If the direction of magnetisation turns around a ring twice as fast as the position angle, the field outside cancels and the field inside is a uniform dipole. Klaus Halbach, at the Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory, published these arrangements for accelerator multipoles in 1980. For a continuous ring of inner radius \(r_i\) and outer radius \(r_o\) the bore field is (quoted from Halbach, 1980)

\[ B = B_R\ln\frac{r_o}{r_i}\cdot K_1, \qquad K_1 = \frac{\sin(2\pi/M)}{2\pi/M}, \]

where \(K_1\) corrects for building the ring from \(M\) uniformly magnetised segments: 0.900 for 8 segments, 0.974 for 16. With \(B_R = 1.2\) T, \(r_i = 10\) mm and \(r_o = 40\) mm, a 16-segment ring gives 1.62 T. The quadrupole version has gradient \(2B_R(1/r_i - 1/r_o)K_2\) with \(K_2 = \cos^2(\pi/M)\sin(2\pi/M)/(2\pi/M)\) for segments of trapezoidal cross-section, as in Halbach’s 16-block quadrupole: with 16 segments, \(r_i = 5\) mm and \(r_o = 25\) mm, 360 T/m, a gradient no iron quadrupole of that bore could reach. For segments shaped as annular sectors the factor is instead \(\sin(3\pi/M)/(3\pi/M)\), giving 362 T/m; for the dipole the two shapes give the same \(K_1\) (Halbach and Schlueter, Handbook, section 7.2.8, eqns 4 and 5). Figure 17.13 draws the 16-segment dipole ring of the example.

Figure 17.13. A Halbach dipole ring of 16 segments, to scale for the example: \(r_i = 10\) mm, \(r_o = 40\) mm, \(B_R = 1.2\) T. The segment at position angle \(\theta\) is magnetised at \(2\theta - 90°\) (arrows), so the direction turns twice round while the position goes once round. The bore field is 1.62 T, uniform and upward; a numerical sum over the segments made here gives the same 1.62 T, and almost no field outside.

Uses. Permanent magnets need no power, no cooling and no supply, and cannot trip. Their fields are fixed (or adjustable only mechanically), so they suit beams of fixed energy. Fermilab’s Recycler, an 8 GeV storage ring in the Main Injector tunnel, used hybrid magnets of strontium ferrite bricks driving steel poles, with a low-Curie-temperature alloy compensating the ferrite’s drift with temperature; the prototypes, 1.2 m long, held \(\Delta B/B < 10^{-4}\) (Foster and colleagues, 1995). About 350 gradient magnets and 60 quadrupoles were built for the ring, and the 8 GeV transfer line had 51 dipoles, 67 gradient magnets and 8 quadrupoles (Nicol and Knauf, 1997); the ring was built in the late 1990s, alongside the Main Injector, which was completed in 1999. Undulators and wigglers in light sources and free-electron lasers are rows of permanent magnets of alternating polarity (chapter 20, chapter 26).

How it was researched and built

The Tevatron. Robert Wilson told the Congressional Joint Committee on Atomic Energy on 9 March 1971 that superconducting magnets were feasible, and Fermilab’s development ran from 1973 to 1979. The design was conservative: niobium-titanium cable in a \(\cos\theta\) coil, clamped in stainless steel collars, with the iron yoke outside the cryostat at room temperature, and coils not impregnated so that helium could reach the conductor. The 6.3 km tunnel housed 990 main superconducting magnets, 774 of them dipoles, with a bending field of about 4.4 T (Lebrun and de Rijk; Wikipedia gives 4.2 T). They were cooled by supercritical helium in forced flow, re-cooled by two-phase helium returning in an annular channel, fed from a central liquefier to 24 satellite refrigerators. The magnets used about 17 tons of niobium-titanium in 50 tons of copper cable (Wilson’s 1978 figures, as reported by Wikipedia) and ran at about 4.5 K. The machine was installed under the Main Ring in its tunnel, and the Main Ring served as its injector. First beam, 512 GeV, came on 3 July 1983; 800 GeV on 16 February 1984; 900 GeV on 21 October 1986, with proton-antiproton collisions at 1.8 TeV on 30 November 1986; 980 GeV with Run II from 2001. It cost about 120 million US dollars and ran until 30 September 2011. Lebrun’s judgement is that it established the feasibility of large superconducting magnet systems and trained a generation of experts.

ISABELLE. Brookhaven’s collider was to have two rings in a 3.8 km tunnel, each with 216 dipoles and 138 quadrupoles at about 4.3 K in the 1977 design (Kassner), and a 25 kW refrigerator at 3.8 K was procured. New York’s politicians pushed funding through before the magnet development was complete. The production magnets of 1981 failed below the needed field, delays led to comparison with the far larger Superconducting Super Collider proposal, and the discovery of the W and Z at CERN removed much of the physics case. It was cancelled in July 1983. The tunnel, halls and refrigerator were later used for RHIC. Robert Crease’s two-part history, cited below, tells the story in detail.

HERA. DESY’s electron-proton collider in Hamburg was the first superconducting accelerator in Europe. Its proton ring, 6.3 km round, took protons to 820 GeV (later 920 GeV) with 416 superconducting dipoles and 224 quadrupoles. Unlike the Tevatron’s, the iron yoke was inside the cryostat, cold, and the magnets were built by industrial firms in several European countries from DESY’s design; they ran at 4.4 K. The production dipoles were made in series in the late 1980s; one firm’s line was planned for 223 of them at a rate of one a day (Dustmann and others, 1988). HERA operated from 1992 to 30 June 2007, and more than 20 per cent of its cost was borne by institutions outside Germany.

RHIC. Brookhaven’s Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider, in ISABELLE’s 3.8 km tunnel, began operation in 2000 with 1740 superconducting magnets, its dipoles at 3.45 T, cooled to about 4.5 K. It ended data-taking on 6 February 2026.

The SSC. The US Superconducting Super Collider, a collider of 20 TeV per beam (40 TeV in collision) in a ring of 83 to 87 km near Waxahachie, south of Dallas (Lebrun gives 83 km; the final plan was 87.1 km), would have had almost 10 000 main superconducting magnets; years of magnet development and industrialisation went into it before it was cancelled in 1993, after construction had begun. Much of that work served the LHC.

The LHC. CERN’s study in the 1980s aimed at twin-aperture magnets near 10 T, about twice the state of the art, and settled on niobium-titanium at 1.9 K in superfluid helium. A full-length twin-aperture prototype used HERA coils; 56 mm aperture model dipoles were built and analysed in the mid-1990s. The CERN Council approved the project in December 1994. CERN gives the construction cost of the LHC machine and its areas as 3756 million Swiss francs in materials, to which Shiltsev and Zimmermann, quoting Evans, add 1224 million francs of labour (chapter 29). Its 1232 main dipoles, each 15 m long and 35 tonnes, were made by industry in series and tested cold at CERN before installation (their field quality was measured cold on 15 to 20 per cent of them, the rest warm, relying on the correlation between warm and cold measurements; Schmüser, Handbook, section 7.2.4); there are 392 main quadrupoles, and some 9600 magnets in all. The magnets use 1200 tonnes of superconducting cable, 470 tonnes of it niobium-titanium. A quick check on the numbers: at 7 TeV, \(B\rho = 23\,349\) T m, so at 8.33 T the bending radius is 2803 m, and \(2\pi\rho = 17.6\) km of dipole field shared among 1232 magnets is 14.3 m each, the magnetic length inside each 15 m magnet.

19 September 2008. Full beam commissioning started on 10 September 2008. Nine days later, during powering tests of sector 3-4 without beam, a soldered splice in the busbar between two magnets, joining the superconducting cables of neighbouring magnets inside a copper stabiliser, overheated. Measurements of cryogenic temperatures later showed its resistance had been about 220 n\(\Omega\), against a nominal value of about 0.3 n\(\Omega\) (Lamont; Wenninger gives 0.35 n\(\Omega\), so sources give 0.3 to 0.35 n\(\Omega\)). The present understanding was that poor thermal and electrical contact between the cable and the copper let it run away thermally; the solder and copper melted, an electric arc formed between the exposed cable ends, and it punctured the helium enclosure. Helium poured into the insulation vacuum, and the pressure wave travelled along the cryostats, displacing and damaging magnets. About two tonnes of liquid helium escaped explosively before an emergency stop, and four more at lower pressure. Fifty-three main magnets, 39 dipoles and 14 quadrupoles, were removed for repair or replacement, and the beam vacuum was cleaned of soot and insulation debris. The cures were an extended quench protection system able to detect resistive splices (a threshold of 300 µV over 10 s, splice resistance resolved to about 1 n\(\Omega\); splices of 50 to 100 n\(\Omega\) were judged dangerous at high current), larger relief valves (DN200) on every dipole cryostat of the four sectors warmed up after the incident, fitted in 2009 (the four sectors that stayed cold had spring-loaded ports fitted as an extra helium release path, and were to get the DN200 valves in the 2010 shutdown), and stronger anchoring. Beams returned on 20 November 2009, about 14 months later. The LHC ran at 3.5 TeV per beam from 2010, 4 TeV in 2012, 6.5 TeV from 2015 after the splices were consolidated, and 6.8 TeV from 2022.

Nb\(_3\)Sn and HL-LHC. For the High-Luminosity LHC, the niobium-titanium quadrupoles beside the experiments, 70 mm aperture and a nominal 205 T/m at 7 TeV (Evans and Bryant), are being replaced by Nb\(_3\)Sn quadrupoles with a 150 mm aperture at 132.6 T/m (132.2 T/m in the HL-LHC technical design report): 9.9 T at the edge of the bore, and a little over 11 T on the conductor. They are built in two lengths, 4.2 m in the United States (MQXFA) and 7.2 m at CERN (MQXFB). A short model reached 95 per cent of its short-sample current (the current at which a short piece of the same cable, tested alone in the same field and temperature, leaves the superconducting state: the most the coil could carry if it had no weak spot), a peak field of 13.4 T, and its performance depended on how much the coil was preloaded. Two of the first US production magnets failed their acceptance tests and were taken apart, which led to revised assembly specifications. They will be the first Nb\(_3\)Sn magnets in an accelerator (Ambrosio and others).

How a magnet is designed today. The designer starts from the beam’s needs: field or gradient, good-field region and its tolerance in units, aperture, length, ramp rate, and the cost of electricity over the magnet’s life. An analytic first design (Ampère’s law, sector formulas, the force estimates above) fixes the size. Two-dimensional and then three-dimensional field computations follow, with POISSON, OPERA, ANSYS or, for superconducting coils, CERN’s ROXIE (Zickler), and finite-element mechanical analysis of the collars and shell. A short model is built and tested, then a full-length prototype, then a pre-series to set the production tolerances. Every series magnet is measured warm and, if superconducting, cold, and the measurements go back into the optics model (chapter 29).

At the bench

This section draws on Walckiers, de Rijk, Lebrun, Lamont and the CAS proceedings.

Measuring a magnet on the bench. A warm iron magnet sits on a stand with a rotating-coil shaft through its aperture, a power supply and a water connection. The procedure:

  1. Connect the water and check the flow and the inlet and outlet temperatures; then connect the supply and check polarity with a Hall probe.
  2. Run the standard cycle: ramp to the maximum current and back to zero several times.
  3. Step the current up in steps, and at each one record the integrated field and harmonics from the rotating coil, and the current from a precision current transducer. Then step down.
  4. Plot the transfer function \(\int B\,ds/I\) against \(I\) (figure 17.14, top). It is flat in the middle, falls at the top where the iron saturates, and the up and down branches split by the hysteresis. At zero current the remanent field, a few millitesla, remains.
  5. Read the harmonics against current: \(b_3\) in a dipole and \(b_6\) in a quadrupole shift as the poles saturate. The coil’s centring gives the magnetic axis; the phase of the main harmonic gives the roll angle.
  6. Map the ends with a Hall probe on a stage, or measure the field integral with a stretched wire, and check the effective length.

A superconducting magnet’s test. The magnet is in a cryostat (vertical, in a bath, or horizontal, as the LHC dipoles were tested) with current leads, voltage taps along the coil, temperature sensors, and quench heaters.

  • Cool-down. Slow, so that temperature differences across the magnet do not stress it; nitrogen-cooled helium gas first, then liquid helium, then, for 1.9 K, pumping the bath. For a single magnet this takes days; for an LHC sector, weeks. The thermometers are resistance thermometers: platinum where the resistance changes enough, above a few tens of kelvin, and semiconducting or ceramic types below. Their readings are the cool-down’s chart.
  • Electrical checks. Insulation to ground at high voltage, coil resistance and inductance, heater circuits. A short between turns or to ground found cold is far cheaper than one found in the tunnel.
  • Ramp to quench. Ramp at a set rate until the magnet quenches. The quench detector sees the resistive voltage between the two half-coils rise past its threshold, of order 100 mV, and fires the heaters and the dump. The current trace collapses over a fraction of a second; the voltage taps tell which segment went first, and the delay between taps tells how the normal zone spread. The helium boils, the pressure in the cryostat jumps, and relief valves vent, audibly, while the recovery line takes back most of the gas. Then the magnet must be cooled again, which takes hours.
  • The training curve. Quench current against quench number (figure 17.14, bottom). A good magnet starts within a few per cent of its target and climbs in a handful of quenches; a poor one starts low, climbs slowly, or quenches erratically at one place, a sign of a loose or damaged turn. A magnet that has passed is cycled to a temperature above its operating point and back to see whether it remembers its training.
  • Magnetic measurement cold. Rotating coils at the injection plateau show the persistent-current harmonics, their decay over minutes at constant current, and their snapback when the ramp resumes. The field at full current shows any iron saturation.
  • Splices and joints. The resistance of every joint is measured, at nano-ohm resolution after 2008; a joint that warms under current is a fault waiting to happen.

Figure 17.14. What the two bench tests show, drawn, not computed or measured. Top: the transfer function of a warm iron magnet, relative to its value in the middle, against the current. It is flat in the middle and falls at the top, where the iron saturates; the down branch lies above the up branch by the hysteresis, most at low current, where the remanent field is the largest part of the field. Bottom: training curves, quench current against quench number, relative to the target. A good magnet starts within a few per cent of the target and climbs towards its conductor’s limit in a handful of quenches, and after a thermal cycle it remembers most of its training; a poor one starts low and climbs slowly and erratically.

Ways it fails, with their signatures. - Low first quench and slow training: coil not prestressed enough or conductor movement; the voltage taps locate the segment. - Quench at a ramp rate but not at a lower one: eddy and coupling currents in the cable heating it; the limit depends on \(dI/dt\). - A quench that will not propagate, or a heater that does not fire: a hot spot that rises past safe limits; a burnt coil. - A slowly warming thermometer at constant current: a resistive joint, as in sector 3-4. - Spurious quench detection: electrical noise on the voltage taps; the circuit trips with no resistive voltage. - In a warm magnet, a field that drifts with time: water temperature changing, a coil warming, or a magnet not cycled.

Where it stops

Iron stops at its saturation, 1.6 to 2 T. Niobium-titanium stops near 10 T at 1.9 K, where its critical surface leaves too little margin: the LHC’s 8.33 T is already about 80 per cent of the way. Nb\(_3\)Sn reaches a practical limit of about 16 T for accelerator dipoles, and the High-Luminosity LHC’s quadrupoles will be its first use in a machine. Above that, high-temperature superconductors in combination with Nb\(_3\)Sn are being developed for fields towards 20 T. Every step up in field is paid for in force (the pressure grows as \(B^2\), from 28 MPa at 8.33 T to 102 MPa at 16 T, and the coil stress from about 40 MPa to well over 100 MPa), in stored energy that must be extracted without burning the coil, and in cost per metre. Field is not the only limit on a circular machine’s energy: for electrons, synchrotron radiation ends the ring long before the magnets do (chapter 20), which is why electron colliders above a few hundred GeV are linear (chapter 25). How magnets’ imperfections limit the beam they hold is chapter 18.

Exercises

1. Find the bending radius needed for 7 TeV protons with dipoles at 8.33 T, and with iron dipoles at 2 T.

Answer

\(p = \sqrt{E^2 - (m_pc^2)^2}/c = 7000\) GeV/\(c\) to six figures, so \(B\rho = 3.3356 \times 7000 = 23\,349\) T m. At 8.33 T, \(\rho = 2803\) m; at 2 T, \(\rho = 11\,675\) m. The bending alone would need \(2\pi\rho = 73\) km of iron dipoles, more than the whole LHC tunnel of 27 km.

2. A dipole has a 50 mm gap and must reach 1.5 T. How many ampere-turns does it need if the iron is ideal? If the iron path is 1 m and \(\mu_r = 1000\)?

Answer

\(NI = BG/\mu_0 = 1.5 \times 0.05/(1.2566\times10^{-6}) = 59.7\) kA turns. With iron, \(NI = (B/\mu_0)(G + l_{Fe}/\mu_r) = (1.5/\mu_0)(0.050 + 0.001) = 60.9\) kA turns: the iron costs 2 per cent, \(\eta_m = 0.980\).

3. A quadrupole has a pole-tip radius of 40 mm and a gradient of 20 T/m. What is the field at the pole tip, and how many ampere-turns does each pole need?

Answer

\(B_{tip} = g r_0 = 0.8\) T, comfortably below saturation. From section 3, \(NI = B_{tip}r_0/(2\mu_0) = gr_0^2/(2\mu_0) = 20 \times 0.0016/(2 \times 1.2566\times10^{-6}) = 12.7\) kA turns per pole.

4. Compute the magnetic pressure at 8.33 T. Express it in bar and compare with 16 T.

Answer

\(p_B = B^2/(2\mu_0) = 8.33^2/(2 \times 1.2566\times10^{-6}) = 2.76\times10^7\) Pa = 27.6 MPa = 276 bar. At 16 T it is 101.9 MPa, 3.7 times more, as \((16/8.33)^2 = 3.69\).

5. For the 1.5 T dipole of exercise 2 with a 4.6 m mean turn length, find the coil power at 5 A/mm\(^2\), the water flow for a 20 K rise, and the voltage at 1000 A.

Answer

\(P = \rho_e J (NI) l_t = 1.72\times10^{-8} \times 5\times10^6 \times 60\,877 \times 4.6 = 24.1\) kW. Water: \(\dot{m} = P/(c_p\Delta T) = 24\,100/(4186 \times 20) = 0.288\) kg/s, 17.3 l/min. At 1000 A, \(N = 61\) turns and \(V = P/I = 24\) V. Halving \(J\) halves the power and doubles the copper.

6. An NMR probe is used to set the LHC dipole field at injection, 450 GeV. What frequency does it resonate at? And at 8.33 T?

Answer

The field scales with momentum: \(B = 8.33 \times 450/7000 = 0.535\) T. With \(\gamma_p'/2\pi = 42.576\) MHz/T, \(f = 22.8\) MHz. At 8.33 T, \(f = 354.7\) MHz. A probe covers a limited range, so different probes serve the two.

7. (a) Show that a surface current \(K_z = -K_0\cos\theta\) on a cylinder of radius \(a\) gives a uniform field \(\mu_0K_0/2\) inside. (b) Show that the force on one quadrant of the shell, towards the midplane, is \(B_0^2a/\mu_0\) per unit length, and evaluate it for the LHC dipole.

Answer

  1. Section 9: with \(A_z = -\alpha r\cos\theta\) inside and \(-\alpha a^2\cos\theta/r\) outside, the jump of \(B_\theta\) across the shell is \(-2\alpha\cos\theta\), and this must equal \(-\mu_0K_0\cos\theta\), so \(\alpha = B_0 = \mu_0K_0/2\).

  2. The mean tangential field at the shell is zero and the radial field is \(B_0\sin\theta\), so the force per area is \(K_0\cos\theta \cdot B_0\sin\theta\), azimuthal, towards the midplane. Over a quadrant, \(\int_0^{\pi/2}K_0B_0a\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta = K_0B_0a/2 = B_0^2a/\mu_0\). With \(B_0 = 8.33\) T and \(a = 28\) mm: \(8.33^2 \times 0.028/(1.2566\times10^{-6}) = 1.55\times10^6\) N/m.

8. An LHC dipole stores about 7 MJ at 11 850 A. Find its inductance, and the inductance and energy of a sector of 154 dipoles in series. If the voltage across the dump resistor must stay below 1000 V, what is the shortest time constant of the discharge?

Answer

From \(U = \tfrac12LI^2\), with \(U\) the stored energy, \(L = 2U/I^2 = 2 \times 7\times10^6/11\,850^2 = 0.0997\) H. A sector: \(15.35\) H and \(154 \times 7 = 1078\) MJ, about 1.1 GJ. \(R \leq 1000/11\,850 = 0.0844\) \(\Omega\), so \(\tau = L/R \geq 182\) s. A quenching magnet must lose its current in a fraction of a second (section 11), which is why each dipole has its own diode and heaters.

9. What is the Carnot minimum electrical power to remove 2.4 kW at 1.8 K, rejecting to 300 K? With eight such units at 28 per cent of Carnot, what is the total?

Answer

\(W_{el}/Q = (300 - 1.8)/1.8 = 165.7\), so 2.4 kW needs at least 398 kW. At 28 per cent of Carnot, 592 W per W: eight units of 2.4 kW, 19.2 kW in all, need \(19\,200 \times 592 = 11.4\) MW.

10. A copper stabiliser of residual resistivity ratio 100 carries 1000 A/mm\(^2\) when a quench starts at 1.9 K. Using the material integral \(1.33\times10^{17}\) A\(^2\) s m\(^{-4}\) to 300 K, how long can the full current flow before the hot spot reaches 300 K? If the current then decays exponentially with time constant \(\tau\) after a delay \(t_d\), what \(\tau\) is allowed with \(t_d = 0.03\) s?

Answer

At constant current, \(t = 1.33\times10^{17}/(10^9)^2 = 0.133\) s. With a delay and an exponential decay, \(\int J^2dt = J_0^2(t_d + \tau/2)\), so \(\tau/2 = 0.133 - 0.03 = 0.103\) s and \(\tau \leq 0.21\) s. Quench detection, heater delay and the spread of the normal zone must all fit inside a few tenths of a second.
