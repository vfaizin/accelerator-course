Pre-requisite (continued): Cross-section, rate and luminosity basics

Pre-requisite. This continues Cross-section, rate and luminosity basics in chapter 3 and its continuation in chapter 5, which gave the rate on a thin target. It adds what a collider needs from the cross-section: luminosity as the number that turns a cross-section into a rate, first for a fixed target and then for a bunch treated as a thin target; the small fractions of the barn and the inverse barn in which integrated luminosity is counted; the expected number of events, \(N_{\text{ev}} = \sigma\int\mathcal{L}\,dt\); and which part of the total cross-section takes particles out of a stored beam. Sections 2 and 3 build the collider’s luminosity on it, and section 10 uses the inelastic cross-section for burn-off and pile-up. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 2, §12 (the invariant flux factor); S. Y. Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 1, footnote 1 and exercise 7 (the definition of luminosity, for a fixed target and for two bunches); and Chao and others, Handbook, §4.1 (luminosity) and §3.3.2 (beam and luminosity lifetime).

Reminder: chapter 3 defined the cross-section by a rate, \(\sigma = R_1/\Phi_b\), the rate of a process at one target divided by the flux of projectiles falling on it, and chapter 1, section 6.1 wrote the rate on a whole target as \(R = \mathcal{L}\sigma\).

From one target to many. A beam brings \(\dot N\) particles per second onto a slab of thickness \(\ell\) (cm) holding \(n\) targets per cm³. If the slab is thin, so that the targets do not hide one another, each projectile undergoes the process with probability \(n\sigma\ell\), and the rate is

\[ R = \dot N\,n\,\ell\,\sigma = \mathcal{L}\,\sigma, \qquad \mathcal{L} = \dot N\,n\,\ell \quad [\text{cm}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}] . \]

The luminosity is the projectile rate times the number of targets per unit area. It holds everything the machine and the target contribute; the cross-section holds the physics. At work: \(10^{12}\) protons per second through a metre of liquid hydrogen, \(4.23 \times 10^{22}\) atoms per cm³, give \(\mathcal{L} = 10^{12} \times 4.23 \times 10^{22} \times 100 = 4.2 \times 10^{36}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\), the number quoted in “The wall”.

A bunch as a target. A colliding bunch is the same thing turned round. Let bunch 2 hold \(N_2\) particles spread over an effective area \(A_{\text{eff}}\) across the beam. Its number of targets per unit area is \(N_2/A_{\text{eff}}\), so a particle of bunch 1 that crosses it undergoes the process with probability \(\sigma N_2/A_{\text{eff}}\). All \(N_1\) particles together give \(\sigma N_1N_2/A_{\text{eff}}\) events per crossing, and with \(n_b\) bunch pairs meeting \(f_0\) times a second,

\[ \mathcal{L} = \frac{f_0\,n_b\,N_1N_2}{A_{\text{eff}}} . \]

Section 3 finds \(A_{\text{eff}} = 4\pi\sigma_x\sigma_y\) for Gaussian bunches; the pre-requisite just before it supplies the integrals. At work: an LHC design bunch, \(1.15 \times 10^{11}\) protons with \(\sigma_x = \sigma_y = 16.6\) µm, presents \(N/(4\pi\sigma^2) = 3.3 \times 10^{15}\) protons per cm², against \(4.23 \times 10^{24}\) per cm² for a metre of liquid hydrogen: \(1.3 \times 10^{9}\) times thinner. A proton crossing it has a chance of only \(8.0 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{cm}^2 \times 3.3 \times 10^{15}\ \text{cm}^{-2} = 2.7 \times 10^{-10}\) of an inelastic collision. The collider makes up the difference by crossing 11 245 times a second, with 2808 bunches, for hours.

The barn and its fractions. The barn is \(10^{-24}\) cm², \(10^{-28}\) m². The cross-sections of collider physics run over thirteen orders of magnitude below it, so its fractions are used:

unit cm² a process of about this size
mb \(10^{-27}\) inelastic proton-proton collision, about 80 mb
µb \(10^{-30}\) (the unit of the LHC’s luminosity displays, Hz/µb)
nb \(10^{-33}\) small-angle Bhabha scattering in a luminometer, about 160 nb (section 9)
pb \(10^{-36}\) Higgs boson production at the LHC, about 50 pb
fb \(10^{-39}\) the rare processes sought at high luminosity

Because the rate is \(\mathcal{L}\sigma\), a luminosity can be written in inverse barns per second. \(1\) nb\(^{-1}\) is \(10^{33}\) cm\(^{-2}\), so \(10^{34}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\) is 10 nb\(^{-1}\) s\(^{-1}\), and 1 Hz/µb is \(10^{30}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\). Read “10 nb\(^{-1}\) per second” as “10 events per second for each nanobarn of cross-section”.

Integrated luminosity and the number of events. Over a time the rate adds up:

\[ N_{\text{ev}} = \int\mathcal{L}\sigma\,dt = \sigma\int\mathcal{L}\,dt . \]

The integral \(\int\mathcal{L}\,dt\) is the integrated luminosity, an inverse area, counted in inverse barns: 1 fb\(^{-1}\) \(= 10^{39}\) cm\(^{-2}\), one expected event for each femtobarn of cross-section. A day of collisions at \(10^{34}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\) gives \(8.64 \times 10^{38}\) cm\(^{-2}\) \(= 0.864\) fb\(^{-1}\). The expected number is a Poisson mean: the number counted scatters about it by \(\sqrt{N_{\text{ev}}}\) (Counting statistics). At work: ATLAS’s 140.1 fb\(^{-1}\) of Run 2, with a top-quark pair cross-section of about 830 pb at 13 TeV (the theoretical value usually quoted), held \(140.1 \times 10^{3}\ \text{pb}^{-1} \times 830\ \text{pb} = 1.2 \times 10^8\) top pairs.

The fixed target wins on rate. The liquid-hydrogen target above collects 140.1 fb\(^{-1}\) in \(1.401 \times 10^{41}/4.23 \times 10^{36} = 3.3 \times 10^4\) s, about 9 hours. What it cannot give is energy: the same 7 TeV protons reach only 114.6 GeV in the centre of mass (section 1). The collider pays for its energy in luminosity, and everything in this chapter is about winning it back.

Total, elastic and inelastic. Chapter 3 split the total cross-section into partial ones that add. For two protons at 13 TeV TOTEM measured a total of 110.6 mb, made of 31.0 mb elastic and 79.5 mb inelastic. They do different things to a beam. - Elastic: both protons survive, deflected by a small angle. At the LHC in Run 2 the typical elastic angle is about 0.03 mrad, very close to the beam’s rms divergence (Chao and others, §3.3.2); at the collision point in 2018 that divergence was \(\sqrt{\varepsilon/\beta^*} = 0.031\) mrad. So most elastically scattered protons stay in the beam; they add a little to its emittance. - Inelastic: the protons break up and new particles are made. These are the collisions the detectors record, and both protons are lost from the beams.

So the cross-section that removes particles, \(\sigma_{\text{r}}\) in section 10, is close to the inelastic one. The LHC’s measured ratio of beam loss rate to luminosity is about 81 mb (Chao and others, §3.3.2). At the Tevatron, where elastic angles were larger compared with the beam divergence, about 60 per cent of the elastic cross-section was added to the inelastic. Pile-up (section 10) counts the inelastic collisions, since those are what the detectors must separate. At work: at \(2 \times 10^{34}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\) an experiment sees \(2 \times 10^{34} \times 79.5 \times 10^{-27} = 1.6 \times 10^{9}\) inelastic collisions a second. With two such experiments and 81 mb, each beam loses \(3.2 \times 10^9\) protons a second, 4.1 per cent per hour of a beam of 2556 bunches of \(1.1 \times 10^{11}\).

Exercise A1. A process has a cross-section of 1 fb. How much integrated luminosity gives 100 events, with what statistical uncertainty, and how long does it take at a steady \(2 \times 10^{34}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\)?

Answer

\(N_{\text{ev}} = \sigma\int\mathcal{L}\,dt\), so 100 events need 100 fb\(^{-1}\) \(= 10^{41}\) cm\(^{-2}\). The count is uncertain by \(\sqrt{100} = 10\), 10 per cent. At \(2 \times 10^{34}\) it takes \(5 \times 10^6\) s, about 58 days of collisions; with fills that decay and turnarounds between them, a good part of a year.

Exercise A2. Show that the luminosity of a fixed target can be written as \(\mathcal{L} = (I/e)\,\rho_A\,N_A\,\ell/A\) for a beam current \(I\) on a target of density \(\rho_A\) (g/cm³), molar mass \(A\) (g/mol) and thickness \(\ell\), and evaluate it for 1 µA of protons on 1 mm of lead (11.35 g/cm³, 207.2 g/mol).

Answer

The projectile rate is \(I/e\) and the number of atoms per cm³ is \(\rho_AN_A/A\). For lead, \(n = 11.35 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}/207.2 = 3.30 \times 10^{22}\) cm\(^{-3}\); \(I/e = 6.24 \times 10^{12}\) s\(^{-1}\); with \(\ell = 0.1\) cm, \(\mathcal{L} = 6.24 \times 10^{12} \times 3.30 \times 10^{22} \times 0.1 = 2.1 \times 10^{34}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\), as much as the LHC from a microampere on a millimetre.

Exercise A3. Why does the elastic cross-section not appear in burn-off at the LHC? If it did, in full, how much faster would the beams burn off?

Answer

An elastically scattered proton survives and is deflected by about 0.03 mrad, comparable with the beam’s divergence at the collision point, so it stays inside the aperture and is not lost. If every elastic collision removed a proton, \(\sigma_{\text{r}}\) would rise from about 80 mb to the total, 110.6 mb, and burn-off would be \(110.6/79.5 = 1.39\) times faster.

In one page

The wall. A beam striking a target at rest spends most of its energy moving the centre of mass. For a 7 TeV proton on a proton at rest the centre-of-mass energy is 114.6 GeV. Two such beams meeting head on give 14 TeV. To match that on a fixed target, the beam would need \(1.04 \times 10^{17}\) eV.

The idea. Store two beams and make them cross again and again. In 1956 Kerst and his MURA colleagues proposed intersecting beams, and O’Neill proposed storage rings fed by a synchrotron. The price is rate: a bunch of \(10^{11}\) protons squeezed to 17 µm is a target about \(10^{9}\) times thinner than a metre of liquid hydrogen. So everything in a collider serves one number, the luminosity \(\mathcal{L}\), defined by

\[ \frac{dN_{\text{ev}}}{dt} = \mathcal{L}\,\sigma . \]

The equations to remember.

\[ \sqrt{s} = 2E \ \text{(head on)}, \qquad \sqrt{s} \approx \sqrt{2Emc^2} \ \text{(fixed target)} \]

\[ \mathcal{L} = \frac{f_0\,n_b\,N_1 N_2}{4\pi\sigma_x\sigma_y}\,S, \qquad S = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\phi_{\text{P}}^2}}, \quad \phi_{\text{P}} = \frac{\sigma_z}{\sigma_x}\tan\frac{\theta_c}{2} \]

with \(f_0\) the revolution frequency (Hz), \(n_b\) the colliding bunch pairs, \(N_{1,2}\) the particles per bunch, \(\sigma_{x,y}\) the rms beam sizes at the collision point (cm), \(\theta_c\) the full crossing angle and \(\phi_{\text{P}}\) the Piwinski angle (subscripted because \(\phi\) alone is the RF phase). Near the collision point, in empty space, with \(\beta^*\) the value of the betatron function at the collision point (m; the beam’s rms size there is \(\sigma^* = \sqrt{\varepsilon\beta^*}\) for a beam of emittance \(\varepsilon\); written without the subscript \(x\) or \(y\) when both planes share it),

\[ \beta_x(s) = \beta^* + \frac{s^2}{\beta^*} , \]

so a small \(\beta^*\), which makes a small spot, means a huge beam in the final quadrupoles. Each beam focuses the other, with a tune shift per crossing for round beams of

\[ \xi = \frac{N r_{\text{cl}}}{4\pi\varepsilon_n} , \]

where \(N\) is the number of particles in the opposing bunch, \(r_{\text{cl}} = e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0mc^2)\) the classical radius of the particle (m) and \(\varepsilon_n = \beta\gamma\varepsilon\) the normalised emittance (m rad), the emittance that stays fixed as the beam is accelerated. \(\xi\) is the shift of the tune, the number of betatron oscillations per turn, that one crossing gives a particle near the axis. It is limited to about 0.01 per crossing for protons and up to about 0.1 for electrons, which radiation damping protects.

Numbers worth knowing. 1 cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\) \(= 10^4\) m\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\); \(10^{34}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\) \(= 10\) nb\(^{-1}\) per second \(= 0.864\) fb\(^{-1}\) per day of collisions. AdA (1964) reached about \(10^{25}\), the ISR \(1.4 \times 10^{32}\) (1982), the LHC \(2.1 \times 10^{34}\) (Run 2), and SuperKEKB \(5.32 \times 10^{34}\) (June 2026). LHC design: \(N = 1.15 \times 10^{11}\), \(n_b = 2808\), \(f_0 = 11.245\) kHz, \(\beta^* = 0.55\) m, \(\sigma^* = 16.6\) µm, 285 µrad, \(S = 0.84\), giving \(10^{34}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\) with 27 collisions per crossing. Absolute luminosity comes from van der Meer’s beam-separation scan (1968), now good to 0.83 per cent at ATLAS.

The wall

By the mid-1950s the Cosmotron gave 3.3 GeV protons and the Bevatron 6.2 GeV, and Brookhaven and CERN were planning machines of about 25 GeV (chapter 16). Each step up in beam energy bought less than it seemed. As chapter 1 showed, a beam of total energy \(E\) on a nucleon at rest gives a centre-of-mass energy \(\sqrt{s} \approx \sqrt{2Em_pc^2}\): doubling the machine gains a factor of 1.4 in what can be made.

The way round was old. Rolf Wideröe applied in 1943 for a German patent on beams of protons or deuterons circulating in opposite directions in a ring, so that the centre of mass is at rest; it was published only in 1953. When he discussed it with Bruno Touschek, Touschek found it obvious and trivial (Hübner’s account). Nobody could see how to use it. Two beams are thin targets. A beam of \(10^{12}\) protons per second through a metre of liquid hydrogen (70.85 kg/m\(^3\), \(4.23 \times 10^{22}\) atoms per cm\(^3\)) gives a fixed-target luminosity of \(4 \times 10^{36}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\). A colliding beam has to recover this factor by making the particles cross the other beam millions of times, in a spot as small as can be focused, with enough particles stored. No one in 1950 knew how to store an intense beam for hours.

Two developments of the mid-1950s opened the way. The first was strong focusing, which shrank beams and made the needed apertures affordable (chapter 16). The second came from the Midwestern Universities Research Association (MURA), whose fixed-field alternating-gradient (FFAG) accelerators could hold many turns of beam at once. In a letter to the Physical Review of 15 April 1956, D. W. Kerst, F. T. Cole, H. R. Crane, L. W. Jones, L. J. Laslett, T. Ohkawa, A. M. Sessler, K. R. Symon, K. M. Terwilliger and N. Vogt Nilsen proposed two FFAG accelerators whose beams intersect. Burton Richter, recounting it, writes that Kerst pointed out that two 21.6 GeV beams colliding head on would match a 1000 GeV accelerator striking a fixed proton target. MURA also invented radio-frequency stacking, which accumulates many injected pulses side by side in momentum.

At the CERN symposium of 1956 and in a letter in the same volume of the Physical Review (page 1418), Gerard O’Neill of Princeton proposed the storage-ring synchrotron. A synchrotron accelerates the beam; it is then transferred into two storage rings with a common straight section, where the beams interact. Since a beam at high energy is small, the storage rings could have small magnets. He added that electron storage rings in the GeV range would allow electron-electron collisions at about 100 times the centre-of-mass energy then available, and that radiation damping might make capture easier.

That remark decided the order of history. Injecting protons into a storage ring was thought very hard; O’Neill’s first scheme, a scattering foil, was soon shown impossible. An electron injected off axis oscillates, radiates, and its oscillation damps away, so another pulse can be added on top: Liouville’s theorem, which allows protons to be stacked only side by side in momentum, does not hold for radiating particles (chapter 20). So the first colliders were small electron rings, and the first proton collider came fifteen years after the proposals.

The physics

1. The centre-of-mass energy

The invariant \(s\) is the squared length of the total four-momentum of the colliding pair,

\[ s = (E_1 + E_2)^2 - |\mathbf{p}_1 c + \mathbf{p}_2 c|^2 , \]

the same in every inertial frame (Landau and Lifshitz, volume 2, sections 9 and 11; derived in chapter 1). It is the relativistic version of a familiar idea: in an inelastic collision (F3, section 6) only the kinetic energy seen from the centre of mass can be turned into something else, because the motion of the centre of mass itself must carry on, by momentum conservation (F3, section 3). \(\sqrt{s}\) is the energy in the frame where the total momentum vanishes, all of it available for making new particles.

Each particle obeys \(E_i^2 - |\mathbf{p}_i|^2c^2 = m_i^2c^4\), so expanding the squares in \(s\) always leaves \(m_1^2c^4 + m_2^2c^4\) plus the cross terms \(2E_1E_2 - 2\mathbf{p}_1\cdot\mathbf{p}_2c^2\). For a beam particle of mass \(m_1\) and total energy \(E\) striking a particle of mass \(m_2\) at rest, \(E_2 = m_2c^2\) and \(\mathbf{p}_2 = 0\), so the dot product vanishes:

\[ s = m_1^2c^4 + m_2^2c^4 + 2Em_2c^2 . \]

For two beams of total energies \(E_1\) and \(E_2\) meeting head on, with \(\mathbf{p}_1 = -\mathbf{p}_2\) in direction, \(\mathbf{p}_1\cdot\mathbf{p}_2 = -|\mathbf{p}_1||\mathbf{p}_2|\), and for \(E_i \gg m_ic^2\) each \(|\mathbf{p}_i|c \approx E_i\):

\[ s = m_1^2c^4 + m_2^2c^4 + 2\left(E_1E_2 + |\mathbf{p}_1||\mathbf{p}_2|c^2\right) \approx 4E_1E_2 \quad (E_i \gg m_ic^2). \]

For equal energies, \(\sqrt{s} = 2E\). Inverting the fixed-target formula, the beam energy that would match a collider of given \(\sqrt{s}\) on a target of the same mass \(m\) is

\[ E_{\text{FT}} = \frac{s - 2m^2c^4}{2mc^2} . \]

Worked numbers. The LHC design, 7 TeV per beam, gives \(\sqrt{s} = 14\) TeV and \(E_{\text{FT}} = 1.04 \times 10^{17}\) eV; Run 3 at 6.8 TeV gives 13.6 TeV and \(9.9 \times 10^{16}\) eV. The same 7 TeV beam on a hydrogen target gives \(\sqrt{s} = 114.6\) GeV, 122 times less. Kerst’s two 21.6 GeV beams give \(\sqrt{s} = 43.2\) GeV and \(E_{\text{FT}} = 994\) GeV, his “1000 GeV” (taking 21.6 GeV as the kinetic energy instead gives 1082 GeV). The ISR at 26.5 GeV/\(c\) in May 1971 matched a 1498 GeV fixed-target beam, the 1500 GeV quoted by Hübner. For electrons the factor is far larger: LEP’s 104.5 GeV beams match \(4.3 \times 10^{16}\) eV electrons on electrons at rest.

Unequal beams. With \(E_1 \neq E_2\) the centre of mass moves along the more energetic beam. Treat the colliding pair as one object of mass \(M = \sqrt{s}/c^2\) and total momentum \(\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{p}_1 + \mathbf{p}_2\); since \(|\mathbf{P}| = \gamma\beta Mc\) for any object, its velocity has \(\beta\gamma = |\mathbf{P}|c/(Mc^2) = |\mathbf{p}_1 + \mathbf{p}_2|c/\sqrt{s}\). Head on, the momenta subtract, and with \(|\mathbf{p}_i|c \approx E_i\) this is \(\beta\gamma \approx (E_1 - E_2)/\sqrt{s}\). HERA collided 27.5 GeV electrons with 920 GeV protons: \(\sqrt{s} = 2\sqrt{E_eE_p} = 318\) GeV, equivalent to 53.9 TeV electrons on protons at rest. The B factories used this motion on purpose: PEP-II (9.0 GeV electrons on 3.1 GeV positrons) made B mesons moving with \(\beta\gamma = 0.56\), so that they travel a measurable 0.25 mm before decaying (exercise 10).

2. Luminosity: the definition

The event rate for a process of cross-section \(\sigma\) (cm\(^2\)) is

\[ \frac{dN_{\text{ev}}}{dt} = \mathcal{L}\,\sigma , \]

which defines the luminosity \(\mathcal{L}\) in cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\). It holds everything the machine contributes; \(\sigma\) holds the physics.

For two beams with particle densities \(n_1(\mathbf{r},t)\) and \(n_2(\mathbf{r},t)\) (m\(^{-3}\)) and velocities \(\mathbf{v}_1\), \(\mathbf{v}_2\), the number of events per unit time in a volume element is \(\sigma K n_1 n_2\,dV\). The reason is the thin-target rule of the pre-requisite above, seen from beam 1. A particle of beam 1 sees the particles of beam 2 rush past at their relative speed \(K\), so the flux falling on it is \(n_2K\) particles per m² per second, and it undergoes the process at the rate \(\sigma n_2K\); the volume element holds \(n_1\,dV\) such particles. The kinematic factor is

\[ K = \sqrt{(\mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_2)^2 - \frac{(\mathbf{v}_1 \times \mathbf{v}_2)^2}{c^2}} . \]

This is the relativistically correct flux factor, which makes the cross-section an invariant (Landau and Lifshitz, volume 2, section 12; quoted here, not derived). Its first term is simply the relative velocity of the two beams as measured in the laboratory; the second, a correction of order \(v^2/c^2\), matters only when the beams are not parallel. For two beams moving head on at \(\pm v\), \(\mathbf{v}_1 \times \mathbf{v}_2 = 0\) (F2, section 3: the cross product of parallel vectors vanishes) and \(K = |\mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_2| = 2v \approx 2c\). (A closing speed of \(2c\) measured in the laboratory breaks no law: no single object moves faster than light.) So

\[ \mathcal{L} = K\int n_1 n_2\,dV . \]

Pre-requisite (continued): Special functions and integrals (Bessel, Gaussian, error function)

Pre-requisite. This continues Special functions and integrals in chapter 2, which proved \(\int e^{-x^2}dx = \sqrt\pi\) and defined the complementary error function, and its continuation in chapter 6, which gave the Gaussian as a distribution with an rms width. It adds the integrals a collider’s luminosity is made of: the Gaussian integral with any coefficient, the integral of a Gaussian’s square, the overlap of two Gaussians of different widths and offset from each other, the rule that their widths add in quadrature, and the integral \(\int e^{-u^2}/(u^2+t^2)\,du\) behind the hourglass factor. Section 3 uses the first three, section 4 the overlap of offset profiles and the product of Gaussians, section 5 the last integral, and section 9 the quadrature rule. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 5, §110 (the Gaussian distribution); Chao and others, Handbook, §4.1 (the overlap integral and its head-on forms for Gaussian bunches); and S. Y. Lee, Accelerator Physics, exercise 1.7 (the overlap of offset Gaussian bunches) and exercise 2.2.17 (the hourglass factor in closed form).

Reminder: chapter 2 showed \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}dx = \sqrt\pi\) by squaring the integral and doing it in polar coordinates, and defined \(\operatorname{erfc}(x) = (2/\sqrt\pi)\int_x^\infty e^{-t^2}dt\).

1. Any coefficient. Put \(x = \sqrt a\,u\) with \(a > 0\), so \(dx = \sqrt a\,du\):

\[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-au^2}\,du = \frac{1}{\sqrt a}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}\,dx = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a}} . \]

With \(a = 1/(2\sigma^2)\) this is \(\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma\), which is why the normalised profile of a beam of rms width \(\sigma\) is

\[ \rho(u) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}\,e^{-u^2/2\sigma^2}, \qquad \int\rho\,du = 1 . \]

\(\rho\) is in m\(^{-1}\): the fraction of the beam per metre of \(u\). A bunch of \(N\) particles has \(N\rho_x(x)\rho_y(y)\) particles per m² across the beam.

2. The square of a Gaussian. In section 3 each particle of one beam meets the density of the other, so the luminosity holds \(\int\rho^2\,du\). The square is \(e^{-u^2/\sigma^2}/(2\pi\sigma^2)\), a Gaussian with \(a = 1/\sigma^2\):

\[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\rho^2\,du = \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2}\sqrt{\pi}\,\sigma = \frac{1}{2\sqrt\pi\,\sigma} . \]

It is an inverse length: the beam behaves as if it were spread evenly over a width \(2\sqrt\pi\,\sigma = 3.54\sigma\). In two planes the effective area is \((2\sqrt\pi\sigma_x)(2\sqrt\pi\sigma_y) = 4\pi\sigma_x\sigma_y\), the denominator of the luminosity formula. At work: for \(\sigma = 16.6\) µm, \(\int\rho^2\,dx = 1/(2\sqrt\pi \times 1.66 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{cm}) = 170\) cm\(^{-1}\).

3. Two Gaussians, offset. Now let the beams differ in width, \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\), and let beam 2 be displaced by \(d\). The overlap is

\[ O(d) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\rho_1(u)\,\rho_2(u - d)\,du = \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_1\sigma_2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left[-\frac{u^2}{2\sigma_1^2} - \frac{(u-d)^2}{2\sigma_2^2}\right]du . \]

The exponent is a quadratic in \(u\). Complete the square. Write \(\Sigma^2 = \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2\) and \(A = 1/\sigma_1^2 + 1/\sigma_2^2 = \Sigma^2/(\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2)\). Expanding and regrouping,

\[ \frac{u^2}{2\sigma_1^2} + \frac{(u-d)^2}{2\sigma_2^2} = \frac{A}{2}\,(u - u_0)^2 + \frac{d^2}{2\Sigma^2}, \qquad u_0 = \frac{\sigma_1^2}{\Sigma^2}\,d . \]

(Check: the \(u^2\) terms give \(A/2\); the \(u\) terms give \(-d/\sigma_2^2 = -Au_0\); the constant left over is \(d^2/(2\sigma_2^2) - Au_0^2/2 = d^2/(2\sigma_2^2)(1 - \sigma_1^2/\Sigma^2) = d^2/(2\Sigma^2)\).) The second term does not depend on \(u\) and comes out of the integral; the first is a Gaussian in \(u\), integral \(\sqrt{2\pi/A} = \sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_1\sigma_2/\Sigma\). So

\[ O(d) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\Sigma}\,e^{-d^2/2\Sigma^2}, \qquad \Sigma = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2} . \]

The overlap is itself a Gaussian in the offset, with an rms width equal to the two widths added in quadrature. This is the convolution of two Gaussians, and it is the same rule as for the sum of two independent random quantities: their variances add. A direct numerical integral with \(\sigma_1 = 10\), \(\sigma_2 = 20\) and \(d = 15\) gives 0.0142465, as the formula does. Four uses follow in the chapter. - Unequal beams. At \(d = 0\) in both planes the overlap is \(1/(2\pi\Sigma_x\Sigma_y)\), so \(\mathcal{L} = f_0n_bN_1N_2/(2\pi\Sigma_x\Sigma_y)\); for equal beams \(\Sigma^2 = 2\sigma^2\) and this becomes \(1/(4\pi\sigma_x\sigma_y)\) again. The Handbook’s table of head-on luminosities in §4.1 is written in this form. - Beams not head on. For equal beams the overlap falls by \(e^{-d^2/2\Sigma^2} = e^{-d^2/4\sigma^2}\). At the 2018 LHC beam size of 9.3 µm, an offset of 3 µm keeps 0.974 of the luminosity, 5 µm keeps 0.930, a whole \(\sigma\) only 0.779: the reason for the optimisation scans of “At the bench”. - The van der Meer scan. The rate against separation is \(R(d) \propto e^{-d^2/2\Sigma^2}\), whatever the two widths are separately, and the area under it divided by its peak is \(\sqrt{2\pi}\,\Sigma\) (section 9). Two beams of 100 µm give \(\Sigma = 141\) µm. - Where the collisions are. In section 4 a particle at \(u\) in one bunch meets one at \(w\) in the other at \(s = (u + w)/2\). Both are Gaussian with rms \(\sigma_z\), so \(u + w\) has variance \(2\sigma_z^2\) and \(s\) has variance \(\sigma_z^2/2\): the luminous region is \(\sigma_z/\sqrt2\), 5.34 cm for the LHC design’s 7.55 cm bunches.

4. Products of Gaussians in one variable. Section 4 multiplies two Gaussian factors in \(s\), \(e^{-s^2/\sigma_z^2}\) for where the collisions happen and \(e^{-s^2\tan^2(\theta_c/2)/\sigma_x^2}\) for the loss of overlap. Their exponents simply add, so the product is a Gaussian with \(a = 1/\sigma_z^2 + \tan^2(\theta_c/2)/\sigma_x^2\), and step 1 does the rest. A product of Gaussians centred at the same point is a narrower Gaussian; that is the whole of the Piwinski factor.

5. The hourglass integral. Section 5 needs

\[ F(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-u^2}}{u^2 + t^2}\,du \qquad (t > 0) . \]

The trick is to write the awkward factor as an integral of exponentials, which combine with \(e^{-u^2}\). For any \(b > 0\), \(\int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda b}\,d\lambda = 1/b\); with \(b = u^2 + t^2\),

\[ \frac{1}{u^2 + t^2} = \int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda(u^2 + t^2)}\,d\lambda . \]

Put this in and swap the order of the two integrals (allowed here, since everything is positive). Then do the \(u\) integral first, a Gaussian with \(a = 1 + \lambda\):

\[ F(t) = \int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda t^2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{1+\lambda}}\,d\lambda = \sqrt\pi\,e^{t^2}\int_1^\infty \frac{e^{-\mu t^2}}{\sqrt\mu}\,d\mu , \]

with \(\mu = 1 + \lambda\). Now let \(\mu = v^2/t^2\), so \(d\mu = 2v\,dv/t^2\) and \(1/\sqrt\mu = t/v\); the limits become \(v = t\) and \(\infty\):

\[ F(t) = \sqrt\pi\,e^{t^2}\,\frac{2}{t}\int_t^\infty e^{-v^2}\,dv = \frac{\pi}{t}\,e^{t^2}\operatorname{erfc}(t) . \]

With \(u = s/\sigma_z\) and \(t = \beta^*/\sigma_z\), the hourglass factor of section 5 is \(R_{\text{hg}} = (t^2/\sqrt\pi)\,F(t) = \sqrt\pi\,t\,e^{t^2}\operatorname{erfc}(t)\) (divide the integrand’s top and bottom by \(\sigma_z^2\) to see the factor \(t^2\)). Numerical integration agrees to at least thirteen digits at every \(t\) tried; at \(t = 1\) both give \(F = 1.34329\).

Its two ends. For a long bunch, \(t \ll 1\), \(\operatorname{erfc}(t) \to 1\) and \(R_{\text{hg}} \approx \sqrt\pi\,t\): the luminosity grows only in proportion to \(\beta^*/\sigma_z\), and squeezing \(\beta^*\) further gains nothing, since the gain of \(1/\beta^*\) in the formula is cancelled. At \(t = 0.15\) this estimate gives 0.27 against the exact 0.23. For a short bunch, \(t \gg 1\), integrate \(\int_t^\infty e^{-v^2}dv\) by parts, writing \(e^{-v^2} = (-1/2v)\,d(e^{-v^2})/dv\):

\[ \int_t^\infty e^{-v^2}dv = \frac{e^{-t^2}}{2t} - \int_t^\infty\frac{e^{-v^2}}{2v^2}\,dv \approx \frac{e^{-t^2}}{2t}\left(1 - \frac{1}{2t^2}\right) , \]

the second step doing the same once more to the remaining integral and keeping its leading term. So \(R_{\text{hg}} \approx 1 - 1/(2t^2)\). For the LHC design, \(t = 7.3\), this gives 0.9906 against the exact 0.9909. In a program, compute \(e^{t^2}\operatorname{erfc}(t)\) with the scaled function (erfcx in SciPy): for large \(t\) the two factors overflow and underflow separately.

Exercise B1. Two beams collide head on with rms sizes 16.6 µm and 18.0 µm in both planes. What fraction of the luminosity of two 16.6 µm beams do they give, for the same bunch populations?

Answer

\(\mathcal{L} \propto 1/(2\pi\Sigma_x\Sigma_y) = 1/(2\pi(\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2))\) for round beams, against \(1/(4\pi\sigma_1^2)\). The ratio is \(2\sigma_1^2/(\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2) = 2 \times 275.6/(275.6 + 324.0) = 0.919\).

Exercise B2. With equal beams of 9.3 µm, what offset between their centres costs 1 per cent of the luminosity?

Answer

\(e^{-d^2/4\sigma^2} = 0.99\) gives \(d = 2\sigma\sqrt{-\ln 0.99} = 2 \times 9.3 \times 0.1003 = 1.86\) µm. The beams must be steered to within two microns of each other at the collision point.

Exercise B3. Evaluate \(R_{\text{hg}}\) at \(t = 0.5\) and \(t = 5\) from the closed form, using \(\operatorname{erfc}(0.5) = 0.4795\) and \(\operatorname{erfc}(5) = 1.537 \times 10^{-12}\), and compare with the two limiting forms.

Answer

\(t = 0.5\): \(\sqrt\pi \times 0.5 \times e^{0.25} \times 0.4795 = 1.7725 \times 0.5 \times 1.2840 \times 0.4795 = 0.546\); the long-bunch form \(\sqrt\pi\,t\) gives 0.89, useless here. \(t = 5\): \(\sqrt\pi \times 5 \times e^{25} \times 1.537 \times 10^{-12} = 0.981\); the short-bunch form gives \(1 - 1/50 = 0.980\). Each limit is good only well inside its own end.

3. Luminosity of two Gaussian bunches

Take bunches of \(N_1\) and \(N_2\) particles with Gaussian profiles, rms sizes \(\sigma_x\), \(\sigma_y\) the same for both beams, and rms length \(\sigma_z\), moving at \(\pm c\) along \(s\):

\[ n_i(x,y,s,t) = N_i\,\rho_x(x)\,\rho_y(y)\,\rho_z(s \mp ct), \qquad \rho_u(u) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_u}e^{-u^2/2\sigma_u^2} . \]

The number of events in one crossing is

\[ \Delta N_{\text{ev}} = \sigma\,N_1N_2 \cdot 2c\int\!\!\int \rho_z(s - ct)\,\rho_z(s + ct)\,ds\,dt \cdot \int \rho_x^2\,dx \int \rho_y^2\,dy . \]

This is \(\sigma\int\!\!\int K\,n_1n_2\,dV\,dt\) from section 2, with \(K = 2c\): the transverse factors of \(n_1n_2\) give \(\rho_x^2\rho_y^2\), whose integrals over \(x\) and \(y\) separate from the rest, and the time integral runs over the whole crossing.

The longitudinal integral. Change variables to \(u = s - ct\) and \(w = s + ct\): \(u\) is a particle’s place measured inside bunch 1, \(w\) inside bunch 2. In the double integral each small patch \(ds\,dt\) becomes a patch \(du\,dw\) of a different area. The change is linear, \((u, w) = (s - ct,\ s + ct)\), a matrix with rows \((1, -c)\) and \((1, c)\), and a linear change scales every area by its determinant (chapter 8, the determinant and areas): \(1 \times c - (-c) \times 1 = 2c\). This factor is called the Jacobian of the change. So \(du\,dw = 2c\,ds\,dt\), that is \(ds\,dt = du\,dw/(2c)\), and

\[ 2c\int\!\!\int \rho_z(u)\,\rho_z(w)\,\frac{du\,dw}{2c} = 1 . \]

since each normalised profile integrates to 1. Every particle of one bunch passes through the whole of the other; the bunch length drops out.

The transverse integrals. \(\int \rho_x^2\,dx = \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_x^2}\int e^{-x^2/\sigma_x^2}dx = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}\,\sigma_x}\), and the same for \(y\). So one crossing gives \(\Delta N_{\text{ev}} = \sigma N_1N_2/(4\pi\sigma_x\sigma_y)\).

Crossings per second. With \(n_b\) colliding bunch pairs and revolution frequency \(f_0\), there are \(f_0 n_b\) crossings per second at this collision point:

\[ \boxed{\ \mathcal{L} = \frac{f_0\,n_b\,N_1N_2}{4\pi\sigma_x\sigma_y}\ } \]

If the two beams differ in size, the same integrals give \(\mathcal{L} = f_0 n_b N_1N_2/(2\pi\Sigma_x\Sigma_y)\) with \(\Sigma_u = \sqrt{\sigma_{1u}^2 + \sigma_{2u}^2}\), the convolved sizes that the van der Meer scan measures (section 9).

Assumptions. Gaussian profiles that do not change during the crossing; no crossing angle; beam size constant along the bunch (no hourglass); beams centred on each other; \(\beta \approx 1\). Each is lifted below.

Units. Luminosity is quoted in cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\) by habit; 1 cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\) \(= 10^4\) m\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\). The barn is \(10^{-24}\) cm\(^2\), so \(10^{34}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\) is 10 nb\(^{-1}\) per second, and \(1\) Hz/µb (the unit on LHC displays) is \(10^{30}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\). The integrated luminosity \(\int\mathcal{L}\,dt\) is counted in inverse barns: 1 fb\(^{-1}\) \(= 10^{39}\) cm\(^{-2}\). The expected number of events is \(N_{\text{ev}} = \sigma\int\mathcal{L}\,dt\).

Worked number: the LHC design. At 7 TeV, \(\gamma = 7460.5\). With normalised emittance \(\varepsilon_n = 3.75\) µm, the geometric emittance is \(\varepsilon = \varepsilon_n/\beta\gamma = 5.03 \times 10^{-10}\) m, and at \(\beta^* = 0.55\) m the rms size at the collision point is \(\sigma^* = \sqrt{\varepsilon\beta^*} = 16.6\) µm (for round beams \(\sigma^*\) stands for \(\sigma_x^* = \sigma_y^*\)) (16.7 µm is also quoted for the design). With \(N = 1.15 \times 10^{11}\), \(n_b = 2808\) and \(f_0 = 11\,245\) Hz, the formula gives \(1.20 \times 10^{34}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\) head on. The crossing angle (section 4) multiplies it by 0.836, giving \(1.0 \times 10^{34}\), the design value (parameters from the HL-LHC technical design report, table 1-1 of Apollinari and others and table 2-1 of its chapter 2; table 1-1 misprints \(N\) as \(1.5 \times 10^{11}\)). The same numbers give a beam current \(n_bNef_0 = 0.58\) A and a stored energy of 362 MJ per beam.

Worked number: events. ATLAS recorded 140.1 fb\(^{-1}\) in Run 2. The gluon-fusion cross-section for a 125 GeV Higgs boson at 13 TeV is 48.58 pb, so that data held about \(140.1 \times 10^{3}\ \text{pb}^{-1} \times 48.58\ \text{pb} = 6.8\) million Higgs bosons from this process alone, nearly all of them lost to backgrounds or undetectable decays.

4. The crossing angle and the Piwinski factor

With many bunches in a shared beam pipe, bunches would meet not only at the collision point but every half bunch spacing on either side: at 25 ns spacing, every 3.75 m. The LHC avoids these parasitic collisions by crossing the beams at a small angle, \(\theta_c = 285\) µrad in the design. The price is overlap (Figure 23.1).

Figure 23.1. Two bunches meeting, not to scale. Top: head on, as section 3 assumes; the ellipses mark \(\pm\sigma_z\) along the beam and \(\pm\sigma_x\) across it (a real LHC bunch is 4500 times longer than it is wide). Bottom: with a crossing angle, the angle much exaggerated. Each bunch travels along its own line through the collision point, at \(\pm\theta_c/2\) to the axis \(s\). A distance \(s\) from the collision point their centres are \(d = 2s\tan(\theta_c/2)\) apart, so the two bunches overlap only near the middle, over a length of order \(\sigma_x/\tan(\theta_c/2)\). When that length is short compared with \(\sigma_z\), the Piwinski angle is large and much of each bunch never meets the other.

Where the collisions happen. From the change of variables in section 3, a particle at position \(u\) in bunch 1 meets one at \(w\) in bunch 2 at \(s = (u + w)/2\). For two Gaussians of rms \(\sigma_z\), \(s\) is Gaussian with rms \(\sigma_z/\sqrt{2}\): the luminous region is shorter than the bunch by \(\sqrt{2}\).

The separation at \(s\). With half-angle \(\theta_c/2\) in the \(x\) plane, the two bunch centres at the moment they overlap at \(s\) sit at \(x = \pm s\tan(\theta_c/2)\), a separation \(d = 2s\tan(\theta_c/2)\). Two equal Gaussians separated by \(d\) overlap less by \(e^{-d^2/4\sigma_x^2}\) (the transverse integral of section 3 with one profile shifted).

The integral. Weighting by where collisions happen: a Gaussian of rms \(\sigma_z/\sqrt2\) is \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,(\sigma_z/\sqrt2)}e^{-s^2/(2\sigma_z^2/2)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt\pi\,\sigma_z}e^{-s^2/\sigma_z^2}\), and each collision at \(s\) is reduced by the overlap factor with \(d^2/4\sigma_x^2 = s^2\tan^2(\theta_c/2)/\sigma_x^2\). So

\[ S = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\sigma_z}\,e^{-s^2/\sigma_z^2}\,e^{-s^2\tan^2(\theta_c/2)/\sigma_x^2}\,ds = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\phi_{\text{P}}^2}}, \qquad \phi_{\text{P}} = \frac{\sigma_z}{\sigma_x}\tan\frac{\theta_c}{2} . \]

The step is the Gaussian integral \(\int e^{-as^2}ds = \sqrt{\pi/a}\) with \(a = 1/\sigma_z^2 + \tan^2(\theta_c/2)/\sigma_x^2\): then \(S = \sqrt{\pi/a}/(\sqrt\pi\,\sigma_z) = 1/\sqrt{a\sigma_z^2} = 1/\sqrt{1 + \sigma_z^2\tan^2(\theta_c/2)/\sigma_x^2}\). The number \(\phi_{\text{P}}\) is the Piwinski angle: the bunch length projected across the beam, in units of the beam width. Small terms of order \(\theta_c^2\) in \(K\) and in the tilt of the profiles are neglected.

Worked number. LHC design: \(\phi_{\text{P}} = 0.0755 \times 142.5 \times 10^{-6}/16.6 \times 10^{-6} = 0.647\) and \(S = 0.840\); with the hourglass effect of section 5 included, a numerical integral gives 0.836, the value in the HL-LHC table. In Run 2 (2018) with \(\beta^* = 0.30\) m, 320 µrad, \(\varepsilon_n = 2.0\) µm at 6.5 TeV and \(\sigma_z = 8\) cm (PDG), \(\sigma^* = 9.3\) µm and \(\phi_{\text{P}} = 1.37\), so \(S = 0.59\). For the HL-LHC, \(\beta^* = 0.15\) m, 590 µrad and \(\varepsilon_n = 2.5\) µm at 7 TeV, with \(\sigma_z = 7.55\) cm, give \(\phi_{\text{P}} = 3.14\) and \(S = 0.30\): most of the gain from focusing would be lost. Crab cavities, which tilt each bunch by half the crossing angle with a transverse RF kick (chapter 13), restore head-on overlap; KEKB first used superconducting crab cavities in a collider, from February 2007.

Coasting beams at a large angle: the ISR. The ISR’s two rings crossed at \(\theta_c = 14.77^\circ\), and their beams were unbunched. Then each beam is a ribbon with \(I_i/(ec)\) particles per unit length. The kinematic factor for beams of speed \(c\) whose directions make an angle \(\pi - \theta_c\) is \(K = c(1+\cos\theta_c)\) (put \(\mathbf{v}_1\cdot\mathbf{v}_2 = -c^2\cos\theta_c\) into the formula of section 2). The ribbons, of widths \(w_1\) and \(w_2\), overlap over an area \(w_1w_2/\sin\theta_c\) in the horizontal plane (two straight strips crossing at an angle \(\theta_c\) overlap in a parallelogram: its sides along each strip are \(w_1/\sin\theta_c\) and \(w_2/\sin\theta_c\), the angle between them is \(\theta_c\), and its area is the product of the sides times \(\sin\theta_c\)). With vertical profiles \(g_1(y)\) and \(g_2(y)\) of unit area,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L} &= c(1+\cos\theta_c)\,\frac{I_1I_2}{e^2c^2}\,\frac{1}{\sin\theta_c}\int g_1g_2\,dy = \frac{I_1I_2}{e^2c\,h_{\text{eff}}\tan(\theta_c/2)}, \\ h_{\text{eff}} &= \frac{1}{\int g_1(y)g_2(y)\,dy} , \end{aligned} \]

using \((1+\cos\theta_c)/\sin\theta_c = \cot(\theta_c/2)\). The widths cancel; only the effective height \(h_{\text{eff}}\) matters. This is the formula van der Meer set out to calibrate in 1968 (section 9).

5. The hourglass effect

Near the collision point the beam size grows with distance, \(\sigma(s) = \sigma^*\sqrt{1 + s^2/\beta^{*2}}\) (section 6). If the bunch is not short compared with \(\beta^*\), collisions away from the centre happen between fatter beams. (The growth comes from \(\beta_x(s) = \beta^* + s^2/\beta^*\) of section 6, with \(\sigma = \sqrt{\varepsilon\beta_x}\); it is used here ahead of its derivation.) The overlap of two equal beams is \(1/(4\pi\sigma_x\sigma_y)\) (section 3), so it falls in proportion to \(\sigma_x^{*2}/\sigma_x^2(s)\) times the same for \(y\). For round beams with \(\beta_x^* = \beta_y^*\) both planes swell alike and the overlap at \(s\) falls as \(1/(1+s^2/\beta^{*2})\). Weighting by where the collisions happen, as in section 4,

\[ R_{\text{hg}} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\sigma_z}\,\frac{e^{-s^2/\sigma_z^2}}{1 + s^2/\beta^{*2}}\,ds = \sqrt{\pi}\,t\,e^{t^2}\operatorname{erfc}(t), \qquad t = \frac{\beta^*}{\sigma_z} . \]

The closed form uses the standard integral \(\int e^{-u^2}/(u^2+t^2)\,du = (\pi/t)\,e^{t^2}\operatorname{erfc}(t)\); it agrees with direct numerical integration to the last digit. Values: \(t = 0.25\), 0.34; \(t = 0.5\), 0.55; \(t = 1\), 0.76; \(t = 2\), 0.91; \(t = 5\), 0.98. Hence the rule that \(\beta^*\) should not be much smaller than \(\sigma_z\). For flat beams in which only \(\beta_y^*\) is small, the denominator is \(\sqrt{1+s^2/\beta_y^{*2}}\) and the loss is milder.

Figure 23.2. The two factors by which a real collision falls short of section 3’s head-on formula. Left: the crossing-angle factor \(S = 1/\sqrt{1 + \phi_{\text{P}}^2}\), with the chapter’s three LHC cases: the design (\(\phi_{\text{P}} = 0.65\), \(S = 0.84\)), 2018 (1.37, 0.59) and the HL-LHC without crab cavities (3.14, 0.30). Right: the hourglass factor \(R_{\text{hg}} = \sqrt\pi\,t\,e^{t^2}\operatorname{erfc}(t)\) against \(t = \beta^*/\sigma_z\) on a logarithmic scale, with its two limits: \(\sqrt\pi\,t\) for long bunches (dashed) and \(1 - 1/2t^2\) for short ones (dotted). The LHC design, \(t = 7.3\), loses less than 1 per cent; round beams with SuperKEKB’s \(\beta^* = 0.9\) mm and 6 mm bunches, \(t = 0.15\), would lose 77 per cent.

Worked numbers. LHC design: \(t = 0.55/0.0755 = 7.3\), \(R_{\text{hg}} = 0.991\). In the SppS, Hatton records that by 1990 \(\beta^*\) had come down from 7 m by 3.5 m (horizontal by vertical) to 0.6 m by 0.15 m, which he calls the limit, since the bunch length had become comparable with the length over which the betatron function is near its minimum. SuperKEKB runs \(\beta_y^* = 1\) mm and lately 0.9 mm with bunches 6 mm long: head on, the flat-beam factor would be 0.39 at 0.9 mm (0.42 at 1 mm). Section 8 shows how it escapes.

6. The low-beta insertion

The waist. Here \(x\) is a particle’s horizontal distance from the reference orbit (m), \(x' = dx/ds\) its slope (rad), and \(\langle\ \rangle\) an average over the particles of the beam. In a field-free drift a particle moves in a straight line, \(x(s) = x_0 + x_0's\), where \(x_0\) and \(x_0'\) are its position and slope at the collision point, \(s = 0\). Squaring, \(x^2 = x_0^2 + 2x_0x_0's + x_0'^2s^2\), and averaging over the beam,

\[ \langle x^2\rangle(s) = \langle x_0^2\rangle + 2\langle x_0x_0'\rangle s + \langle x_0'^2\rangle s^2 . \]

A waist is the place where the beam is narrowest, like the focus of a lens: there the position-angle correlation vanishes, \(\langle x_0x_0'\rangle = 0\) (particles on the right are as likely to be heading left as right). Chapter 16 (section 9, from the Courant-Snyder theory of section 7) wrote the three averages of a matched beam with its rms emittance \(\varepsilon\) (m rad, the beam’s area in the \((x, x')\) plane divided by \(\pi\)) and the Twiss parameters: \(\langle x^2\rangle = \varepsilon\beta_x\), \(\langle xx'\rangle = -\varepsilon\alpha_x\) and \(\langle x'^2\rangle = \varepsilon\gamma_x\), with \(\gamma_x = (1+\alpha_x^2)/\beta_x\). At the waist, then, \(\alpha_x = 0\) and \(\gamma_x = 1/\beta^*\). In a drift the slopes do not change, so \(\langle x'^2\rangle = \varepsilon/\beta^*\) all along it. Put these into the line above and divide by \(\varepsilon\); for \(\alpha_x\), do the same with \(xx' = x_0x_0' + x_0'^2s\), whose average is \(\varepsilon s/\beta^*\):

\[ \beta_x(s) = \beta^* + \frac{s^2}{\beta^*}, \qquad \alpha_x(s) = -\frac{s}{\beta^*} . \]

The physical picture: a smaller spot is made by bringing the rays in at steeper angles, rms \(\sqrt{\varepsilon/\beta^*}\), so the beam swells faster on either side. \(\beta^*\) is the distance over which the area of the spot doubles: at \(s = \beta^*\), \(\beta_x = 2\beta^*\), so \(\sigma_x^2\) (and, for round beams, \(\sigma_y^2\) too) has doubled. Pre-requisite (continued): Gauss’s law and potentials of simple shapes

Pre-requisite. This continues Gauss’s law and potentials of simple shapes in chapter 1, with its later continuations, which gave Gauss’s law, the field \(\lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0r)\) of a line charge and the field of a uniform round beam. It adds the field of a round beam whose charge is spread in a Gaussian: the charge inside a radius, the field that rises linearly near the axis and falls as \(1/r\) outside, where it peaks, and why a short slice of a bunch can be treated as a piece of an infinite line. The next section turns this field into a force on an oncoming particle, and section 7 into the beam-beam kick and tune shift. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, §2.1 and §2.1.3 (Gauss’s law and the line charge), and S. Y. Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 5, §II.1 (the beam-beam force for round Gaussian beams).

Reminder: Gauss’s law says the electric flux out of any closed surface is the charge inside divided by \(\varepsilon_0\); for a long line of charge \(\lambda\) (C/m), a coaxial cylinder of radius \(r\) gives \(\mathcal{E}_r\,2\pi r = \lambda/\varepsilon_0\).

The charge distribution. Take a slice of a bunch, of length \(\Delta s\) along the beam, carrying a line density \(\lambda\) (C/m). Across the beam the charge is a round Gaussian of rms width \(\sigma_\perp\) in \(x\) and in \(y\):

\[ \rho(r) = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\sigma_\perp^2}\,e^{-r^2/2\sigma_\perp^2} \quad [\text{C/m}^3], \qquad r^2 = x^2 + y^2 . \]

This is the product \(\lambda\,\rho_x(x)\,\rho_y(y)\) of two normalised profiles of the previous pre-requisite, and integrating over the plane, \(\int_0^\infty\rho\,2\pi r\,dr = \lambda\), as it should. (Note that \(\sigma_\perp\) is the width in each plane; the rms radius is \(\sqrt2\,\sigma_\perp\).)

Gauss’s law on a cylinder. By symmetry the field points radially and depends only on \(r\), provided the charge changes slowly along \(s\) (see below). Draw a cylinder of radius \(r\) and length \(\Delta s\) on the axis. The ends carry no flux; the side carries \(\mathcal{E}_r\,2\pi r\,\Delta s\). The charge inside is

\[ Q_{\text{in}} = \Delta s\int_0^r\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\sigma_\perp^2}\,e^{-r'^2/2\sigma_\perp^2}\,2\pi r'\,dr' = \lambda\,\Delta s\left(1 - e^{-r^2/2\sigma_\perp^2}\right) , \]

using the substitution \(w = r'^2/2\sigma_\perp^2\), \(dw = r'\,dr'/\sigma_\perp^2\), which turns the integral into \(\int_0^{r^2/2\sigma_\perp^2}e^{-w}dw\). Setting \(\mathcal{E}_r\,2\pi r\,\Delta s = Q_{\text{in}}/\varepsilon_0\),

\[ \mathcal{E}_r(r) = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\,r}\left(1 - e^{-r^2/2\sigma_\perp^2}\right) . \]

The fraction of the charge inside \(r\) is \(1 - e^{-r^2/2\sigma_\perp^2}\): 39.3 per cent inside \(\sigma_\perp\), 86.5 per cent inside \(2\sigma_\perp\), 98.9 per cent inside \(3\sigma_\perp\).

Its shape. Near the axis, \(1 - e^{-x} \approx x\) for small \(x\), so

\[ \mathcal{E}_r \approx \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\sigma_\perp^2}\,r \qquad (r \ll \sigma_\perp) , \]

linear in \(r\), as inside a uniform beam: a particle there feels a force proportional to its distance from the axis, the force of a lens. Far out, the exponential vanishes and \(\mathcal{E}_r \to \lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0r)\), the field of a line charge: from far away the beam’s shape does not matter. In between the field peaks. Write \(u = r/\sigma_\perp\); the shape is \(f(u) = (1 - e^{-u^2/2})/u\), and \(df/du = 0\) gives \(e^{-u^2/2}(1 + u^2) = 1\). This has no closed solution; solved numerically, \(u = 1.585\), where \(f = 0.451\) and 71.5 per cent of the charge is enclosed. The linear form is good only close in: at \(r = 0.5\sigma_\perp\) the true field is 0.940 of it, at \(r = \sigma_\perp\) only 0.787. This is why section 7 says only small-amplitude particles get the full tune shift.

Figure 23.4. The radial field of a round Gaussian beam against the distance from its axis, in units of \(\lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0\sigma_\perp)\), computed from \((1 - e^{-u^2/2})/u\) with \(u = r/\sigma_\perp\). Near the axis it follows the straight line \(u/2\), the field of a lens; far out it joins \(1/u\), the field of a line charge, since from outside the beam’s shape no longer matters. Why a slice is a line. Gauss’s law alone fixes the field only when symmetry does: here, when the field has no part along \(s\), which needs the charge to change little along \(s\) over distances of order \(\sigma_\perp\). An LHC bunch is 7.55 cm long and 16.6 µm wide, already a ratio of 4500. In the bunch’s own frame, where its charges are at rest and the field is purely electrostatic, it is \(\gamma\sigma_z = 563\) m long, \(3.4 \times 10^7\) times its width (Lorentz transformations). Each slice is then very nearly a piece of an infinite line. The next pre-requisite carries this rest-frame field to the laboratory.

At work: the field of an LHC bunch. At the centre of a design bunch the line density is \(\lambda = Ne/(\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_z) = 1.15 \times 10^{11} \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19}/(2.5066 \times 0.0755) = 9.74 \times 10^{-8}\) C/m. With \(\sigma_\perp = 16.6\) µm, the peak field, at \(r = 26.3\) µm, is

\[ \mathcal{E}_{\max} = \frac{9.74 \times 10^{-8}}{2\pi \times 8.854 \times 10^{-12} \times 1.66 \times 10^{-5}} \times 0.451 = 4.76 \times 10^{7}\ \text{V/m} , \]

48 MV/m, comparable with the accelerating field of the best superconducting cavities, accompanied (next section) by a magnetic field \(\mathcal{E}/c\) of 0.16 T. It lasts only as long as the bunch takes to pass.

Flat beams. Electron rings collide flat beams, \(\sigma_y \ll \sigma_x\). Then there is no circular symmetry, Gauss’s law on a cylinder no longer fixes the field, and Poisson’s equation must be solved; the result for a Gaussian is a single integral (Lee, chapter 5, §II.1, part A, eq. 5.49) that can be written with the complex error function, the Bassetti-Erskine formula. Near the axis its linear part gives the kicks \(2Nr_{\text{cl}}x/[\gamma\sigma_x(\sigma_x + \sigma_y)]\) and \(2Nr_{\text{cl}}y/[\gamma\sigma_y(\sigma_x + \sigma_y)]\), quoted here (Lee, eq. 5.50), and from them the flat-beam \(\xi_y\) of section 7. For \(\sigma_x = \sigma_y\) they reduce to the round-beam kick.

Pre-requisite (continued): Relativistic motion in electric and magnetic fields

Pre-requisite. This continues Relativistic motion in electric and magnetic fields in chapter 1, which gave the Lorentz force \(d\mathbf{p}/dt = q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})\) with \(\mathbf{p} = \gamma m\mathbf{v}\), the near cancellation of the electric and magnetic forces between charges moving together, and the small-angle kick \(\Delta p_\perp/p\); the transformation of fields between frames was taught in chapter 8. It adds the case a collider lives on: the field of a relativistic bunch in the laboratory, the force it exerts on a particle coming the other way, \(q\mathcal{E}_r(1 + \beta^2)\) instead of \(q\mathcal{E}_r(1 - \beta^2)\), the impulse delivered as the two pass at a relative speed of \(2c\), and that impulse written as an angle through the classical radius. Section 7 uses all of it. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 2, §24 (the Lorentz transformation of the field) and §38 (the field of a uniformly moving charge); Tong, Electromagnetism, §5.3.3 and §5.3.4; and S. Y. Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 5, §II.1.

Reminder: a particle of momentum \(p\) that receives a small transverse impulse \(\Delta p_\perp\) leaves at an angle \(\Delta p_\perp/p\) to its old direction.

The bunch’s field in the laboratory. In the bunch’s own frame its charges are at rest and there is only an electric field \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}'\), radial, given by the previous pre-requisite with the rest-frame line density \(\lambda'\). The laboratory sees the bunch moving at \(\beta c\) along \(s\). The fields across the direction of motion transform as (Landau and Lifshitz, §24)

\[ \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_\perp = \gamma\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}'_\perp - \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}'\right), \qquad \mathbf{B}_\perp = \gamma\left(\mathbf{B}'_\perp + \frac{\mathbf{v}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}'}{c^2}\right) , \]

with \(\mathbf{v}\) the bunch’s velocity in the laboratory. With \(\mathbf{B}' = 0\),

\[ \mathcal{E}_r = \gamma \mathcal{E}'_r, \qquad \mathbf{B} = \frac{\mathbf{v}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}{c^2}, \quad\text{so}\quad B_\theta = \frac{\beta \mathcal{E}_r}{c} . \]

The transverse sizes are the same in both frames, but the bunch is contracted along \(s\), so the laboratory line density is \(\lambda = \gamma\lambda'\). Then \(\mathcal{E}_r = \gamma \mathcal{E}'_r = \gamma\lambda'(\ldots)/(2\pi\varepsilon_0r) = \lambda(\ldots)/(2\pi\varepsilon_0r)\): the laboratory field is what Gauss’s law gives with the laboratory line density, as section 7 uses it. This is the flattened disc of field of Landau and Lifshitz’s §38, seen slice by slice. For a positive bunch moving along \(+s\), \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) points outwards and \(\mathbf{B}\) circles the beam in the sense of \(\hat{\mathbf{s}}\times\hat{\mathbf{r}} = \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}\).

The force on a particle moving with the bunch, and against it. Let a test particle of charge \(q\) move along \(s\) with velocity \(u\), positive if it travels with the bunch and negative if it comes the other way. The magnetic force is \(q\,u\,\hat{\mathbf{s}}\times B_\theta\hat{\boldsymbol\theta} = -q\,u\,B_\theta\,\hat{\mathbf{r}}\), since \(\hat{\mathbf{s}}\times\hat{\boldsymbol\theta} = -\hat{\mathbf{r}}\). So the radial force is

\[ F_r = q\left(\mathcal{E}_r - uB_\theta\right) = q\mathcal{E}_r\left(1 - \frac{u\,\beta}{c}\right) . \]

  • Moving with the bunch, \(u = +\beta c\): \(F_r = q\mathcal{E}_r(1 - \beta^2) = q\mathcal{E}_r/\gamma^2\). The electric push and the magnetic pull almost cancel, the result already met for a beam’s own space charge.
  • Moving against it, \(u = -\beta c\): \(F_r = q\mathcal{E}_r(1 + \beta^2) \approx 2q\mathcal{E}_r\). The magnetic force now points the same way as the electric one, and the two add.

At 7 TeV, \(1/\gamma^2 = 1.8 \times 10^{-8}\): a proton feels the opposing bunch \(1.1 \times 10^8\) times more strongly than it feels a bunch of the same charge travelling beside it. The physics that makes a beam’s own field harmless at high energy makes the other beam’s field as strong as it can be.

The impulse. Let the test particle sit at a fixed radius \(r\) and move along \(-s\) at speed \(c\), at \(s_p = -ct\), while the bunch moves along \(+s\), its centre at \(s_b = ct\). The particle meets the bunch slice at distance \(s_p - s_b = -2ct\) from the centre, so the line density it feels at time \(t\) is \(\lambda(-2ct)\). The time integral is

\[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lambda(-2ct)\,dt = \frac{1}{2c}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lambda(\xi)\,d\xi = \frac{Ne}{2c} , \]

with \(\xi = -2ct\). The bunch’s length and shape drop out: only its total charge \(Ne\) and the relative speed \(2c\) matter. Multiplying \(2q\mathcal{E}_r\) by this,

\[ \Delta p_r = \int F_r\,dt = 2q\,\frac{Ne}{2c}\,\frac{1 - e^{-r^2/2\sigma_\perp^2}}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\,r} . \]

For a singly charged particle, \(q = \pm e\), write \(e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) = r_{\text{cl}}\,mc^2\), which defines the classical radius \(r_{\text{cl}}\) of a particle of mass \(m\) (\(1.535 \times 10^{-18}\) m for the proton, \(2.818 \times 10^{-15}\) m for the electron). Then \(\Delta p_r = 2Nr_{\text{cl}}mc\,(1 - e^{-r^2/2\sigma_\perp^2})/r\), and dividing by \(p = \gamma mc\),

\[ \Delta r' = \mp\frac{2Nr_{\text{cl}}}{\gamma\,r}\left(1 - e^{-r^2/2\sigma_\perp^2}\right) , \]

inwards (upper sign) for opposite charges, outwards for like ones, the convention section 7 uses.

Approximations. The particle’s radius is taken as fixed during the passage. An LHC particle crosses a 7.55 cm bunch in about \(\sigma_z/2c = 0.13\) ns, moving inwards or outwards meanwhile by about \(30\ \text{µrad} \times 3.8\ \text{cm} = 1.1\) µm, small against \(\sigma_\perp = 16.6\) µm; the same condition, \(\sigma_z \ll \beta^*\), keeps the bunch’s own size constant through the crossing. Both beams are taken at \(\beta = 1\), the field of the opposing bunch as not changed by the crossing, and the longitudinal field, which is small, is ignored. The force is transverse, so the particle’s energy does not change.

At work. LHC design, \(N = 1.15 \times 10^{11}\), \(\gamma = 7460.5\), \(\sigma_\perp = 16.6\) µm. The prefactor \(2Nr_p/\gamma = 4.73 \times 10^{-11}\) m. The largest kick, at \(r = 1.585\sigma_\perp\), is \(4.73 \times 10^{-11} \times 0.451/1.66 \times 10^{-5} = 1.29\) µrad, against an rms angle in the beam at the collision point of \(\sqrt{\varepsilon/\beta^*} = 30.2\) µrad. Near the axis the kick has the size \(r/f\) with \(1/f = Nr_p/(\gamma\sigma_\perp^2)\): a lens of focal length \(f = 11.7\) m, defocusing for two proton beams (the kick points away from the axis) and focusing for opposite charges. It is weak, but it is met at every crossing, turn after turn.

7. The beam-beam force

The field of a bunch. A bunch moving at nearly \(c\) carries a field flattened into a thin disc: in the laboratory the transverse electric field is \(\gamma\) times its rest-frame value and the magnetic field is \(B_\theta = \beta \mathcal{E}_r/c\) (Landau and Lifshitz, volume 2, sections 24 and 38; Tong, Electromagnetism, sections 5.3.3 and 5.3.4). For a round Gaussian bunch of rms transverse size \(\sigma_\perp = \sigma_x = \sigma_y\) (m) with line density \(\lambda(s)\) (C/m), Gauss’s law applied to a slice gives

\[ \mathcal{E}_r(r,s) = \frac{\lambda(s)}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\,r}\left(1 - e^{-r^2/2\sigma_\perp^2}\right) . \]

This holds because in its own frame the bunch is \(\gamma\sigma_z\) long, far longer than it is wide, so each slice looks like a piece of a long line charge.

Two cases. A particle of charge \(q\) moving with the bunch at velocity \(\beta c\) feels \(q(\mathcal{E}_r - \beta cB_\theta) = q\mathcal{E}_r(1-\beta^2) = q\mathcal{E}_r/\gamma^2\): electric repulsion and magnetic attraction nearly cancel. This is why a beam’s own space charge fades at high energy (chapter 22). A particle moving the other way feels \(q\mathcal{E}_r(1+\beta^2) \approx 2q\mathcal{E}_r\): the two add.

The kick. The oncoming particle meets the bunch at relative speed \(2c\), so the time integral of the line density during the passage is \(\int\lambda\,dt = Ne/(2c)\). The transverse momentum change is

\[ \Delta p_r = 2q\,\frac{Ne}{2c}\,\frac{1 - e^{-r^2/2\sigma_\perp^2}}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\,r} . \]

Dividing by \(p = \gamma mc\) and writing \(r_{\text{cl}} = e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 mc^2)\) for a singly charged particle (\(r_p = 1.535 \times 10^{-18}\) m, \(r_e = 2.818 \times 10^{-15}\) m):

\[ \Delta r' = \mp\frac{2Nr_{\text{cl}}}{\gamma}\,\frac{1}{r}\left(1 - e^{-r^2/2\sigma_\perp^2}\right) , \]

towards the axis for opposite charges (\(e^+e^-\), \(p\bar{p}\)), away from it for like charges (\(pp\)). The kick is linear near the axis, largest at \(r = 1.585\sigma_\perp\), and falls as \(1/r\) far out. For the LHC design the largest kick is 1.3 µrad, against a beam divergence at the collision point of 30 µrad: a weak lens, but one met every turn.

The beam-beam parameter. Near the axis, \(1 - e^{-r^2/2\sigma_\perp^2} \approx r^2/2\sigma_\perp^2\) and the kick is that of a thin lens of focal length \(f\) with \(1/f = Nr_{\text{cl}}/(\gamma\sigma_\perp^2)\). (Use \(1 - e^{-x} \approx x\) for small \(x\), the first term of the Taylor series of F1, section 8; then the kick is \(\Delta r' = -r/f\), the bending of a thin lens of focal length \(f\), written here for opposite charges.)

What does such a lens do to the tune \(Q_x\), the number of betatron oscillations a particle makes in one turn (chapter 16, section 8)? Write \(\mu = 2\pi Q_x\), the phase advance per turn. At the collision point \(\alpha_x = 0\), so chapter 16’s one-turn matrix there is

\[ M = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\mu & \beta^*\sin\mu \\ -\sin\mu/\beta^* & \cos\mu \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \operatorname{tr}M = 2\cos\mu . \]

The other beam adds a thin lens there, the matrix with rows \((1, 0)\) and \((-1/f, 1)\) (chapter 8). The new one-turn matrix is the product \(M\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ -1/f & 1\end{pmatrix}\), whose diagonal elements are \(\cos\mu - (\beta^*/f)\sin\mu\) and \(\cos\mu\). So the trace is lowered by \((\beta^*/f)\sin\mu\). The trace of the new matrix is still \(2\cos\) of the new phase advance \(\mu + \Delta\mu\), so

\[ \cos(\mu + \Delta\mu) = \cos\mu - \frac{\beta^*}{2f}\sin\mu . \]

For a small change, \(\cos(\mu + \Delta\mu) \approx \cos\mu - \Delta\mu\sin\mu\) (F1, section 5: the derivative of \(\cos\) is \(-\sin\)). Comparing, \(\Delta\mu = \beta^*/(2f)\), and since \(\Delta Q_x = \Delta\mu/2\pi\), \(\Delta Q_x = \beta^*/(4\pi f)\). A focusing lens raises the tune; for like charges the lens defocuses and the tune falls by the same amount. The beam-beam parameter \(\xi\) is this shift’s size, quoted as a positive number. With \(\sigma_\perp^2 = \varepsilon\beta^*\) and \(\varepsilon_n = \beta\gamma\varepsilon \approx \gamma\varepsilon\) (for \(\beta \approx 1\)):

\[ \xi = \frac{Nr_{\text{cl}}\,\beta^*}{4\pi\gamma\sigma_\perp^{2}} = \frac{Nr_{\text{cl}}}{4\pi\varepsilon_n} \qquad \text{(round beams)} . \]

For flat beams, \(\xi_y = Nr_{\text{cl}}\beta_y^*/[2\pi\gamma\sigma_y(\sigma_x + \sigma_y)]\). Only small-amplitude particles get the full shift; a particle at many \(\sigma\) gets almost none. So the beam-beam force spreads the tunes over a range of about \(\xi\) per crossing, and the spread must fit between resonances, the tunes \(Q_x\) and \(Q_y\) at which a small field error kicks a particle in step with its own oscillation turn after turn so that its amplitude grows (chapter 18, section 8). Note that \(\xi\) does not depend on \(\beta^*\) for round beams: luminosity can be raised by focusing harder without strengthening the beam-beam force.

Worked numbers. LHC design: \(\xi = 1.15 \times 10^{11} \times 1.535 \times 10^{-18}/(4\pi \times 3.75 \times 10^{-6}) = 0.0037\); with the crossing-angle reduction 0.836 it is 0.0031, the value in the HL-LHC table. In 2018, \(N = 1.1 \times 10^{11}\) and \(\varepsilon_n = 2.0\) µm give 0.0067 before the crossing reduction (the PDG table quotes 0.0045 per crossing).

The limits. Hadron colliders have tolerated a total over all collision points of about 0.01 to 0.03: the Tevatron worked consistently at 0.025 to 0.030 for its two collision points, with the tunes squeezed between the fifth- and seventh-order resonances (Holmes and Shiltsev). The Princeton-Stanford ring found beam degradation above \(\Delta Q = 0.025\). Electron rings tolerate more, because radiation damping pulls particles back from the resonances that the beam-beam force excites: LEP reached 0.083 per crossing, KEKB up to 0.129. Above the limit the core grows, the tails fill and lifetime falls.

Luminosity at the beam-beam limit. For an electron ring, substitute \(N\) from the flat-beam \(\xi_y\) into one factor of \(N\) in the luminosity, and write the current of one beam \(I = f_0n_bNe\) (A). In steps: the flat-beam formula gives \(N = 2\pi\gamma\sigma_y(\sigma_x + \sigma_y)\xi_y/(r_e\beta_y^*)\), and \(f_0n_bN = I/e\), so \(\mathcal{L} = (I/e)\,N/(4\pi\sigma_x\sigma_y) = (I/e)\,\gamma(\sigma_x + \sigma_y)\xi_y/(2r_e\beta_y^*\sigma_x)\):

\[ \mathcal{L} = \frac{\gamma\,I\,\xi_y}{2e\,r_e\,\beta_y^*}\left(1 + \frac{\sigma_y}{\sigma_x}\right) . \]

At fixed \(\xi_y\), luminosity grows with current and with \(1/\beta_y^*\). Worked number: SuperKEKB in the PDG table (2022): positrons at 4 GeV, 1.393 A, \(\xi_y = 0.0398\), \(\beta_y^* = 1\) mm, gives \(4.8 \times 10^{34}\); electrons at 7 GeV, 1.118 A, \(\xi_y = 0.0278\), give \(4.7 \times 10^{34}\); the table’s luminosity is \(4.71 \times 10^{34}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\). The agreement is closer than the formula deserves, since it neglects hourglass and crossing-angle factors.

8. The nano-beam scheme

SuperKEKB crosses its beams at a large angle, a half-angle of 41.5 mrad, with horizontal sizes of about 17 µm. The Piwinski angle is then about 15 (with \(\sigma_z = 6\) mm, \(\phi_{\text{P}} = 6 \times 0.0415/0.017 = 14.6\)): each bunch passes through the other only over a length of about \(\sigma_x^*/(\theta_c/2) = 0.4\) mm, shorter than \(\beta_y^* = 0.9\) to 1 mm (the bottom of Figure 23.1, taken to an extreme). The hourglass condition then applies to this overlap length, not to the 6 mm bunch, so \(\beta_y^*\) can be far smaller than \(\sigma_z\). The large-Piwinski-angle collision, with “crab-waist” sextupoles added to suppress the resonances it excites (a sextupole, whose kick grows as the square of the offset, chapter 18, section 6, placed on each side of the collision point to tilt the vertical waist, so that particles at each horizontal position meet the other beam where their own beam is thinnest), was proposed by P. Raimondi in 2006, at the second SuperB workshop, and tested at the DAΦNE collider in Frascati from 2008.

Pre-requisite (continued): How particle detectors work

Pre-requisite. This continues How particle detectors work in chapter 1 and its continuation in chapter 5; chapter 8, section 11, described scintillators read by photomultipliers and silicon detectors. It adds the two instruments a collider’s luminosity and pile-up depend on. The first is the electromagnetic calorimeter, which measures the energy of an electron or photon by letting it make a shower: the radiation length, the critical energy, why the signal is proportional to the energy and its precision improves as \(1/\sqrt E\), and the two kinds met in this chapter, lead glass read by its Cherenkov light and silicon-tungsten sampling. The second is the vertex tracker, which traces charged tracks back to the point where they were made and so separates the collisions of one crossing. Section 9 uses the calorimeters to count Bhabha pairs, and section 10 and “At the bench” the vertices for pile-up; exercise 10 needs the vertex resolution. Taught from Chao and others, Handbook, §3.3.1 (radiation length, critical energy and multiple scattering) and §3.3.12 (properties of materials), and Livingston and Blewett, §14-3 (electron absorption and the radiation length) and §14-8 (electron showers, measured in radiation lengths and critical energies). Heitler’s shower model and the estimate of vertex resolution are the standard textbook ones; the books listed treat their ingredients, not the detectors themselves.

Reminder: a photomultiplier turns a flash of light into electrons at a photocathode and multiplies them at about ten dynodes, for gains of \(10^5\) to \(10^7\) (chapter 8, section 11); a silicon diode is a solid ionisation chamber, finely divided into strips or pixels.

Electrons and photons in heavy matter. A high-energy electron crossing matter is deflected by the nuclei and radiates (bremsstrahlung). The energy it loses this way is proportional to its energy, so on average its energy falls exponentially, \(E(x) = E_0e^{-x/X_0}\). The length \(X_0\) is the radiation length (Chao and others, §3.3.1; Livingston and Blewett, §14-3). It is short in heavy elements, since radiation grows with the nuclear charge as \(Z^2\). A high-energy photon in turn converts into an electron-positron pair, with a mean free path of \(\tfrac{9}{7}X_0\). From the Handbook’s table (§3.3.12), dividing \(X_0\) in g/cm² by the density:

material \(X_0\) (g/cm²) density (g/cm³) \(X_0\) (cm)
tungsten 6.76 19.3 0.350
lead 6.37 11.35 0.561
lead glass 7.87 6.22 1.27
silicon 21.82 2.33 9.37

A 300 µm silicon layer is only 0.3 per cent of a radiation length; a centimetre of tungsten is almost three.

The shower. The two processes feed each other. An electron radiates a photon, the photon makes a pair, each of them radiates again. A simple model (Heitler’s) captures the result: every radiation length, each particle becomes two, sharing its energy equally. After \(t\) radiation lengths there are \(2^t\) particles of energy \(E_0/2^t\). The doubling stops when the energy per particle falls to the critical energy \(E_c\), where an electron loses as much by ionisation in a radiation length as it has; below it particles stop by ionising instead of multiplying. The Handbook gives \(E_c \approx 800\ \text{MeV}/(Z + 1.2)\): 10.6 MeV for tungsten, 9.6 MeV for lead. So

\[ N_{\max} = \frac{E_0}{E_c}, \qquad t_{\max} = \frac{\ln(E_0/E_c)}{\ln 2} . \]

At work: a 45.6 GeV electron, the beam energy at the Z peak at LEP, in tungsten: \(N_{\max} = 45\,600/10.6 = 4300\) particles at a depth of \(t_{\max} = 12.1\,X_0 = 4.2\) cm, and about \(20X_0 = 7\) cm of tungsten holds nearly all of it. The depth grows only as the logarithm of the energy: a hundred times more energy adds \(\log_2 100 = 6.6\,X_0\), 2.3 cm of tungsten. Across the beam the shower is narrow: its width is set by the Molière radius, about 0.9 cm in tungsten, inside which about 90 per cent of the energy lies.

Why the signal measures energy. In the end all the energy goes into ionisation by the shower’s electrons and positrons. One way to define the critical energy (the Handbook’s second one, §3.3.1) is as the ionisation loss per radiation length of track: a charged particle loses about \(E_c\) to ionisation for every \(X_0\) it travels. So the total length of charged track is about \(E_0/(E_c/X_0) = (E_0/E_c)\,X_0\), 15 m for the 45.6 GeV electron in tungsten. It is proportional to \(E_0\), and so is any signal proportional to track length: the charge freed in silicon layers, or the light emitted in glass. The precision comes from counting. If the signal is made of \(n\) independent pieces, with \(n \propto E_0\), it fluctuates by \(\sqrt n\), so

\[ \frac{\sigma_E}{E} \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{E}} , \]

the Poisson rule of Counting statistics. A calorimeter becomes more precise as the energy rises, the opposite of a magnetic spectrometer, whose precision worsens as tracks straighten.

Lead glass. Glass with much lead oxide is dense and transparent. Shower electrons moving faster than light moves in the glass, \(\beta > 1/n\) with \(n\) the refractive index, emit Cherenkov light in a cone of half-angle \(\cos\theta_C = 1/(n\beta)\). For \(n \approx 1.65\) the threshold is \(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 1/n^2} = 1.26\), for an electron a kinetic energy of only 0.13 MeV, and the angle at \(\beta = 1\) is 52.7°. Almost the whole track length radiates, so the light, collected by photomultipliers on the back face, is proportional to the energy. AdA’s 150 kg lead-glass counter detected bremsstrahlung photons this way.

Silicon-tungsten. A sampling calorimeter alternates absorber, where the shower develops, with thin detectors that sample it. With tungsten plates one radiation length thick and silicon between them, each charged particle of the shower crosses roughly one silicon layer per radiation length of its path, so the number of crossings is about \(E_0/E_c\), 4300 for 45.6 GeV. Counting alone would then give \(1/\sqrt{4300} = 1.5\) per cent; tracks at angles, the spread of each crossing’s energy loss and leakage make the real figure worse. Tungsten’s short radiation length and small Molière radius make the device compact, and its silicon pads, a few millimetres across, locate a shower’s centre far more finely than its width. That is what section 9 needs: the acceptance edge at \(\theta_{\min}\) is defined by the shower position, and seen from 2.5 m, 10 µrad is 25 µm.

Trackers and vertices. A charged particle crossing several layers of silicon at radii \(r_1 < r_2 < \ldots\) leaves a point in each; a solenoid’s field bends the track, and its curvature gives the momentum. Extrapolating back to the beam line finds where the track began. Take a straight track measured at \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) with position errors \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\). The line through the two points meets \(r = 0\) at

\[ x_0 = \frac{r_2x_1 - r_1x_2}{r_2 - r_1}, \qquad \sigma_0^2 = \left(\frac{r_2\,\sigma_1}{r_2 - r_1}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{r_1\,\sigma_2}{r_2 - r_1}\right)^2 , \]

the errors of independent measurements adding in quadrature (Errors and their propagation). The lever arm matters: layers at 3 and 7 cm with 10 µm each give 19 µm, layers at 4 and 10 cm with 15 µm give 27 µm. So the first layer must be as close to the beam as the beam pipe allows. Material spoils this. A particle crossing a thickness \(x\) is scattered by a random angle of rms

\[ \theta_0 = \frac{13.6\ \text{MeV}}{\beta cp}\sqrt{\frac{x}{X_0}}\left[1 + 0.038\ln\frac{x}{X_0}\right] \]

(Chao and others, §3.3.1). With the beam pipe and the first layer together \(0.5\) per cent of a radiation length, a 1 GeV/\(c\) track is scattered by 0.77 mrad, which moves its extrapolated origin by \(0.77\ \text{mrad} \times 3\ \text{cm} = 23\) µm; at 10 GeV/\(c\), by only 2.3 µm. Many tracks from one collision fit together to a vertex located to tens of microns along the beam. That is enough for the B factories’ flight distances of 127 to 251 µm (exercise 10), and for separating the collisions of one crossing.

Pile-up in space. The collisions of one crossing are spread along the luminous region, rms \(\sigma_z/\sqrt2\), with a Gaussian density of vertices. For the LHC design, \(\mu_{\text{pu}} = 27\) over 5.34 cm gives \(27/(\sqrt{2\pi} \times 5.34\ \text{cm}) = 2.0\) vertices per cm at the centre, one every 5 mm, a hundred times the vertex resolution. In 2018, 59 collisions over \(8/\sqrt2 = 5.66\) cm gave one every 2.4 mm; at 140 in a region of the same length, one every millimetre. The HL-LHC experiments therefore add precise timing of tracks, which separates vertices that coincide in position but not in time, since the luminous region also has a spread in time.

9. Measuring luminosity

The luminosity must be known absolutely, since a cross-section is a rate divided by it.

From the beams. Measure \(N_1\), \(N_2\) (current transformers, chapter 8) and the overlap. The overlap is the hard part.

The van der Meer scan. Simon van der Meer’s three-page ISR note of 1968 measures the overlap with the colliding beams themselves. Move one beam vertically by \(\Delta y\) and record the rate \(R(\Delta y)\). For any profiles,

\[ R(\Delta y) \propto \int g_1(y)\,g_2(y - \Delta y)\,dy, \qquad \int R(\Delta y)\,d\Delta y \propto \int g_1\,dy\int g_2\,dy = 1 , \]

so

\[ h_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\int R(\Delta y)\,d\Delta y}{R(0)} . \]

No calibration of the detector is needed, only of the displacement. For bunched beams the same argument, done in both planes, gives the convolved sizes \(\Sigma_{x,y} = \int R\,d\Delta/(\sqrt{2\pi}\,R_{\max})\), and with the bunch populations the absolute luminosity; dividing the peak rate of any monitor by it calibrates that monitor’s visible cross-section \(\sigma_{\text{vis}}\). The method assumes the profiles factorise into \(x\) and \(y\) parts, that the beams do not deflect each other during the scan, and that the displacement scale is right; modern analyses correct each. Figure 23.6 shows a scan with the numbers of “At the bench”.

Figure 23.6. A van der Meer scan as ATLAS ran it in Run 2, computed for two beams of 100 µm rms: the rate, relative to its peak, against the separation of the beams, \(R/R_0 = e^{-d^2/2\Sigma^2}\) with \(\Sigma = \sqrt2 \times 100 = 141\) µm. The dots are the 25 steps of half a beam size. The dashed rectangle has the peak’s height and a width \(\sqrt{2\pi}\,\Sigma = 354\) µm, the same area as under the curve: the area divided by the peak gives \(\Sigma\), whatever the detector’s efficiency, since that efficiency multiplies both alike.

Small-angle Bhabha scattering. At electron-positron colliders the reference is a process whose cross-section QED predicts: \(e^+e^- \to e^+e^-\) at small angles, dominated by photon exchange. For a small scattering angle \(\theta\) (rad), with \(\alpha \approx 1/137\) the fine-structure constant and \(\hbar c = 0.1973\) GeV fm, the leading term of the QED result is \(d\sigma/d\Omega \approx 16\alpha^2(\hbar c)^2/(s\,\theta^4)\) (quoted; it is the small-angle limit of the exchange term \((\alpha^2(\hbar c)^2/2s)(1+\cos^4(\theta/2))/\sin^4(\theta/2)\)), and integrating over the solid angle between \(\theta_{\min}\) and \(\theta_{\max}\). A thin ring of angles from \(\theta\) to \(\theta + d\theta\) round the beam has solid angle \(d\Omega = 2\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta \approx 2\pi\theta\,d\theta\), so \(\sigma = (32\pi\alpha^2(\hbar c)^2/s)\int\theta^{-3}\,d\theta\), and \(\int_{\theta_{\min}}^{\theta_{\max}}\theta^{-3}\,d\theta = \tfrac12(1/\theta_{\min}^2 - 1/\theta_{\max}^2)\):

\[ \sigma \approx \frac{16\pi\alpha^2(\hbar c)^2}{s}\left(\frac{1}{\theta_{\min}^2} - \frac{1}{\theta_{\max}^2}\right) . \]

For OPAL’s silicon-tungsten luminometer, 25 to 58 mrad, at \(\sqrt{s} = 91.2\) GeV this gives 163 nb before acceptance cuts, about 4 counts per second at the LEP Z-peak luminosity of \(2.4 \times 10^{31}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\). The steep \(1/\theta_{\min}^2\) is the difficulty: a 10 µrad error at 25 mrad shifts the cross-section by \(10^{-3}\). OPAL located its acceptance edges to a few microns and reached a total experimental systematic error of \(3.4 \times 10^{-4}\), below the \(5.4 \times 10^{-4}\) then assigned to the QED calculation.

Pre-requisite (continued): Ordinary differential equations (linear, constant and varying coefficients)

Pre-requisite. This continues Ordinary differential equations in chapter 2 and its continuations, which solved first-order equations by separating the variables and taught exponential decay, \(dN/dt = -N/\tau\). It adds a decay whose rate goes as the square of what is left, \(dN/dt = -kN^2\): its solution \(N_0/(1 + t/\tau)\), how it differs from exponential decay, the integral of a quantity proportional to \(N^2\) over a time, and, in an exercise, the same decay with an ordinary exponential loss added. Section 10 uses it for burn-off, the luminosity during a fill and the luminosity delivered by one. Taught from Chao and others, Handbook, §3.3.2 (beam and luminosity lifetime: a loss rate set by the luminosity) and §4.1 (luminosity).

Reminder: an equation \(dN/dt = g(N)h(t)\) is solved by collecting each variable on its own side, \(dN/g(N) = h(t)\,dt\), and integrating both sides.

Why a square. Every inelastic collision takes one particle from each beam, and the collision rate is \(\sigma_{\text{r}}\mathcal{L}\). For equal beams of \(N\) particles whose sizes do not change, \(\mathcal{L} \propto N^2\). The loss rate is then proportional to \(N^2\), not to \(N\): each particle’s chance of being lost per second depends on how many are in the other beam, and that number falls too.

The solution. Write \(dN/dt = -kN^2\) with \(k > 0\) a constant (in s\(^{-1}\) per particle). Separate:

\[ \frac{dN}{N^2} = -k\,dt \quad\Rightarrow\quad \int_{N_0}^{N}\frac{dN'}{N'^2} = -k\int_0^t dt' \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{1}{N} - \frac{1}{N_0} = kt . \]

So

\[ N(t) = \frac{N_0}{1 + t/\tau}, \qquad \tau = \frac{1}{kN_0} . \]

Check by differentiating: \(dN/dt = -(N_0/\tau)/(1 + t/\tau)^2 = -kN_0^2/(1 + t/\tau)^2 = -kN^2\). A numerical solution of the equation agrees with this to eleven digits.

How it differs from exponential decay. At the start the slope is \(-N_0/\tau\), the same as for \(N_0e^{-t/\tau}\). But the rate falls as \(N^2\), so the decay slows: after a time \(\tau\), half is left, against \(e^{-1} = 0.37\) for the exponential; after \(3\tau\) a quarter, against 0.05. The tail falls only as \(1/t\). Note that \(\tau\) is here the time to halve, not to fall by \(e\).

Quantities that go as \(N^2\). The luminosity is

\[ \mathcal{L}(t) = \frac{\mathcal{L}_0}{(1 + t/\tau)^2} , \]

with an initial relative decay rate \(-(1/\mathcal{L})\,d\mathcal{L}/dt = 2/\tau\), so its decay time at the start is \(\tau/2\). The integral over a fill of length \(T\) uses \(\int dt/(1 + t/\tau)^2 = -\tau/(1 + t/\tau)\):

\[ \int_0^T\mathcal{L}\,dt = \mathcal{L}_0\,\tau\left[1 - \frac{1}{1 + T/\tau}\right] = \mathcal{L}_0\,\frac{\tau T}{\tau + T} . \]

For \(T \ll \tau\) this is \(\mathcal{L}_0T\), as if the luminosity were constant; for \(T \gg \tau\) it tends to \(\mathcal{L}_0\tau\), the most one fill can give.

At work. For the LHC in 2018, section 10 finds \(\tau = 23.2\) h. After 7.6 h, \(N = 0.754N_0\) and \(\mathcal{L} = 0.568\mathcal{L}_0\), where an exponential with the same initial slope would have left \(0.721N_0\). With \(\mathcal{L}_0 = 2.1 \times 10^{34}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\) the fill delivers \(2.1 \times 10^{34} \times (23.2 \times 7.6/30.8) \times 3600\ \text{s} = 0.433\) fb\(^{-1}\), against 0.575 fb\(^{-1}\) had the luminosity held at its peak.

10. Lifetime, burn-off, levelling and pile-up

Burn-off. Each inelastic collision removes two particles, one from each beam. With \(n_{\text{IP}}\) collision points, a total luminosity \(\mathcal{L}\) at each, and a removal cross-section \(\sigma_{\text{r}}\), the total number of particles in one beam, \(N_{\text{tot}} = n_bN\), falls as

\[ \frac{dN_{\text{tot}}}{dt} = -\sigma_{\text{r}}\,n_{\text{IP}}\,\mathcal{L} = -k_{\text{b}}N_{\text{tot}}^2 , \]

since \(\mathcal{L} \propto N^2\) at fixed beam sizes, with \(k_{\text{b}}\) a constant. The solution is \(N_{\text{tot}}(t) = N_{\text{tot},0}/(1 + t/\tau_N)\), with \(\tau_N = 1/(k_{\text{b}}N_{\text{tot},0})\); written out, and with the luminosity and its integral over a fill of length \(t_{\text{f}}\),

\[ \tau_N = \frac{n_bN_0}{\sigma_{\text{r}}\,n_{\text{IP}}\,\mathcal{L}_0}, \qquad \mathcal{L}(t) = \frac{\mathcal{L}_0}{(1 + t/\tau_N)^2}, \qquad \int_0^{t_{\text{f}}}\mathcal{L}\,dt = \mathcal{L}_0\,\frac{\tau_Nt_{\text{f}}}{\tau_N + t_{\text{f}}} . \]

The initial luminosity decay time, \(-\mathcal{L}/(d\mathcal{L}/dt)\) at \(t = 0\), is \(\tau_N/2\).

Worked number. LHC 2018: \(n_b = 2556\), \(N_0 = 1.1 \times 10^{11}\), \(\mathcal{L}_0 = 2.1 \times 10^{34}\) at ATLAS and CMS, \(\sigma_{\text{r}} = 80\) mb: \(\tau_N = 23\) h, a decay time of 11.6 h from burn-off alone. The PDG table gives 8.4 h measured: emittance growth and other losses add the rest. In the Tevatron, with only 36 bunches of \(9 \times 10^{10}\) antiprotons, the same formula with 0.07 b and \(4.31 \times 10^{32}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\) at two points burns 6.7 per cent of the antiprotons per hour; the precious antiprotons (chapter 24) were consumed by the physics itself. Holmes and Shiltsev describe Tevatron stores with luminosity lifetimes of 5.2 to 5.7 h, set mainly by intrabeam scattering (small-angle collisions between particles of the same bunch, which slowly heat it and enlarge its emittance; chapter 19), and the store integral \(\mathcal{L}_0\tau_{\mathcal{L}}\ln(1 + t_{\text{f}}/\tau_{\mathcal{L}})\) for \(\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_0/(1 + t/\tau_{\mathcal{L}})\).

The optimum fill. If refilling takes \(t_{\text{ta}}\), the average luminosity over a cycle is \(\mathcal{L}_0\tau_Nt_{\text{f}}/[(\tau_N + t_{\text{f}})(t_{\text{f}} + t_{\text{ta}})]\). Setting the derivative of its logarithm with respect to \(t_{\text{f}}\) to zero, \(1/t_{\text{f}} - 1/(\tau_N + t_{\text{f}}) - 1/(t_{\text{f}} + t_{\text{ta}}) = 0\), which reduces to \(t_{\text{f}}^2 = \tau_Nt_{\text{ta}}\) (the first two terms combine to \(\tau_N/[t_{\text{f}}(\tau_N + t_{\text{f}})]\); setting this equal to \(1/(t_{\text{f}} + t_{\text{ta}})\) and cross-multiplying, \(\tau_Nt_{\text{f}} + \tau_Nt_{\text{ta}} = \tau_Nt_{\text{f}} + t_{\text{f}}^2\)), gives \(t_{\text{opt}} = \sqrt{\tau_Nt_{\text{ta}}}\). (The logarithm turns the product and quotient into a sum and difference, \(\ln t_{\text{f}} - \ln(\tau_N + t_{\text{f}}) - \ln(t_{\text{f}} + t_{\text{ta}})\) plus constants, and the derivative of \(\ln x\) is \(1/x\), F1, section 4; the maximum of a function is the maximum of its logarithm.) For \(\tau_N = 23\) h and a turnaround of 2.5 h, \(t_{\text{opt}} = 7.6\) h, with an average of 57 per cent of the peak. A long turnaround or a long lifetime lengthens the fill; machine faults, which end many fills early, shorten the average.

Pile-up. The mean number of inelastic collisions per bunch crossing is

\[ \mu_{\text{pu}} = \frac{\mathcal{L}\,\sigma_{\text{inel}}}{n_bf_0} , \]

with \(\sigma_{\text{inel}}\) the inelastic cross-section (the experiments write \(\mu\); the subscript keeps it apart from the phase advance per cell). LHC design: \(10^{34} \times 85\) mb\(/(2808 \times 11\,245) = 27\). In 2018, \(2.1 \times 10^{34}\) with 80 mb and 2544 colliding pairs gives 59. The detectors must separate these overlapping collisions, mostly by their vertices along the luminous region; the HL-LHC designers chose to cap the luminosity at \(5 \times 10^{34}\), 138 per crossing, at the experiments’ request.

Levelling. Rather than start a fill at the highest luminosity and let it decay, hold it constant at a level the detectors accept, and spend the excess as the beams burn off. The LHC levels by three means: a transverse offset between the beams (first asked for by ALICE), the crossing angle, and \(\beta^*\) (Brüning and Zerlauth). Worked number: the HL-LHC design has \(n_b = 2748\), \(N_0 = 2.2 \times 10^{11}\), a virtual luminosity with crab cavities of \(19.54 \times 10^{34}\) (the luminosity the beams would give if fully focused and head on, unlevelled) and a levelled \(5 \times 10^{34}\) at two points, with 100 mb assumed for burn-off. The virtual luminosity goes as \(N^2\), so it falls to the levelled value, and levelling ends, when \((N/N_0)^2 = 5/19.54\), that is when \(N\) has fallen to \(N_0\sqrt{5/19.54} = 0.51N_0\); while levelled, the beams lose particles at the constant rate \(\sigma_{\text{r}}\,2\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{lev}}\), so the time is \(n_b(N_0 - N_{\text{end}})/(\sigma_{\text{r}}\,2\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{lev}}) = 8.3\) h, the value in the HL-LHC table.

11. One ring or two

A particle and its antiparticle in the same magnetic field, moving in opposite directions, follow the same orbit: reversing both \(q\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) leaves \(q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\) unchanged. So AdA, ADONE, SPEAR, PETRA, LEP, the SppS and the Tevatron each used one ring and one set of magnets. With \(n_b\) bunches each way there are \(2n_b\) crossings per turn, and every one gives a beam-beam kick. An electric field separates the beams, because \(q\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) changes sign with the charge while the velocity does not enter. The SppS used electrostatic separators to keep protons and antiprotons on different orbits except near the experiments once it ran six bunches each way. The Tevatron’s 22 separators put the beams on helical orbits, so that of 72 encounters per turn only the two at CDF and D0 were head on (Holmes and Shiltsev).

Two beams of the same charge need two rings with opposite fields. The ISR used two separate rings interleaved; RHIC two rings with 12 common dipoles near the crossings; the B factories two rings even for \(e^+e^-\), to store thousands of bunches without parasitic crossings. The LHC put both rings in one magnet: each of its 1232 dipoles of 8.33 T has two apertures 194 mm apart, with opposite fields, in a common iron yoke and cryostat, because the LEP tunnel had little room (Védrine). The beams change from outer to inner channel at the four crossing points, so each beam travels the same length.

How it was researched and built

AdA. On 7 March 1960 Touschek gave a seminar at Frascati proposing electron-positron collisions and, as a first step, a very small ring fed by the laboratory’s 1.1 GeV electron synchrotron. AdA (Anello di Accumulazione) stored its first particles on 27 February 1961, according to Bernardini’s account; it was not clear at the time whether they were electrons or positrons. The ring was 4.1 m round, for 250 MeV per beam, with a 1.1 T weak-focusing magnet. Bremsstrahlung photons from the synchrotron’s beam converted on a tantalum target inside the ring to give electrons; then the whole ring was turned through 180° to fill positrons the other way. Below 1 nTorr, beams lived for up to 40 hours, but the currents were tiny, and in July 1962 AdA was moved to the 1 GeV linac at Orsay. There the team found that at about \(3 \times 10^7\) particles the lifetime fell from some 50 hours to a few: scattering within the bunch threw particles out of the RF bucket, the Touschek effect (chapter 19). To prove collisions they counted single-bremsstrahlung photons, \(e^+e^- \to e^+e^-\gamma\), in a 150 kg lead-glass counter. Beam-gas photons scale as \(N_1\), beam-beam photons as \(N_1N_2\); so the rate divided by \(N_1\), plotted against \(N_2\), is a line whose slope is the luminosity. The runs of December 1963 to April 1964 gave about \(10^{25}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\), reported on 16 July 1964.

VEP-1 and the Princeton-Stanford rings. G. I. Budker’s group began work on colliding electron beams late in 1956. VEP-1, two rings 2.7 m round stacked one above the other, for 160 MeV per beam (130 MeV in routine use), was built in Novosibirsk, assembled in Moscow and returned to Novosibirsk in 1962; the first electron-electron scattering was recorded on 19 May 1964. It reached about \(4 \times 10^{28}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\). The Princeton-Stanford group (Barber, Gittelman, O’Neill and Richter) began construction in December 1958 with Office of Naval Research funds: two rings of 11.8 m in a figure eight, for 500 MeV, fed by the 300 MeV Stanford Mark III linac. The first beam was stored on 28 March 1962; the first collisions were announced in March 1965, and the machine closed in 1968. Its troubles taught the field: a pressure rise of a factor of 300 from gas desorbed by synchrotron light, instabilities cured with octupoles, chromaticity correction and separated tunes (chapter 22), and the beam-beam limit.

ADONE and SPEAR. ADONE at Frascati, 105 m round and 1.5 GeV per beam, begun in 1963, came into operation at the end of 1968, began taking data in 1969 and showed hadrons produced more copiously than expected. At SLAC, Richter’s group proposed a 3 GeV-per-beam ring, in a preliminary proposal in 1964 and a formal one in 1965; funds came only in 1970 for a reduced version, SPEAR, built in 21 months from the start of construction and completed in 1972: one ring about 80 m across, one bunch of each kind, two collision points. On Sunday 10 November 1974 the SLAC-LBL group found a narrow resonance at 3.1 GeV, the \(\psi\); it was announced with Ting’s J from Brookhaven on 11 November.

The ISR. CERN’s Accelerator Research group turned to proton storage rings fed by the PS in 1960. MURA’s RF stacking had to be proved first, so CERN built CESAR, a 24 m electron ring at 2 MeV, ready in 1964, which demonstrated stacking. The ISR design report of May 1964 estimated 312 million Swiss francs, against 1556 million for a 300 GeV synchrotron; CERN Council approved the ISR in December 1965 with Kjell Johnsen as project leader. Excavation began in November 1966. The rings were 942.64 m round, 1.5 times the PS, with eight crossings at 14.77°. Average pressure had to stay below \(10^{-9}\) Torr, and below \(10^{-11}\) Torr at the crossings, over nearly 2 km of baked stainless-steel chamber (chapter 3). The first collisions came on 27 January 1971 at 15 GeV/\(c\), watched anxiously in case the beam-beam effect destroyed the beams; it did not. Collisions at 26.5 GeV/\(c\) followed in May. The project closed on 1 March 1971 within its budget of 332 million francs (1965 prices). In 1971 the ISR reached \(3 \times 10^{29}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\) against a design of \(4 \times 10^{30}\), with 95 per cent availability. By December 1982 it reached \(1.4 \times 10^{32}\), 35 times the design, a record for all colliders until CESR’s \(1.7 \times 10^{32}\) in 1991. Its beams of 30 to 40 A lived through physics runs of 40 to 60 hours.

The SPS collider and the W and Z. Approval for an antiproton source and the conversion of the SPS came in June 1978 (chapter 24). The first collisions, two proton bunches against one antiproton bunch at 270 GeV, came on 10 July 1981. The W was announced on 25 January 1983 and the Z on 1 June 1983. Rubbia and van der Meer received the Nobel Prize in 1984. Hatton’s history records the gains: 315 GeV from 1984 after more cooling water was pumped to the magnets, six bunches each way after 1987, separators against the extra crossings, and \(\beta^*\) reduced to 0.6 m by 0.15 m. Integrated luminosity per year rose from 28 nb\(^{-1}\) in the first year of physics to 7240 nb\(^{-1}\) in 1990; the peak reached \(6 \times 10^{30}\) against a design of \(10^{30}\).

PETRA, the Tevatron, LEP, HERA. DESY built PETRA, 2.3 km round, in open excavation from 1976 to 1978, more than a year ahead of schedule; first beam was stored in July 1978 and first collisions came in September. By spring 1979 it reached 27 GeV in the centre of mass, and in June 1979 TASSO showed three-jet events, the gluon’s signature. Fermilab’s Tevatron, the first high-energy accelerator built with superconducting magnets (chapter 17), saw its first proton-antiproton collisions on 13 October 1985, began its first collider run in 1987, found the top quark in 1995 and closed on 30 September 2011; it reached \(4.3 \times 10^{32}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\) at 1.96 TeV against an original goal of \(10^{30}\), and delivered more than 12 fb\(^{-1}\). LEP, 26.66 km round, had first beam on 14 July 1989 and first collisions on 13 August 1989, reached 209 GeV in the centre of mass in 2000, and closed on 2 November 2000. HERA at DESY, 6.336 km, collided 27.5 GeV electrons or positrons with 820 GeV protons (920 GeV from 1998) from 1992 to 30 June 2007.

RHIC and the B factories. RHIC at Brookhaven, 3.834 km, recorded its first gold-gold collisions on 12 June 2000 and its last collisions, of oxygen ions, on 6 February 2026; its tunnel and much of its complex pass to the Electron-Ion Collider. PEP-II (SLAC, 2.2 km, 1999 to 2008) reached \(1.2 \times 10^{34}\) against a design of \(3 \times 10^{33}\); KEKB (3.016 km, 1999 to 2010) reached \(2.11 \times 10^{34}\) with 1585 bunches. Their recipe was high current in many bunches, two rings and small \(\beta_y^*\). SuperKEKB, rebuilt in the KEKB tunnel with nano-beam optics, began physics in 2018 and set a record of \(5.1 \times 10^{34}\) in December 2024; in 2026 it reached \(5.28 \times 10^{34}\) with the Belle II detector on (30 May) and \(5.32 \times 10^{34}\) without it (29 June), with \(\beta_y^* = 0.9\) mm. Its design goal was set at \(8 \times 10^{35}\) (Chao and Tigner’s table of colliders) and is now quoted nearer \(6 \times 10^{35}\); sudden beam losses stand in the way, and a target above \(10^{35}\) for the 2027 run is under discussion (Okada).

The LHC. The first mention, in Richter’s account, is a 1983 LEP note by S. Myers and W. Schnell on a superconducting proton collider in the LEP tunnel. Construction began in 1998. The first beam circulated on 10 September 2008; on 19 September a badly soldered joint between two magnets failed, and the arc that followed damaged most of the magnets in one sector. Repairs took a little over a year. After low-energy collisions late in 2009, collisions at 3.5 TeV per beam began on 30 March 2010, and the Higgs boson was announced on 4 July 2012. Run 2 at 6.5 TeV (2015 to 2018) reached \(2.1 \times 10^{34}\), twice the design, and with Run 1 delivered about 160 fb\(^{-1}\); Run 3 from 2022 at 6.8 TeV levels at \(2 \times 10^{34}\), and 2024 alone gave ATLAS and CMS 122.2 fb\(^{-1}\). The HL-LHC is to collect 3000 fb\(^{-1}\) by the early 2040s.

How a collider is designed today. The luminosity formula, with \(S\), \(R_{\text{hg}}\) and \(\xi\), sets the parameter table; the experiments fix the acceptable pile-up and luminous-region length. Optics are designed in MAD-X, the successor of CERN’s MAD program (chapter 16): the arcs, the dispersion suppressors and the matched low-beta insertion with zero dispersion at the collision point. Standard choices follow from this chapter: \(\beta^*\) near \(\sigma_z\) for round beams; a crossing angle that keeps the first parasitic encounters about 10 \(\sigma\) apart (9.4 \(\sigma\) in the LHC design, 12.5 \(\sigma\) for the HL-LHC); crab cavities when the Piwinski angle is large; flat beams and very large Piwinski angles for lepton factories. Beam-beam simulations then set the tunes and estimate lifetimes, and the final quadrupoles, their radiation shielding and machine protection are designed around the result.

At the bench

The luminosity monitor. In the control room of a modern collider, luminosity is a live number per collision point, and often per bunch. At the LHC, ATLAS’s LUCID luminometer has 16 photomultiplier tubes in each forward arm, read out bunch by bunch, integrated over each “luminosity block” of data and over 1 to 2 s for fast feedback. Displays show Hz/µb: 20 000 Hz/µb is \(2 \times 10^{34}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\), when the inelastic collision rate at one experiment is about \(1.6 \times 10^9\) per second. At LEP the monitor counted small-angle Bhabha pairs in calorimeters on both sides of the collision point at a few per second on the Z peak, so a luminosity to 0.1 per cent took about \(10^6\) events. At VEP-1, scintillation counters recorded up to 30 scattered electron pairs per second, enough to tune the machine by scanning the relative beam position.

A van der Meer scan. At ATLAS in Run 2 the procedure was as follows, once a year, in a fill dedicated to it. 1. Inject 44 to 140 isolated bunches, about \(0.8 \times 10^{11}\) protons each, with enlarged emittance of 3 to 4 µm, so that there are no parasitic encounters and the crossing angle can be zero. 2. Set special optics with \(\beta^* = 19.2\) m, making the beams about 100 µm across at the collision point and the convolved size \(\Sigma \approx 141\) µm. 3. Optimise head-on collisions in both planes. 4. Move the two beams in opposite directions in 25 steps of 0.5 \(\sigma\), from \(-6\sigma\) to \(+6\sigma\) of separation, recording the rate per bunch pair at each step; about 20 minutes per plane. At the peak there are about 0.5 interactions per crossing; at \(6\sigma\) the rate is \(10^{-4}\) of the peak. 5. Repeat in the other plane, then repeat the pair several times, with off-axis and diagonal scans to test whether the profiles factorise, and length-scale scans that move both beams together to calibrate the steering magnets with the tracking detector. 6. Fit each curve, compute \(\Sigma_x\), \(\Sigma_y\) and \(\sigma_{\text{vis}}\) for every bunch pair, then correct for orbit drifts, beam-beam deflection, non-factorisation and magnet hysteresis.

A vdM fill lasts up to a day, alternating ATLAS and CMS. The result carried to the Run 2 physics data gave 140.1 fb\(^{-1}\) with an uncertainty of 0.83 per cent. At the ISR the scans ran semi-automatically under the LUMS program, with corrections for the coherent tune shift and for hysteresis in the steering magnets.

Collision optimisation scans. When beams are first brought into collision, the orbits at the collision point are never exactly right: a few microns of offset at 9 µm beam size costs luminosity. Operators scan each beam’s position in small steps in both planes and set it at the peak of the rate. At CMS since 2015 short “emittance scans” do this with physics beams at the start and end of fills: 7 separation steps, 10 s each, out to 3.5 nominal \(\sigma\) in the separation plane and 2.5 \(\sigma\) in the crossing plane, a pair of planes in under 4 minutes. Fitted bunch by bunch, they give the emittance of each bunch and its orbit offset.

Beam lifetime under collisions. When the beams are brought into collision, the loss rate jumps. In a modern hadron collider, burn-off is a large part of it; in a lepton ring, particles lost through scattering on the other beam at the collision point (radiative Bhabha scattering) add to it. The Tevatron at the end of its life lost 1.5 to 2.0 per cent of its protons and 1.0 to 1.5 per cent of its antiprotons per hour early in a store; earlier in Run II beam-beam losses had cost 30 to 40 per cent of the integrated luminosity, and bunches at the ends of trains, with tunes nearest a resonance, lost intensity fastest. In the SppS, once the vacuum had been improved, proton lifetimes of several hundred hours were recorded, and reducing the noise in the RF system brought the lifetime of the beams in a coast to better than 50 hours (Hatton).

The fill pattern. The LHC’s 400.79 MHz RF has 35 640 buckets round the ring; at 25 ns spacing, one bunch per tenth bucket, there are 3564 slots. Bunches come in trains of 96 or 144 from the injectors, separated by gaps for the injection kickers’ rise times, with one gap, no longer than 3 µs, left empty for the rise of the dump kicker. The nominal design injects 288 bunches per transfer from the SPS. Because ALICE and LHCb are not symmetric round the ring, some bunches collide in only some experiments: in Run 2, 2556 bunches per beam gave 2544 pairs colliding at ATLAS and CMS. On each side of a collision point a bunch meets up to 45 bunches of the other beam before the beams separate, at 8 to 10 \(\sigma\) near the collision point (Hostettler and others); bunches at the ends of trains miss some of these and so have slightly different orbits, tunes and chromaticity, the “PACMAN” bunches.

A long fill, hour by hour (LHC, Run 3). Turnaround from one fill’s end to the next fill’s collisions is typically 150 to 180 minutes: magnet cycle, injection of a pilot, then trains, the ramp to 6.8 TeV, the squeeze of \(\beta^*\), and bringing the beams into collision. - Hour 0: the beams collide at \(\beta^* = 1.2\) m. The experiments declare stable beams; luminosity is levelled at \(2 \times 10^{34}\) with about 60 collisions per crossing. - The next several hours: as burn-off reduces the bunch populations, \(\beta^*\) is stepped down towards 0.3 m and the crossing angle from 320 to 270 µrad (the PDG table); the luminosity display stays flat. Emittance scans at the start check the beam sizes. - After levelling ends: luminosity decays as the beams burn off and their emittances grow. For comparison, in Run 2, without \(\beta^*\) levelling, the initial decay time was 8.4 h (PDG). The loss monitors and lifetime displays show a slow decline unless a bunch or a train misbehaves. - The end: the operators dump when the luminosity has fallen to the point where refilling pays (section 10), or a fault dumps the beam first. A year’s integral then depends as much on availability as on peak luminosity: 2024’s 122.2 fb\(^{-1}\) came with few major faults (Brüning and Zerlauth).

Ways it fails, and their signatures. - Beams not head on: luminosity below expectation, recovered by an optimisation scan; a slow drift over hours shows as falling specific luminosity (luminosity per bunch-population product). - Emittance blow-up (intrabeam scattering, noise): specific luminosity falls steadily; emittance scans and synchrotron-light monitors confirm it. - Beam-beam limit: lifetime drops when collisions start; tails grow, background in the detectors rises; the losses concentrate on particular bunches, often train ends. - Vacuum trouble: background and losses at one region. The ISR fought runaway pressure bumps from ions desorbing gas from the walls; in 2023 the LHC’s proton run ended early because of a vacuum problem in the triplet at Point 8. - RF or cryogenic faults: the beam is dumped; in 2022 superconducting RF problems cost a long interruption at the LHC. - Sudden beam loss: at SuperKEKB, rapid large losses of stored beam, with no single cause found, which limit the current that can be stored; cleaning a vacuum sealant from the positron ring’s beam pipe during the 2025 shutdown reduced them there (Okada). - Radiation damage: not a signature on a screen but a deadline. The LHC triplets were designed for about 300 fb\(^{-1}\); above that, the insulation receives more than 10 MGy and becomes brittle, which is one reason for their replacement.

Where it stops

For hadron colliders the energy is set by the magnets and the tunnel: \(B\rho = 3.3356\,p\) gives 23 349 T m at 7 TeV, so the LHC’s 8.33 T dipoles need a bending radius of 2803 m (chapter 17). Luminosity is held back by the beam-beam limit of about 0.01 per crossing, by pile-up that detectors can untangle (about 60 now, 140 to 200 planned), by the radiation dose to the final quadrupoles, and by the intensity limits of chapter 22: electron clouds, impedance and machine protection with hundreds of megajoules stored. Antiproton colliders were also limited by how fast antiprotons could be made and cooled (chapter 24).

For electron rings the limit is radiation. The energy lost per turn grows as \(E^4/\rho\) (chapter 20); LEP stopped at 104.5 GeV per beam with all its superconducting RF, and a ring for much higher electron energies must be much larger. The way past is not to bend: linear colliders, which accelerate each bunch once and collide it once, with spots of nanometres to make up for the single pass (chapter 25).

Exercises

1. The ISR reached 31.4 GeV/\(c\) per beam. Find \(\sqrt{s}\) and the energy of a proton beam on a hydrogen target that would give the same.

Answer

\(E = \sqrt{31.4^2 + 0.938^2} = 31.414\) GeV, so \(\sqrt{s} = 2E = 62.8\) GeV. Then \(s = 3947.4\) GeV\(^2\) and \(E_{\text{FT}} = (s - 2m_p^2c^4)/(2m_pc^2) = (3947.4 - 1.76)/1.877 = 2103\) GeV, about 2.1 TeV.

2. Convert \(2 \times 10^{34}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\) to m\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\) and to Hz/µb. How much integrated luminosity does it give in \(10^7\) s of collisions, and how many gluon-fusion Higgs bosons (48.58 pb)?

Answer

\(2 \times 10^{38}\) m\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\); since 1 µb \(= 10^{-30}\) cm\(^2\), it is 20 000 Hz/µb. In \(10^7\) s: \(2 \times 10^{41}\) cm\(^{-2}\) \(= 200\) fb\(^{-1}\) \(= 2 \times 10^5\) pb\(^{-1}\), times 48.58 pb gives \(9.7 \times 10^6\) Higgs bosons.

3. Using the LHC design numbers (\(N = 1.15 \times 10^{11}\), \(n_b = 2808\), \(f_0 = 11\,245\) Hz, 7 TeV, \(\varepsilon_n = 3.75\) µm, \(\beta^* = 0.55\) m, \(\sigma_z = 7.55\) cm, 285 µrad), compute \(\sigma^*\), the head-on luminosity, the Piwinski angle, the reduction factor and the luminosity.

Answer

\(\gamma = 7460.5\), \(\varepsilon = 5.03 \times 10^{-10}\) m, \(\sigma^* = \sqrt{5.03 \times 10^{-10} \times 0.55} = 16.6\) µm. Head on, \(\mathcal{L}_0 = 11\,245 \times 2808 \times (1.15 \times 10^{11})^2/(4\pi(1.66 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{cm})^2) = 1.20 \times 10^{34}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\). \(\phi_{\text{P}} = 0.0755 \times 1.425 \times 10^{-4}/1.66 \times 10^{-5} = 0.647\), \(S = 1/\sqrt{1.419} = 0.840\), \(\mathcal{L} = 1.01 \times 10^{34}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\).

4. In 2018 the LHC ran with \(\beta^* = 0.30\) m. Find \(\beta_x\) at Q1, 23 m away, and the ratio of beam sizes. Why is \(\beta^* = 23\) m the value that would minimise \(\beta_x\) at Q1, and why is it not used?

Answer

\(\beta_x = 0.30 + 23^2/0.30 = 1764\) m, a size \(\sqrt{1764/0.30} = 77\) times that at the collision point. \(d\beta_x(L^*)/d\beta^* = 1 - L^{*2}/\beta^{*2} = 0\) at \(\beta^* = L^*\), giving \(\beta_x = 2L^* = 46\) m. But luminosity grows as \(1/\beta^*\); the collider accepts a huge beam in the triplet for a small one at the collision point.

5. Derive the hourglass factor for round beams and evaluate it for SuperKEKB-like numbers, \(\beta^* = 0.9\) mm and \(\sigma_z = 6\) mm, if the beams met head on with round optics. Then explain why SuperKEKB avoids this loss.

Answer

Collisions occur along \(s\) with density \(e^{-s^2/\sigma_z^2}/(\sqrt{\pi}\sigma_z)\), and at \(s\) the overlap is reduced by \(\sigma^{*2}/\sigma^2(s) = 1/(1 + s^2/\beta^{*2})\). Integrating gives \(R_{\text{hg}} = \sqrt{\pi}\,t\,e^{t^2}\operatorname{erfc}(t)\) with \(t = 0.15\): \(R_{\text{hg}} = 0.23\). With a half crossing angle of 41.5 mrad and \(\sigma_x^* \approx 17\) µm, the bunches overlap only over \(\sigma_x^*/(\theta_c/2) \approx 0.4\) mm, shorter than \(\beta_y^*\), so collisions happen only where the beams are thinnest.

6. Compute the beam-beam parameter for the LHC design and for HL-LHC beams (\(N = 2.2 \times 10^{11}\), \(\varepsilon_n = 2.5\) µm), head on. Why does \(\beta^*\) not appear?

Answer

\(\xi = Nr_p/(4\pi\varepsilon_n)\): design, \(1.15 \times 10^{11} \times 1.535 \times 10^{-18}/(4\pi \times 3.75 \times 10^{-6}) = 0.0037\); HL-LHC, 0.0107. \(\beta^*\) cancels because the lens strength grows as \(1/\sigma_\perp^2 = 1/(\varepsilon\beta^*)\) while its effect on the tune grows as \(\beta^*\). For HL-LHC, the large crossing angle without crab cavities reduces \(\xi\) to 0.0033 per crossing, and with crab cavities the HL-LHC table gives 0.011.

7. In a van der Meer scan at ATLAS each beam is about 100 µm rms and each bunch holds \(0.8 \times 10^{11}\) protons. Find \(\Sigma\), the peak number of inelastic collisions per crossing (80 mb), and the relative rate at a separation of \(6\sigma\). Show that a sum over 25 equal steps of 0.5 \(\sigma\) recovers \(\Sigma\).

Answer

\(\Sigma = \sqrt{2} \times 100 = 141\) µm. Per crossing, \(\mu_{\text{pu}} = \sigma_{\text{inel}}N_1N_2/(2\pi\Sigma_x\Sigma_y) = 8 \times 10^{-30} \times 6.4 \times 10^{21}/(2\pi \times 2 \times 10^{-8}) = 0.41\) (ATLAS quotes about 0.5). At \(d = 600\) µm, \(R/R_0 = e^{-d^2/2\Sigma^2} = 1.2 \times 10^{-4}\). The trapezoidal sum of \(e^{-d^2/2\Sigma^2}\) over 25 points from \(-600\) to \(+600\) µm, divided by \(\sqrt{2\pi}\), gives 141.42 µm.

8. Show that the luminosity of two coasting beams crossing at an angle \(\theta_c\) is \(\mathcal{L} = I_1I_2/(e^2c\,h_{\text{eff}}\tan(\theta_c/2))\). For the ISR (\(\theta_c = 14.77^\circ\)), what effective height gives \(1.4 \times 10^{32}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\) if each beam carries 30 A? 40 A?

Answer

See section 4: \(K = c(1 + \cos\theta_c)\), densities \(I_i/(ec)\) per unit length, overlap area \(w_1w_2/\sin\theta_c\), so \(\mathcal{L} = I_1I_2(1+\cos\theta_c)/(e^2c\sin\theta_c\,h_{\text{eff}})\). Solving for \(h_{\text{eff}}\) with \(\mathcal{L} = 1.4 \times 10^{36}\) m\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\): 0.64 mm for 30 A and 1.15 mm for 40 A.

9. Burn-off. For the LHC in 2018 (\(n_b = 2556\), \(N_0 = 1.1 \times 10^{11}\), \(\mathcal{L}_0 = 2.1 \times 10^{34}\) at each of two points, 80 mb), find \(\tau_N\), the initial luminosity decay time and the optimum fill length for a turnaround of 2.5 h. How much does a fill of that length deliver?

Answer

\(\tau_N = 2556 \times 1.1 \times 10^{11}/(8 \times 10^{-26} \times 2 \times 2.1 \times 10^{34}) = 8.4 \times 10^4\) s \(= 23.2\) h; decay time 11.6 h. \(t_{\text{opt}} = \sqrt{23.2 \times 2.5} = 7.6\) h. Delivered: \(\mathcal{L}_0\tau_Nt_{\text{f}}/(\tau_N + t_{\text{f}}) = 0.43\) fb\(^{-1}\). The measured decay time (8.4 h) is shorter, so real fills were judged by the observed decay and by faults, not by burn-off alone.

10. For PEP-II (9.0 GeV on 3.1 GeV) find \(\sqrt{s}\) and the boost \(\beta\gamma\) of the centre of mass, and the mean flight distance of a B meson of lifetime 1.5 ps, neglecting its motion in that frame. Repeat for SuperKEKB (7 on 4 GeV) and say why its designers accepted a smaller boost.

Answer

PEP-II: \(\sqrt{s} = 2\sqrt{9.0 \times 3.1} = 10.56\) GeV, \(\beta\gamma = 5.9/10.56 = 0.56\), distance \(0.56 \times 450\) µm \(= 251\) µm. SuperKEKB: 10.58 GeV, \(\beta\gamma = 0.28\), 127 µm. Intrabeam and Touschek scattering fall steeply with energy (chapter 19), so a 4 GeV positron beam keeps its emittance and lifetime better at nano-beam densities than a 3.5 GeV one; the price is a shorter flight, which a vertex detector close to the beam can still resolve.
