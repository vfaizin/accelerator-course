Appendix C: reading list

Where to read further, chapter by chapter, to go deeper than the course: for Part 0, Foundations, the texts its chapters cite; for chapters 1 to 29, free sources first, then textbooks, the CERN Accelerator School, the original papers, histories, journals, and the books already in references/.

The course needs no reading beyond itself. It assumes high-school mathematics and physics, at the level of SPM Additional Mathematics or A-level, and Part 0, Foundations, teaches the first-year mathematics and physics built on that; the “Pre-requisite” sections inside each chapter teach the rest where it is first needed. Nothing here has to be read before a chapter. The books listed, including those in references/ (Landau and Lifshitz, Tong, Penrose, Schwichtenberg, and the accelerator texts in references/accelerators/), are for going deeper, after a chapter has been read.

Every chapter ends with its own Sources section, which lists everything that chapter used, down to single facts. This appendix is shorter and more selective. It names the sources worth reading in full or in part, says what each is good for and at what level, and ties each to the chapters it serves. Where a chapter’s Sources section gives more, the appendix says so and does not repeat it.

How to use this list

Read in this order.

  1. The chapter first. Each chapter gives the physical picture, the derivation and real numbers. Read it, do its exercises, and note what was quoted without derivation.
  2. One textbook beside it. For the derivations a chapter quotes, go to the textbook named for that chapter in the table below. Two textbooks are free and between them cover most of the course: Humphries for Parts I to III and S. Y. Lee for Parts IV and V. Wiedemann, also free, is the fullest single book.
  3. A CERN Accelerator School lecture. These are short, written by people who run the machines, and free. They are the best second view of any topic and usually carry the numbers of a real machine.
  4. The original paper. Read it once you know the result, to see what the authors knew, what they measured and what they got wrong. Most are short.
  5. The theory texts you already own. To go deeper where a chapter leans on mechanics or electrodynamics (Liouville’s theorem, adiabatic invariants, synchrotron radiation), the exact sections in Landau and Lifshitz and in Tong are listed at the end of this appendix.

For Part 0, the first two steps are the same: the Foundations chapter first, then one of the textbooks its Sources name, listed below.

A note on levels. The Part 0 texts are first-year undergraduate books unless marked otherwise. For chapters 1 to 29, “introductory” here means a final-year undergraduate in physics can read it straight through. “Graduate” means it assumes Hamiltonian mechanics and electrodynamics at the level of Landau and Lifshitz and moves fast. “Reference” means a book to look things up in, not to read through.

Part 0, Foundations

The texts each Foundations chapter cites in its Sources section, for going further than the chapter. The chapters themselves need nothing beyond high-school mathematics and physics. The textbooks here are first-year undergraduate books unless an entry says it is the next step; “(free)” marks a text that is free to read, with the place named in the chapter’s Sources. Two free series run through Part 0: The Feynman Lectures on Physics (feynmanlectures.caltech.edu), which every Foundations chapter cites, and David Tong’s Cambridge lecture notes (damtp.cam.ac.uk), which F2, F3, F4 and F7 cite.

Textbooks

Each entry gives what the book is good for, its level, and the chapters of this course it serves. The chapter numbers are this course’s. Where a book’s own chapter titles are given, they were read from the book or from its publisher’s records.

S. Humphries, Principles of Charged Particle Acceleration (Wiley, 1986). Free.

The best single book for Parts I to III. It is written for engineers and physicists who will build things: every result is derived from the Lorentz force and Maxwell’s equations, with circuits, insulators and magnets treated as seriously as orbits. It stops short of the modern theory of rings (no Courant-Snyder theory beyond transfer matrices, no synchrotron radiation). Introductory.

Its fifteen chapters and the course chapters they serve: 1 Introduction and 2 Particle Dynamics (chapters 1, 2); 3 Electric and Magnetic Forces, 4 Steady-State Electric and Magnetic Fields and 5 Modification of Electric and Magnetic Fields by Materials (4, 6, 17); 6 Electric and Magnetic Field Lenses (2, 7); 7 Calculation of Particle Orbits in Focusing Fields (10, 11, 15); 8 Transfer Matrices and Periodic Focusing Systems (16); 9 Electrostatic Accelerators and Pulsed High Voltage (4, 5, 6); 10 Linear Induction Accelerators (27); 11 Betatrons (11); 12 Resonant Cavities and Waveguides (9, 13); 13 Phase Dynamics (12, 14, 19); 14 Radio-Frequency Linear Accelerators (9, 14); 15 Cyclotrons and Synchrotrons (10, 12, 15).

S. Humphries, Charged Particle Beams (Wiley, 1990). Free.

The companion volume, on beams rather than single particles: emittance and brightness, the Child law and its relativistic form, the Pierce gun, extraction from a plasma, space-charge-dominated beams and the envelope equation. Introductory to graduate. Serves chapters 2, 7, 16 and 22.

E. J. N. Wilson, An Introduction to Particle Accelerators (Oxford University Press, 2001).

A short, clear book by a CERN accelerator physicist, built from his lectures. It favours the physical picture over the full derivation and is the quickest way into the theory of rings. Introductory. Chapters (from the publisher’s records): History of accelerators; Transverse motion; Lattices; Circulating beams; Longitudinal dynamics; Imperfections and multipoles; Non-linearities and resonances; Electrons; Space charge and instabilities; Radio frequency cavities; Colliders; Cooling; Applications of accelerators; The future. It serves chapters 15 to 24 and 28, and is a good first read before Lee or Wiedemann.

K. Wille, The Physics of Particle Accelerators: An Introduction (Oxford University Press, 2000; translated from the German).

Strongest on electron machines: synchrotron radiation, linear optics, injection and extraction, RF systems, radiative effects, luminosity, wigglers and undulators, the free-electron laser and diagnostics (its chapter titles, from the publisher’s records). Its introduction treats the betatron and the early machines. Introductory. Serves chapters 11, 13, 15 to 17, 19 to 21, 23, 26 and 8.

H. Wiedemann, Particle Accelerator Physics (Springer, 2015). Free.

The fullest single textbook: 27 chapters from linear accelerators to free-electron lasers, with chapters marked with an asterisk for the harder material (Vlasov and Fokker-Planck equations, equilibrium distributions, Hamiltonian nonlinear dynamics, beam-cavity interaction, coupled motion, collective effects, wake fields). Introductory to graduate. The chapters map onto this course as follows: 2 Linear Accelerators (14); 3 Circular Accelerators (10 to 12, 15); 4 Elements of Classical Mechanics and 5 Particle Dynamics in Electro-Magnetic Fields (background for all); 7 Single Particle Dynamics, 8 Particle Beams and Phase Space, 10 Periodic Focusing Systems (16); 9 Longitudinal Beam Dynamics and 18 Charged Particle Acceleration (19); 11 Particle Beam Parameters, 12 and 13 on distributions, 14 Beam Emittance and Lattice Design, 23 to 26 on synchrotron radiation and insertion devices (20); 15 Perturbations, 16 Resonances, 17 Hamiltonian Nonlinear Beam Dynamics, 20 Coupled Motion (18); 19 Beam-Cavity Interaction, 21 Statistical and Collective Effects, 22 Wake Fields and Instabilities (13, 22); 27 Free Electron Lasers (26).

S. Y. Lee, Accelerator Physics, 4th edition (World Scientific, 2019). Free; a copy is in references/accelerators/.

The standard graduate text for rings, built on the Hamiltonian from the start. Five long chapters: 1 Introduction (with a history and the layout of accelerators); 2 Transverse Motion (the Hamiltonian, linear betatron motion, linear imperfections, the off-momentum orbit, chromatic aberration, linear coupling, nonlinear resonances, collective instability and Landau damping); 3 Synchrotron Motion (the longitudinal equation, adiabatic and nonadiabatic motion, RF phase and voltage modulation, beam manipulation in phase space, RF systems, longitudinal collective instabilities, an introduction to linear accelerators); 4 Physics of Electron Storage Rings (fields of a moving charge, radiation damping and excitation, emittance); 5 Special Topics (the free-electron laser, the beam-beam interaction). Its appendices on Hamiltonian dynamics, stochastic beam dynamics and the Fourier transform are worth reading on their own. Graduate. Serves chapters 12, 14 to 16, 18 to 20, 22 to 24 and 26.

M. Conte and W. W. MacKay, An Introduction to the Physics of Particle Accelerators, 2nd edition (World Scientific, 2008; first edition 1991).

A compact introduction that starts from weak focusing and the mechanics of trajectories and builds up to strong focusing, synchrotron oscillations, synchrotron radiation, RF linacs, resonances, space charge, cooling (a chapter called “How to Baffle Liouville”), spin dynamics, and position measurements and spectra (its chapter titles, from the publisher’s records). It has a chapter of lattice exercises. Introductory. Serves chapters 11, 14 to 16, 18 to 20, 22, 24 and 8. Its spin chapter is the gentlest place to read about the polarisation used in chapter 18’s energy calibration.

D. A. Edwards and M. J. Syphers, An Introduction to the Physics of High Energy Accelerators (Wiley, 1993).

Written by two Fermilab physicists for the US Particle Accelerator School. Eight chapters: Introduction; Acceleration and Phase Stability; Transverse Linear Motion; Resonances and Transverse Nonlinear Motion; Transverse Coupled Motion; Intensity Dependent Effects; Emittance Preservation; Synchrotron Radiation. Short, careful, and the clearest account of transition and of emittance preservation through a chain of machines. Introductory to graduate. Serves chapters 11, 15, 16, 18, 19, 21 and 22.

T. P. Wangler, RF Linear Accelerators, 2nd edition (Wiley-VCH, 2008; the first edition, 1998, was titled Principles of RF Linear Accelerators). A copy is in references/accelerators/.

The standard text on linacs, from Los Alamos. Twelve chapters: Introduction; RF Acceleration in Linacs; Periodic Accelerating Structures; Standard Linac Structures (with a section on structures for superconducting linacs); Microwave Topics for Linacs; Longitudinal Particle Dynamics; Transverse Particle Dynamics; Radiofrequency Quadrupole Linac; Multiparticle Dynamics with Space Charge; Beam Loading; Wakefields; Special Structures and Techniques. It uses the cosine convention for the RF phase (\(\Delta W = qE_0 T L\cos\phi\) with \(\phi_s\) negative), which this course converts to the sine form. Graduate. Serves chapters 4 (the Kilpatrick criterion), 9, 13, 14 and 22.

A. W. Chao, M. Tigner, H. Weise and F. Zimmermann (eds.), Handbook of Accelerator Physics and Engineering, 3rd edition (World Scientific, 2023). A copy is in references/accelerators/.

A reference, not a textbook: short sections by specialists, each a page or a few pages of formulas, tables and references. By its own preface the first edition (A. W. Chao and M. Tigner, eds.) was issued in September 1998, the second in July 2012 and the third in December 2022. Its seven parts: 1 Introduction (constants, units, fundamental formulae, a glossary of accelerator types, codes); 2 beam dynamics (2.1 Phase Space, 2.2 Optics and Lattice, 2.3 Nonlinear Dynamics, 2.4 Collective Effects, 2.5 Beam-Beam Effects, 2.6 Polarization, 2.7 Beam Cooling); 3 Electromagnetic and Nuclear Interactions (3.1 Synchrotron Radiation, 3.2 Impedances and Wake Functions, 3.3 Particle-Matter Interaction); 4 Operational and Design Considerations (luminosity, brightness, linac operation, bunch compression, final focus, injection and extraction, high-intensity hadron beams, among others); 5 Mechanical Considerations (vacuum, alignment, ground vibration); 6 Electrical Considerations (materials, transmission lines and cavities, multipacting, power converters, high-gradient limits, high-voltage technique, magnetic measurements); 7 Subsystems (particle sources, focusing, acceleration, instrumentation, controls). Reference. Serves every chapter from 3 on; the chapters of this course cite it most in 18, 19, 22, 23, 24 and 27.

M. S. Livingston and J. P. Blewett, Particle Accelerators (McGraw-Hill, 1962). Three scanned copies are in references/accelerators/.

Written by two of the builders (Livingston worked on the first cyclotrons, Blewett on the Brookhaven machines and the theory of strong focusing). It gives each kind of machine its own chapter, with its history, its theory and its engineering as they stood in 1962: 1 Introduction; 2 Low-voltage Generators; 3 The Electrostatic Generator; 4 Ion Sources; 5 Particle Motion in Electric and Magnetic Fields; 6 The Cyclotron (Magnetic Resonance Accelerator); 7 The Betatron (Magnetic Induction Accelerator); 8 Magnet Design and Measurement; 9 The Principle of Phase Stability; 10 The Linear Accelerator; 11 The Synchrocyclotron; 12 The Electron Synchrotron; 13 The Proton Synchrotron; 14 Shielding for Accelerators; and a final chapter on alternating-gradient accelerators. Introductory. The best single source for Parts II to IV as the people of the time understood them; serves chapters 5 to 17.
