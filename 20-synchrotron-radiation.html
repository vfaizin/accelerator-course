20 Synchrotron radiation and light sources

1898 to today: Liénard’s formula, the light seen at General Electric’s 70 MeV synchrotron in April 1947, the dedicated rings from Daresbury (1980) to MAX IV (2016) and ESRF-EBS (2020), and LEP at 104 GeV; past the wall of the radiation that ended the betatron, by paying for it with radio-frequency power and then using it.

A note on symbols. In this chapter \(E\) is always an energy (a particle’s total energy, in J or GeV), and the electric field is \(\mathcal{E}\) (V/m), as in F4. Three Greek letters do double duty, and the subscript says which: \(\rho\) (m) is a bending radius, \(\rho_q\) (C/m\(^3\)) a charge density; \(\varepsilon_0\) is the permittivity of free space, \(\varepsilon_c\) (J or keV) the critical photon energy, \(\varepsilon\) or \(\varepsilon_{x0}\) (m rad) an emittance; \(\gamma\) alone is the Lorentz factor and \(\beta\) alone is \(v/c\), while \(\alpha_x\), \(\beta_x\), \(\gamma_x\) (and the same with \(y\)) are the optical functions of chapter 16.

In one page

The wall. A charge bent on a circle is accelerated towards the centre, so it radiates. For electrons the loss grows as the fourth power of the energy. Iwanenko and Pomeranchuk warned in 1944 that it would cap the betatron; Blewett saw it shrink the orbit of General Electric’s 100 MeV betatron in 1946. An induction machine cannot replace the loss.

The idea. In a synchrotron a radio-frequency (RF) cavity replaces it, turn by turn. Then the radiation turns out to be a gift twice over. It damps the beam’s oscillations in all three planes, so an electron beam forgets its injection and settles to a size fixed by the ring itself. And the light, intense, collimated and spanning infrared to hard X-rays, became the purpose of rings built for it.

The equations to remember. For a particle of charge \(e\), mass \(m\), total energy \(E\), on a bend of radius \(\rho\) (m), the energy lost per turn in an isomagnetic ring (J):

\[ U_0 = \frac{e^2\beta^3\gamma^4}{3\varepsilon_0\rho}, \qquad U_0\,[\mathrm{keV}] = 88.46\,\frac{E^4\,[\mathrm{GeV}]}{\rho\,[\mathrm{m}]}\ \ \text{(electrons)} \]

The light comes in a cone of half-angle about \(1/\gamma\), with a spectrum reaching to the critical photon energy

\[ \varepsilon_c = \frac{3\hbar c\gamma^3}{2\rho}, \qquad \varepsilon_c\,[\mathrm{keV}] = 2.218\,\frac{E^3\,[\mathrm{GeV}]}{\rho\,[\mathrm{m}]} = 0.665\,E^2\,[\mathrm{GeV}]\,B\,[\mathrm{T}] \]

Damping times, with revolution period \(T_0\) and partition numbers obeying Robinson’s theorem:

\[ \tau_{x,y,E} = \frac{2ET_0}{J_{x,y,E}\,U_0}, \qquad J_x + J_y + J_E = 4 \]

Quantum excitation against damping sets the natural emittance, \(\varepsilon_{x0} = C_q\gamma^2 I_5/(J_x I_2)\) with \(C_q = 3.832\times10^{-13}\) m; for a lattice of dipoles each bending by \(\theta\), \(\varepsilon_{x0} = F\,C_q\gamma^2\theta^3/J_x\). An undulator of period \(\lambda_u\) and strength \(K = 0.934\,B\,[\mathrm{T}]\,\lambda_u\,[\mathrm{cm}]\) emits, in harmonic \(n\) and at an angle \(\theta\) to its axis (here \(\theta\) is the observation angle, not a bend), at

\[ \lambda = \frac{\lambda_u}{2n\gamma^2}\left(1 + \frac{K^2}{2} + \gamma^2\theta^2\right) \]

Numbers to know. At 3 GeV, \(1/\gamma = 0.17\) mrad. MAX IV (3 GeV, 528 m) loses 363.8 keV a turn in its dipoles, damps in 16, 29 and 25 ms, and has a natural emittance of 328 pm rad. LEP at 104 GeV on \(\rho = 3026\) m lost 3.42 GeV a turn, 3.3 per cent of its energy, and needed about 3.7 GV of RF. The LHC’s 7 TeV protons lose 6.7 keV a turn, about 3.9 kW per beam into a cold beam pipe. Second-generation rings had emittances near 100 nm rad; fourth-generation rings have tens to a few hundred pm rad.

The wall

By the end of the Second World War the betatron (chapter 11) had taken electrons to 100 MeV at General Electric in Schenectady. The question was how far it could go. In 1944 Dmitri Iwanenko and Isaak Pomeranchuk, in Moscow, published “On the maximal energy attainable in a betatron” in Physical Review. Their point was classical electrodynamics: a charge on a circle has a centripetal acceleration and must radiate, and for a relativistic electron the radiated power grows so fast with energy that it sets an upper limit to what an induction machine can reach.

The betatron gives energy by the changing flux through its orbit. The flux follows a programme fixed in advance by the two-to-one rule. Energy radiated is momentum lost that nothing replaces, and a momentum deficit pulls the orbit inwards. John Blewett at General Electric worked out what this does to the orbit and looked for it in the 100 MeV betatron (Blewett, Physical Review 69, 87, 1946). The orbit shrank as predicted: a smaller orbit-shift pulse than expected was needed to move the electrons onto a target inside the equilibrium orbit, and at a phase of the field cycle that theory predicted they reached it with no shift pulse at all. The radiation had been seen through its effect on the orbit before anyone saw the light (chapter 11 has the numbers).

The way past was already known. Veksler (1944) and McMillan (1945) had shown that a ring with an RF gap and phase stability (chapter 12) keeps particles in step while the field rises; such a synchrotron (chapter 15) gives energy from a gap whose voltage can be raised to pay whatever the radiation takes. General Electric built one, for 70 MeV. Its vacuum chamber was glass. On 24 April 1947, pushing the electron gun and pulse transformer hard, the team were warned by a technician (Floyd Haber, in later accounts) watching the tube in a mirror around the shielding wall that there was an arc in the tube (figure 20.1). The vacuum was fine. Herbert Pollock and Robert Langmuir looked for themselves and saw it too: a gleam of bluish-white light from the tube, in the words of a CERN Courier account of 1997. They considered Cherenkov radiation, then recognised it as the radiation Iwanenko and Pomeranchuk had predicted (Pollock’s account, 1983, as quoted in later histories). F. R. Elder, A. M. Gurewitsch, R. V. Langmuir and H. C. Pollock reported it in “Radiation from electrons in a synchrotron”, received by Physical Review on 7 May 1947 and published on 1 June (volume 71, page 829). The light changed colour and brightness with the electron energy, as theory said it should.

Figure 20.1. How the light was first seen, at General Electric’s 70 MeV synchrotron on 24 April 1947, seen from above; schematic, with the wall and mirror placed for the drawing, not from a plan. The electrons circulate in a glass vacuum chamber, and each point of the orbit sends its light forward along the tangent, within about \(1/\gamma\) of it (section 4). The glow could therefore be seen only from where a tangent reaches: here past the end of the shielding wall, in the mirror that a technician watched from behind it.

The theory was older than the machine. Alfred-Marie Liénard had found the fields of a point charge in arbitrary motion in 1898 and with them the power radiated by a fast charge; Emil Wiechert found the potentials independently in 1900. Julian Schwinger worked out the spectrum and angular distribution in a preprint of 1945 and a talk of 1946, and published “On the classical radiation of accelerated electrons” in 1949 (Physical Review 75, 1912). From then on the loss was understood exactly, and the problem was engineering: how much RF power, how much heat in the vacuum chamber, and what the radiation does to the beam.

That last question had a surprising answer. The radiation does not only take energy; it damps the beam’s oscillations and also excites them with the randomness of photon emission. An electron ring therefore reaches an equilibrium that depends only on its magnets. That made electron storage rings possible (the colliders of chapter 23), and the light itself made a new kind of laboratory. This chapter derives the radiation, its effect on the beam, and the light sources built on it, and ends at the wall the radiation sets for electron rings at high energy.

The physics

Pre-requisite: Retarded potentials, Lienard-Wiechert fields

Pre-requisite. This section gives you the field of a charge in any motion. It sets up the retarded time, the moment at which the field now seen was sent out. It derives the retarded potentials and, for a point charge, the Liénard-Wiechert potentials, and from them the part of the field that falls as \(1/R\): the radiation field \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{rad} \propto \mathbf{n}\times(\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{a})\) with \(\mathbf{B}_{rad} = \mathbf{n}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{rad}/c\), and the energy it carries away. It also shows why a fast charge squeezes what it sends ahead of it by the factor \(1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta\). Section 1 starts from the radiation field to derive Larmor’s formula, and section 5 uses the squeezing to find the critical frequency. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, volume 2, §62 (the retarded potentials), §63 (the Liénard-Wiechert potentials) and §66 (the field at large distances), and Tong, Electromagnetism, §7.1 (retarded potentials) and §7.4.1 to §7.4.3 (the Liénard-Wiechert potentials and the fields computed from them).

The idea. Coulomb’s law gives the field here from where the charge is now. That cannot be right when the charge moves, because no news travels faster than light. The field at a point \(\mathbf{r}\) at time \(t\) must be set by what the charge was doing at an earlier time, early enough for a signal at speed \(c\) to cover the distance. If the charge was at distance \(R\) (m) when it sent that signal, the signal left at the retarded time

\[ t_r = t - \frac{R}{c} \]

Chapter 1’s kink picture used this idea for one sudden push. This section makes it exact for any motion.

Potentials obey a wave equation. Write the fields through a scalar potential \(\phi\) (V) and a vector potential \(\mathbf{A}\) (T m):

\[ \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\nabla\phi - \frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial t}, \qquad \mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A} \]

Why these two forms? Since \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0\) (F4, section 13), \(\mathbf{B}\) can always be written as the curl of something, and that something is \(\mathbf{A}\) (a curl has no divergence; chapter 2 met \(\mathbf{A}\) for a lens). Faraday’s law, \(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t\) (F4, section 14), then reads \(\nabla\times(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} + \partial\mathbf{A}/\partial t) = 0\). A field with no curl is the gradient of a potential, as in electrostatics (F2, section 11), so \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} + \partial\mathbf{A}/\partial t = -\nabla\phi\). For fields that do not change, this is the familiar \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\nabla\phi\).

The potentials are not unique: one may add the gradient of any function to \(\mathbf{A}\) if one subtracts its time derivative from \(\phi\), and the fields do not change. Fix this freedom by asking that \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A} + (1/c^2)\,\partial\phi/\partial t = 0\) (the Lorenz condition, after Ludvig Lorenz). Then Maxwell’s equations (F4, section 16) with a charge density \(\rho_q\) (C/m\(^3\)) and a current density \(\mathbf{J}\) (A/m\(^2\)) become four copies of one equation (one for \(\phi\), one for each component of \(\mathbf{A}\)). For \(\phi\): put \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\nabla\phi - \partial\mathbf{A}/\partial t\) into Gauss’s law \(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = \rho_q/\varepsilon_0\), to get \(-\nabla^2\phi - \partial(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A})/\partial t = \rho_q/\varepsilon_0\), and replace \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A}\) by \(-(1/c^2)\,\partial\phi/\partial t\) from the Lorenz condition. For \(\mathbf{A}\): put both fields into the Ampère-Maxwell law \(\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{J} + (1/c^2)\,\partial\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}/\partial t\), use the identity \(\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{A}) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A}) - \nabla^2\mathbf{A}\) (which you can check component by component), and the terms in \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A}\) and \(\phi\) collect into the gradient of the Lorenz condition, which is zero. What is left is

\[ \nabla^2\phi - \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial t^2} = -\frac{\rho_q}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \nabla^2\mathbf{A} - \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\mathbf{A}}{\partial t^2} = -\mu_0\mathbf{J} \]

(Tong, §7.1, and Landau and Lifshitz, §62, carry out the substitution.) For charges at rest the time derivative drops out and the first equation is Poisson’s equation, whose solution is Coulomb’s potential. The task is to find what replaces Coulomb when the sources change.

One small source. Put a small charge \(dq(t)\) at the origin and let it change with time. (Charge cannot appear from nothing; think of \(dq\) as one small piece of a larger distribution whose total is conserved.) Away from the origin there is no source, and by symmetry \(\phi\) depends only on the distance \(r\) and on \(t\). For a function of \(r\) alone the Laplacian is \(\nabla^2\phi = \partial^2\phi/\partial r^2 + (2/r)\,\partial\phi/\partial r\) (in spherical coordinates, F2, section 13), and this can be written \(\nabla^2\phi = (1/r)\,\partial^2(r\phi)/\partial r^2\): differentiating twice, \((r\phi)'' = (\phi + r\phi')' = 2\phi' + r\phi''\), and dividing by \(r\) gives the same two terms. So \(\chi = r\phi\) obeys

\[ \frac{\partial^2\chi}{\partial r^2} - \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\chi}{\partial t^2} = 0 \]

the wave equation in one dimension. Its solutions are any function of \(t - r/c\), a wave travelling outwards, plus any function of \(t + r/c\), a wave coming in. A source makes waves that go out; an incoming wave would have to be prepared from outside, before the source did anything. Keep only the first:

\[ \phi = \frac{f(t - r/c)}{r} \]

Now fix \(f\). Close to the origin \(\phi\) grows like \(1/r\), so its space derivatives (of order \(1/r^3\)) swamp its time derivative. Near the charge, then, the equation is Poisson’s, and \(\phi\) must look like Coulomb’s potential of the charge at that moment, \(dq(t)/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)\). That gives \(f(t) = dq(t)/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)\), and

\[ \phi(r, t) = \frac{dq(t - r/c)}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,r} \]

It is Coulomb’s potential, with the charge read at the retarded time.

The retarded potentials. Every piece of a distribution acts this way, and the equations are linear, so add the pieces. With \(R = |\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'|\) the distance from the source point \(\mathbf{r}'\) to the field point \(\mathbf{r}\):

\[ \phi(\mathbf{r}, t) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{\rho_q(\mathbf{r}', t - R/c)}{R}\,dV', \qquad \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r}, t) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{r}', t - R/c)}{R}\,dV' \]

For steady sources these are Coulomb’s law and the Biot-Savart potential. The only change is the time at which each source is read.

A point charge: the Liénard-Wiechert potentials. Let a charge \(q\) move along a path \(\mathbf{r}_q(t')\) with velocity \(\mathbf{v} = \boldsymbol\beta c\). Its retarded time \(t'\) is the root of

\[ t - t' = \frac{|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_q(t')|}{c} \]

There is exactly one root, because the charge moves slower than light: as \(t'\) runs back from \(t\) the left side grows at rate 1 and the right side at a rate below 1, so they cross once. The obvious guess, \(\phi = q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 R)\) with \(R\) taken at \(t'\), is wrong by a factor. To find it, write the point charge as a density concentrated at one point, \(\rho_q(\mathbf{r}', t'') = q\,\delta^3(\mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r}_q(t''))\), and write the retarded time with a delta function in time as well. (A delta function \(\delta(x)\) is zero except at \(x = 0\) and has unit area, so \(\int g(x)\,\delta(x - x_0)\,dx = g(x_0)\).)

\[ \phi(\mathbf{r}, t) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\!\!\int\frac{\rho_q(\mathbf{r}', t'')}{|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'|}\,\delta\!\left(t'' - t + \frac{|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'|}{c}\right)dt''\,dV' = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{\delta\left(F(t'')\right)}{R(t'')}\,dt'' \]

where \(R(t'') = |\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_q(t'')|\) and \(F(t'') = t'' - t + R(t'')/c\). Near its root \(t'\), \(F \approx F'(t')\,(t'' - t')\), and a delta function of a scaled argument is the delta function divided by the scale: \(\delta(F) = \delta(t'' - t')/|F'(t')|\). (Substitute \(u = F'(t')\,(t'' - t')\) in \(\int g\,\delta(F)\,dt''\): then \(dt'' = du/|F'|\), with the absolute value because reversing the direction of integration does not change the sign of an area, and the integral is \(g(t')/|F'(t')|\).) The derivative is \(F' = 1 + (1/c)\,dR/dt''\). The distance shrinks at the rate at which the charge moves towards the field point: \(dR/dt'' = -\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v}\), with \(\mathbf{n}\) the unit vector from the charge to the field point. So \(F' = 1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta\), and

\[ \phi = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{1}{R\,(1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta)}, \qquad \mathbf{A} = \frac{\mu_0 q}{4\pi}\,\frac{\mathbf{v}}{R\,(1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta)} = \frac{\mathbf{v}}{c^2}\,\phi \]

with \(R\), \(\mathbf{n}\) and \(\boldsymbol\beta\) all taken at the retarded time \(t'\). These are the Liénard-Wiechert potentials (Liénard 1898, Wiechert 1900).

The squeezing factor. Differentiate the retardation condition \(t = t' + R(t')/c\) with respect to \(t'\):

\[ \frac{dt}{dt'} = 1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta \]

Signals the charge sends over a time \(dt'\) reach the observer spread over \(dt = (1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta)\,dt'\). If the charge moves towards the observer, each later signal starts from a point closer to them and has less distance to cover; the first signal’s head start shrinks as the charge chases it. Seen from the side (\(\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta = 0\)) there is no squeezing. Seen from straight ahead, with \(\beta\) close to 1, the squeezing is enormous. Seen from behind, \(\mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta = -\beta\), the signals are stretched instead, by \(1 + \beta\).

Figure 20.2. The squeezing factor, drawn to scale. A charge moving to the right at \(\beta = 0.7\) sends a signal at five equal intervals of its time, \(t' = 0\) to 4 (red dots, where it was). Each signal spreads as a sphere at \(c\) about the point it left from (blue, as they are one time unit after the last). Ahead of the charge the spheres are only \(1 - \beta = 0.3\) units apart, so an observer there receives the five signals in 0.3 of the time it took to send them; behind, they are \(1 + \beta = 1.7\) units apart. At \(\beta = 0.99999999\) the crowding ahead is a factor of \(10^8\).

At work. A 3 GeV electron (\(\gamma = 5871\)) has \(1 - \beta = 1.45\times10^{-8}\). Let it travel 1 m straight towards a detector. It takes 3.336 ns to do so, but everything it sends during that time arrives within \(3.336\ \mathrm{ns}\times1.45\times10^{-8} = 48\) attoseconds: the light emitted over a metre arrives as a pulse 14.5 nm long. An electron leaving an RF gun at 1 MeV kinetic energy (\(\beta = 0.941\)) squeezes 3.54 ns into 209 ps. Section 5 uses exactly this to explain why a ring’s light reaches X-ray frequencies.

The fields, and the part that radiates. Now take \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\nabla\phi - \partial\mathbf{A}/\partial t\) and \(\mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}\). The work is in remembering that \(t'\) depends on \(\mathbf{r}\) and \(t\). The full result (Landau and Lifshitz, equation 63.8; Tong, §7.4.3) has two parts. One depends only on the velocity and falls as \(1/R^2\): it is the field of a uniformly moving charge, carried along with it. The other depends on the acceleration and falls as \(1/R\):

\[ \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{rad} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 c}\,\frac{\mathbf{n}\times\left[(\mathbf{n} - \boldsymbol\beta)\times\dot{\boldsymbol\beta}\right]}{R\,(1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta)^3}, \qquad \mathbf{B}_{rad} = \frac{1}{c}\,\mathbf{n}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{rad} \]

with \(\dot{\boldsymbol\beta} = \mathbf{a}/c\). Three powers of \(1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta\) appear. One is in the potentials. Differentiating that factor \(1/(1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta)\) squares it, and the derivative is taken with respect to the observer’s time, which brings the third through \(\partial t'/\partial t = 1/(1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta)\).

The slow case shows where the \(1/R\) part comes from, and it is short enough to do in full. Take \(v \ll c\), so that \(1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta \approx 1\) except where it is differentiated. Differentiating \(1/R\) gives \(1/R^2\), which dies away; the \(1/R\) part comes only from differentiating the retarded time inside \(\mathbf{v}(t')\). From \(t' = t - R/c\), the gradient of \(t'\) at fixed \(t\) is \(\nabla t' = -\mathbf{n}/c\) (moving the field point away by \(dR\) makes the signal older by \(dR/c\)), and \(\partial t'/\partial t \approx 1\).

  • Magnetic field. \(\mathbf{A} = \mu_0 q\,\mathbf{v}(t')/(4\pi R)\), and the curl of a vector that depends on position only through \(t'\) is \(\nabla t'\times d\mathbf{v}/dt' = -(\mathbf{n}/c)\times\mathbf{a}\). (By the chain rule, \(\partial v_z/\partial y = (dv_z/dt')\,\partial t'/\partial y\), and so on for each component of the curl; collecting them gives the cross product of \(\nabla t'\) with \(d\mathbf{v}/dt'\).) So \(\mathbf{B}_{rad} = -\mu_0 q\,\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{a}/(4\pi c R)\).
  • Electric field. \(-\partial\mathbf{A}/\partial t = -\mu_0 q\,\mathbf{a}/(4\pi R)\). In \(\phi \approx q(1 + \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v}/c)/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 R)\) (from \(1/(1 - x) \approx 1 + x\) for small \(x\), the Taylor series of the Liénard-Wiechert \(\phi\)) the \(1/R\) part of the gradient is \((q/4\pi\varepsilon_0 R c)(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{a})\nabla t'\), so \(-\nabla\phi\) contributes \(+q(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{a})\mathbf{n}/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 c^2 R)\). With \(\mu_0 = 1/(\varepsilon_0 c^2)\) the two add to

\[ \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{rad} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 c^2 R}\left[(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{a})\,\mathbf{n} - \mathbf{a}\right] = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 c^2 R}\,\mathbf{n}\times(\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{a}) \]

using the vector identity \(\mathbf{n}\times(\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{a}) = \mathbf{n}(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{a}) - \mathbf{a}\) for a unit vector \(\mathbf{n}\). It is the general formula with \(\boldsymbol\beta = 0\) in it. And \(\mathbf{n}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{rad}/c = -q\,\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{a}/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 c^3 R)\), which is the \(\mathbf{B}_{rad}\) just found.

Read the formula. The bracket is minus the part of \(\mathbf{a}\) across the line of sight, so the field is transverse to \(\mathbf{n}\), has size \(q\,a\sin\Theta/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 c^2 R)\) with \(\Theta\) the angle between \(\mathbf{n}\) and \(\mathbf{a}\), and vanishes along the acceleration. \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\), \(\mathbf{B}\) and \(\mathbf{n}\) are mutually perpendicular, with \(B = \mathcal{E}/c\): locally, a plane wave moving outwards. Against the Coulomb field \(q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 R^2)\) it is larger by \(aR\sin\Theta/c^2\), so beyond a distance of about \(c^2/a\) the radiation dominates.

At work. A 1 keV electron circling in 1 T has \(v = 1.876\times10^7\) m/s and \(a = evB/m_e = 3.30\times10^{18}\) m/s\(^2\), so \(c^2/a = 27\) mm. At 1 m, side-on, its radiation field is \(5.3\times10^{-8}\) V/m against a Coulomb field of \(1.4\times10^{-9}\) V/m. An electron starting from rest in an RF gun at 100 MV/m has \(a = e\mathcal{E}/m_e = 1.76\times10^{19}\) m/s\(^2\) and \(c^2/a = m_e c^2/(e\mathcal{E}) = 5.1\) mm, the distance over which it gains its own rest energy.

The energy carried away. The energy flow is the Poynting vector, \(\mathbf{S} = \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\times\mathbf{B}/\mu_0\) (W/m\(^2\)). With \(\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{n}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}/c\) and \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\perp\mathbf{n}\), \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\times(\mathbf{n}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}) = \mathbf{n}|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}|^2 - \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\mathbf{n}) = \mathbf{n}|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}|^2\), so

\[ \mathbf{S} = \frac{|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}|^2}{\mu_0 c}\,\mathbf{n} = \varepsilon_0 c\,|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}|^2\,\mathbf{n} \]

outwards along the line of sight. A small detector at distance \(R\) subtending a solid angle \(d\Omega\) (sr) has area \(R^2\,d\Omega\) (a solid angle is the area cut out on a sphere divided by the radius squared; the whole sphere is \(4\pi\) sr), so the power per unit solid angle is

\[ \frac{dP}{d\Omega} = R^2 S = \varepsilon_0 c\,\left|R\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{rad}\right|^2 \]

Since \(\mathcal{E}_{rad}\) falls as \(1/R\), this does not depend on \(R\): the same power crosses every sphere, so it has left the charge for good. The velocity field, falling as \(1/R^2\), gives a flux that dies as \(1/R^4\) and carries nothing to infinity. For a fast charge one more factor matters: this is power per unit of the observer’s time \(t\). Per unit of the retarded time \(t'\), the laboratory time at which the charge emits, which is what the charge loses, multiply by \(dt/dt' = 1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta\) (Landau and Lifshitz, §73).

At work. For the 1 keV electron, side-on, \(S = 7.4\times10^{-18}\) W/m\(^2\) at 1 m, so \(dP/d\Omega = 7.4\times10^{-18}\) W/sr. Section 1 integrates the \(\sin^2\Theta\) pattern over all directions and gets \(6.2\times10^{-17}\) W.

1. The power radiated by a slow charge: Larmor

Start with a charge \(q\) moving slowly, \(v \ll c\), with acceleration \(\mathbf{a}\) (m/s\(^2\)). Far away, at distance \(R\) (m) in direction \(\mathbf{n}\) (in this section only, \(R\) is the distance to the observer, not a ring’s average radius), its field has a part that falls only as \(1/R\). This is the radiation field, evaluated at the retarded time \(t - R/c\):

\[ \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{rad} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 c^2 R}\,\mathbf{n}\times(\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{a}), \qquad \mathbf{B}_{rad} = \frac{1}{c}\,\mathbf{n}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{rad} \]

Its magnitude is \(|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{rad}| = q a\sin\Theta/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 c^2 R)\), where \(\Theta\) is the angle between \(\mathbf{n}\) and \(\mathbf{a}\). The Poynting vector \(\mathbf{S} = \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\times\mathbf{B}/\mu_0\) points along \(\mathbf{n}\) with magnitude \(|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}|^2/(\mu_0 c) = \varepsilon_0 c|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}|^2\). The power per unit solid angle is \(R^2 S\):

\[ \frac{dP}{d\Omega} = \frac{q^2 a^2}{16\pi^2\varepsilon_0 c^3}\sin^2\Theta \]

This is the doughnut pattern of a dipole: nothing along the acceleration, most at right angles to it. To add it up over all directions, measure \(\Theta\) from \(\mathbf{a}\) as the polar angle of spherical coordinates, with \(\varphi\) the angle round \(\mathbf{a}\). A small patch of the unit sphere is \(d\Omega = \sin\Theta\,d\Theta\,d\varphi\) (a strip at angle \(\Theta\) is a circle of radius \(\sin\Theta\)). Then

\[ \int\sin^2\Theta\,d\Omega = \int_0^{2\pi}d\varphi\int_0^\pi\sin^3\Theta\,d\Theta = 2\pi\cdot\frac43 = \frac{8\pi}{3} \]

using \(\sin^3\Theta = \sin\Theta\,(1 - \cos^2\Theta)\) and the substitution \(u = \cos\Theta\), which turns the second integral into \(\int_{-1}^{1}(1 - u^2)\,du = 4/3\). Multiplying gives Larmor’s formula:

\[ P = \frac{q^2 a^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0 c^3} \]

in watts. Landau and Lifshitz derive it as dipole radiation in The Classical Theory of Fields, section 67; Tong’s Electromagnetism, section 7.2.2, does the same.

A number. An electron of 1 keV kinetic energy circling in a field of 1 T has speed \(\sqrt{2W/m_e} = 1.876\times10^7\) m/s (non-relativistically; the exact value is \(1.873\times10^7\) m/s) and acceleration \(evB/m_e\). Larmor gives \(6.2\times10^{-17}\) W, or 388 eV per second. The e-folding time of its energy (the time to fall by a factor \(e = 2.718\), here 1000 eV divided by 388 eV/s) is 2.6 s, against a revolution period of 36 ps: cyclotron radiation, the name for this slow circling charge’s radiation, is negligible at low energy. Relativity changes this.

Pre-requisite (continued): Lorentz transformations, time dilation, length contraction

Pre-requisite. Chapter 1 gave the Lorentz transformation, time dilation and length contraction, and chapter 8 and chapter 12 the transformation of fields. This section adds what changes for light seen from a moving source: the instantaneous rest frame of an accelerated particle, the approximation \(1 - \beta \approx 1/(2\gamma^2)\), the aberration that folds a source’s light forward into a cone of half-angle \(1/\gamma\), and the Doppler shift by \(2\gamma\) straight ahead. Section 2 works in the instantaneous rest frame, section 4 uses the aberration for the opening angle, section 5 the approximation, and section 11 the Doppler shift and the contracted undulator period. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, volume 2, §4 (the Lorentz transformation), §5 (transformation of velocities, and aberration, equation 5.5) and §48 (the Doppler effect), and Tong, Electromagnetism, §5.1 (a review of special relativity).

The instantaneous rest frame. An accelerated particle has no rest frame in the usual sense: an inertial frame moves at constant velocity, and the particle does not. But at each instant there is an inertial frame moving with the particle’s velocity at that instant. In it the particle is momentarily at rest, and a moment later it is moving slowly. Physics in that frame is the physics of a slow particle: Larmor’s formula holds there, and so does the Coulomb field of a charge at rest. Section 2 does its work in this frame and carries the result back to the laboratory with the Lorentz transformation. A new instantaneous rest frame is used at each instant; none of them accompanies the particle for long.

How close to \(c\). In an electron ring \(\beta\) differs from 1 only in the eighth decimal place, and what matters is that difference. Write

\[ 1 - \beta = \frac{1 - \beta^2}{1 + \beta} = \frac{1}{\gamma^2(1 + \beta)} \approx \frac{1}{2\gamma^2} \]

for \(\gamma \gg 1\). The approximation replaces \(1 + \beta\) by 2, so its relative error is \((1 - \beta)/2\). At 3 GeV (\(\gamma = 5871\)) the exact value and the approximation are both \(1.4507\times10^{-8}\); at 70 MeV (\(\gamma = 137\)), \(2.664\times10^{-5}\). The same step gives the quantity that controls all the beaming of a fast source, for a small angle \(\theta\) between the line of sight and the velocity. With \(\cos\theta \approx 1 - \theta^2/2\) (the Taylor series of the cosine, \(\theta\) in radians), and \(\beta \approx 1\) in the second term,

\[ 1 - \beta\cos\theta \approx (1 - \beta) + \frac{\beta\theta^2}{2} \approx \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\gamma^2} + \theta^2\right) \]

It changes by a factor of two between \(\theta = 0\) and \(\theta = 1/\gamma\). That is why \(1/\gamma\) keeps appearing as the angle that matters.

Light from a moving source: the photon’s four-momentum. A photon of energy \(\varepsilon\) travelling at angle \(\theta\) to the \(x\) axis has momentum of size \(\varepsilon/c\), so its four-momentum is \((\varepsilon/c,\ (\varepsilon/c)\cos\theta,\ (\varepsilon/c)\sin\theta,\ 0)\). Let the source’s rest frame \(K'\) move along \(x\) at \(\beta c\) relative to the laboratory \(K\). The photon has energy \(\varepsilon'\) and angle \(\theta'\) in \(K'\). The four-momentum transforms like \((ct, x, y, z)\), so

\[ \begin{aligned} \varepsilon &= \gamma\left(\varepsilon' + \beta c\,p'_x\right) = \gamma\varepsilon'\left(1 + \beta\cos\theta'\right), \\ \varepsilon\cos\theta &= \gamma\varepsilon'\left(\cos\theta' + \beta\right), \\ \varepsilon\sin\theta &= \varepsilon'\sin\theta' . \end{aligned} \]

The first line is the Doppler shift, the ratio of the last two the aberration.

Aberration. Dividing the third line by the second,

\[ \tan\theta = \frac{\sin\theta'}{\gamma\left(\cos\theta' + \beta\right)} \]

A photon sent sideways in the source’s frame, \(\theta' = 90°\), arrives in the laboratory at \(\tan\theta = 1/(\beta\gamma)\), about \(1/\gamma\). Everything sent into the forward hemisphere in the source’s frame lands inside a cone of half-angle \(1/\gamma\). Even photons sent backwards at \(\theta' = 120°\) come out forwards, at \(1.73/\gamma\). For \(\beta \to 1\) and small \(\theta\) the formula becomes \(\gamma\theta = \sin\theta'/(1 + \cos\theta') = \tan(\theta'/2)\): the whole sphere of directions, from \(\theta' = 0\) to \(180°\), is laid out on the laboratory’s angles as \(\gamma\theta = \tan(\theta'/2)\), from 0 to infinity.

At work. At 3 GeV the cone half-angle is \(1/(\beta\gamma) = 0.1703\) mrad. Light sent at \(\theta' = 30°\), \(60°\), \(90°\), \(120°\) and \(150°\) in the electron’s frame arrives at 0.046, 0.098, 0.170, 0.295 and 0.636 mrad. In the electron’s frame, the dipole pattern of a charge accelerated across its motion sends half its energy into the forward hemisphere by symmetry, so half the photons land within \(1/\gamma\). Those photons are also the ones the Doppler shift below boosts most: weighting by \(1 + \beta\cos\theta'\), 78 per cent of the laboratory energy lands within \(1/\gamma\) (the fraction is \(\tfrac12 + \tfrac{9}{32}\beta\)), and most of the rest not far outside it.

The Doppler shift. (F5, section 14 gave the everyday Doppler effect, for sound and slow sources; this is its relativistic form, with the source’s clock slowed by \(\gamma\) as well.) From the first line, a photon sent at \(\theta'\) in the source’s frame has, in the laboratory, \(\varepsilon = \gamma(1 + \beta\cos\theta')\,\varepsilon'\). Straight ahead, \(\theta' = 0\), the factor is \(\gamma(1 + \beta) \approx 2\gamma\); straight back, \(\gamma(1 - \beta) \approx 1/(2\gamma)\). In terms of the laboratory angle (invert the transformation, \(\varepsilon' = \gamma\varepsilon(1 - \beta\cos\theta)\)):

\[ \varepsilon = \frac{\varepsilon'}{\gamma\left(1 - \beta\cos\theta\right)} \approx \frac{2\gamma\,\varepsilon'}{1 + \gamma^2\theta^2} \]

The forward light is shifted up by \(2\gamma\), and the shift falls to half at \(\theta = 1/\gamma\). The factor \(1 - \beta\cos\theta\) is the squeezing factor \(1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta\) of the previous section: a frequency is one over a time, and the time between wave crests is squeezed by that factor, then stretched by \(\gamma\) because the source’s clock runs slow.

The undulator, seen from the electron. Section 11 needs one application. An undulator is a row of magnets with period \(\lambda_u\) (m), at rest in the laboratory. In the electron’s frame it rushes past at nearly \(c\) and, by length contraction, its period is \(\lambda_u/\gamma\). Its fields reach the electron at a frequency \(\gamma c/\lambda_u\) (in that frame they also form, nearly, an electromagnetic wave), so the electron oscillates and radiates at the wavelength \(\lambda' = \lambda_u/\gamma\). Doppler-shift it to the laboratory:

\[ \lambda = \lambda'\,\gamma\left(1 - \beta\cos\theta\right) \approx \frac{\lambda_u}{2\gamma^2}\left(1 + \gamma^2\theta^2\right) \]

This is the undulator equation of section 11 without its factor \(1 + K^2/2\), which comes from the electron’s wiggle slowing it down and appears when the deflection is not small.

At work. For BioMAX’s undulator, \(\lambda_u = 18\) mm. At 3 GeV the electron sees a period of \(18\ \mathrm{mm}/5871 = 3.07\) µm, infrared, and radiates there. Forward in the laboratory this becomes \(\lambda_u/(2\gamma^2) = 0.261\) nm, 4.75 keV. At \(\theta = 0.5/\gamma\) (85 µrad) it is 0.326 nm, at \(\theta = 1/\gamma\) 0.522 nm. Two Lorentz factors turn a centimetre-scale magnet into an X-ray source.

Pre-requisite (continued): Four-momentum, energy-momentum relation, invariant mass

Pre-requisite. Chapter 1 built the four-momentum \((E/c, \mathbf{p})\) with \(E = \gamma mc^2\) and \(\mathbf{p} = \gamma m\mathbf{v}\), showed that it transforms like \((ct, \mathbf{x})\), and used its invariant square; chapter 8 used it for collisions. This section adds two things section 2 needs: what happens to a burst of energy that carries no momentum, and the four-acceleration, whose invariant square is the acceleration a particle feels in its own frame. With it the next section derives Liénard’s formula in one line. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, volume 2, §7 (four-velocity and four-acceleration, equations 7.3 to 7.5) and §9 (energy and momentum), and Tong, Electromagnetism, §5.1.1 to §5.1.2 (4-vectors and proper time).

Energy emitted with no momentum. Suppose that in some frame \(K'\) a particle sends out energy \(dE'\) in a pattern that is symmetric front to back, so that the total momentum sent out is zero. In the laboratory \(K\), in which \(K'\) moves at \(\beta c\) along \(x\), the four-momentum of that radiation transforms like any other:

\[ dE = \gamma\left(dE' + \beta c\,dp'_x\right) = \gamma\,dE', \qquad dp_x = \gamma\left(dp'_x + \frac{\beta}{c}\,dE'\right) = \frac{\gamma\beta}{c}\,dE' \]

The laboratory sees \(\gamma\) times more energy, and a forward momentum \(\beta\,dE/c\): the radiation goes forwards. Section 2 combines the first result with time dilation, \(dt = \gamma\,dt'\), to show that the radiated power \(dE/dt\) is the same in both frames.

Four-velocity. Proper time \(\tau\) is what a clock carried with the particle reads, \(d\tau = dt/\gamma\). Differentiating the position four-vector \((ct, \mathbf{x})\) with respect to it gives the four-velocity

\[ U = \frac{d}{d\tau}(ct, \mathbf{x}) = \gamma\,(c, \mathbf{v}) \]

It is a four-vector, because \((ct, \mathbf{x})\) is one and \(d\tau\) is the same for every observer. Its square is \(U\cdot U = \gamma^2(c^2 - v^2) = c^2\) in every frame, and \(mU\) is the four-momentum.

Four-acceleration. Differentiate again:

\[ A = \frac{dU}{d\tau} = \gamma\,\frac{d}{dt}\left(\gamma c,\ \gamma\mathbf{v}\right) \]

Since \(U\cdot U\) is constant, \(U\cdot A = 0\): the four-acceleration is always “perpendicular” to the four-velocity. In the instantaneous rest frame \(U = (c, \mathbf{0})\), so \(A\) has no time part there, and its space part is the ordinary acceleration \(\mathbf{a}'\) that the particle feels. Hence its invariant square is

\[ A\cdot A = -a'^2 \]

in every frame. The acceleration felt in the rest frame can therefore be computed from laboratory quantities, by working out \(A\cdot A\) in the laboratory.

In the laboratory. Use \(d\gamma/dt = \gamma^3(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a})/c^2\), which follows from differentiating \(\gamma = (1 - v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}\) with the chain rule: \(d\gamma/dt = -\tfrac12(1 - v^2/c^2)^{-3/2}\cdot(-2\,\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a}/c^2)\), since \(d(v^2)/dt = d(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v})/dt = 2\,\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a}\). Then

\[ A = \gamma\left(\frac{\gamma^3(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a})}{c},\ \ \gamma\mathbf{a} + \frac{\gamma^3(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a})}{c^2}\,\mathbf{v}\right) \]

Square it, time part squared minus space part squared. The space part squared is \(\gamma^2[\gamma^2a^2 + 2\gamma^4(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a})^2/c^2 + \gamma^6(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a})^2v^2/c^4]\), and the time part squared is \(\gamma^2\gamma^6(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a})^2/c^2\). Collecting the \((\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a})^2\) terms with \(\gamma^6(1 - v^2/c^2) = \gamma^4\):

\[ a'^2 = -A\cdot A = \gamma^4\left[a^2 + \gamma^2\,\frac{(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a})^2}{c^2}\right] \]

(A numerical check with random vectors at \(\beta = 0.9\) gives the two sides equal to 15 digits.) The two cases of section 2 come out at once:

  • acceleration across the motion, \(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a} = 0\): \(a' = \gamma^2 a\);
  • acceleration along the motion, \((\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a})^2 = v^2a^2\): \(a'^2 = \gamma^4a^2(1 + \gamma^2\beta^2) = \gamma^6a^2\), so \(a' = \gamma^3a\).

At work. In a MAX IV dipole (\(\rho = 19.7\) m, 3 GeV) the laboratory acceleration is \(c^2/\rho = 4.56\times10^{15}\) m/s\(^2\). The electron itself feels \(\gamma^2 = 3.45\times10^7\) times more, \(1.57\times10^{23}\) m/s\(^2\). The same number comes from the field seen in its frame: the 0.51 T dipole appears as an electric field \(\gamma\beta cB = 8.9\times10^{11}\) V/m, and \(e\mathcal{E}'/m_e = 1.57\times10^{23}\) m/s\(^2\). In a linac at 20 MV/m, by contrast, the electron feels \(e\mathcal{E}/m_e = 3.5\times10^{18}\) m/s\(^2\) at every energy: a longitudinal force is the same in both frames.

Exercise F1. Show that \(U\cdot A = 0\) by differentiating \(U\cdot U = c^2\), and check it in the laboratory for motion on a circle.

Answer

\(d(U\cdot U)/d\tau = 2U\cdot A = 0\). On a circle \(\gamma\) is constant, so \(A = \gamma(0, \gamma\mathbf{a})\) and \(U\cdot A = \gamma c\cdot0 - \gamma\mathbf{v}\cdot\gamma^2\mathbf{a} = -\gamma^3\,\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a} = 0\), since \(\mathbf{a}\) is perpendicular to \(\mathbf{v}\).

\(\gamma = 1957\), \(\rho = 3.3356\times1/1.5 = 2.224\) m, laboratory \(a = c^2/\rho = 4.04\times10^{16}\) m/s\(^2\), felt \(a' = \gamma^2a = 1.55\times10^{23}\) m/s\(^2\). Pre-requisite (continued): Larmor formula and relativistic radiated power

Pre-requisite. Chapter 1 derived Larmor’s formula \(P = q^2a^2/(6\pi\varepsilon_0c^3)\), showed that the radiated power is the same in every inertial frame, and found the \(\gamma^4\) of a charge bent on a circle; chapter 11 took it to the energy lost per turn and to the comparison of bending with pushing. This section adds the general formula for any direction of acceleration, Liénard’s, and shows that the transverse and longitudinal cases are its two ends. Section 2 quotes it. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, volume 2, §73 (radiation from a rapidly moving charge, equations 73.4 to 73.6), and Tong, Electromagnetism, §7.4.4.

From the rest frame to any motion. In the instantaneous rest frame Larmor’s formula holds with the acceleration \(a'\) the particle feels there. The power is the same in the laboratory. And the previous section found \(a'^2\) from laboratory quantities. So

\[ P = \frac{q^2a'^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0c^3} = \frac{q^2\gamma^4}{6\pi\varepsilon_0c^3}\left[a^2 + \gamma^2\,\frac{(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a})^2}{c^2}\right] \]

That is the whole result. Landau and Lifshitz write it in the same way, as \(-A\cdot A\) times a constant, which makes plain that it is an invariant.

Liénard’s form. Take out \(\gamma^6\): the bracket becomes \(a^2/\gamma^2 + (\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a})^2/c^2 = a^2 - \beta^2a^2 + (\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a})^2/c^2\). The identity \(|\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{a}|^2 = v^2a^2 - (\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a})^2\) (from \(|\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{a}| = va\sin\chi\) and \(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a} = va\cos\chi\), with \(\chi\) the angle between them) turns the last two terms into \(-|\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{a}|^2/c^2\):

\[ P = \frac{q^2\gamma^6}{6\pi\varepsilon_0c^3}\left[a^2 - \frac{|\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{a}|^2}{c^2}\right] = \frac{q^2\gamma^6a^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0c^3}\left(1 - \beta^2\sin^2\chi\right) \]

This is the form section 2 quotes. At \(\chi = 0\) (pushing) the bracket is \(a^2\) and \(P \propto \gamma^6a_\parallel^2\); at \(\chi = 90°\) (bending) it is \(a^2/\gamma^2\) and \(P \propto \gamma^4a_\perp^2\). For a general direction split \(\mathbf{a}\) into its parts along and across \(\mathbf{v}\). The cross term vanishes, and the power is the sum of the two cases: \(P \propto \gamma^6a_\parallel^2 + \gamma^4a_\perp^2\).

Why the \(\gamma^6\) is harmless. A longitudinal force \(F_\parallel\) produces only \(a_\parallel = F_\parallel/(\gamma^3m)\), from \(dp = \gamma^3m\,dv\). A transverse force produces \(a_\perp = F_\perp/(\gamma m)\). Written with the forces,

\[ P = \frac{q^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0m^2c^3}\left(F_\parallel^2 + \gamma^2F_\perp^2\right) \]

For equal forces the transverse one radiates \(\gamma^2\) times more. That is the content of section 2’s comparison.

At work: a betatron, where both act. In a betatron the electron is bent by the guide field and pushed along by the induced electric field at the same time. Take 300 MeV on a 1 m orbit with a gain of 7.1 keV a turn, so the induced field is \(7.1\ \mathrm{kV}/(2\pi\ \mathrm{m}) = 1130\) V/m. The bending part radiates 5.5 nW. The pushing part radiates \(2.3\times10^{-25}\) W, \(4\times10^{-17}\) of it. The induced field supplies energy at \(e\mathcal{E}v = 54\) nW, so at this energy a tenth of what the betatron gives is radiated, all of it because of the bending.

Exercise P1. Show that Liénard’s bracket \(a^2 - |\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{a}|^2/c^2\) is never negative for \(v < c\).

Answer

\(|\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{a}| \le va\), so \(|\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{a}|^2/c^2 \le \beta^2a^2 < a^2\). The bracket lies between \(a^2/\gamma^2\) (acceleration across the velocity) and \(a^2\) (along it).

Exercise P2. A 3 GeV electron in MAX IV radiates \(1.41\times10^{-7}\) W in a dipole of radius 19.7 m. In an RF cavity it meets a longitudinal field of about 5 MV/m. How much does it radiate there, and why can the cavities be ignored in counting \(U_0\)?

Answer

\(P_\parallel = e^2F^2/(6\pi\varepsilon_0m_e^2c^3)\) with \(F = e\times5\times10^6\) V/m gives \(4.4\times10^{-18}\) W, \(3\times10^{-11}\) of the dipole power. Radiation comes from bending; the energy lost per turn is counted in the bends alone.

2. The relativistic generalisation: Liénard, 1898

Larmor’s formula holds in any frame where the charge is momentarily at rest. Go to that frame, the instantaneous rest frame, where the charge radiates \(P' = q^2 a'^2/(6\pi\varepsilon_0 c^3)\), \(a'\) being the acceleration there. The dipole pattern is symmetric front to back, so the radiated momentum in that frame is zero. Transform back to the laboratory: an energy \(dE'\) with zero momentum becomes \(dE = \gamma\,dE'\), and the time \(dt'\) becomes \(dt = \gamma\,dt'\). The ratio is unchanged:

\[ P = \frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{dE'}{dt'} = P' \]

The radiated power is a Lorentz invariant. All that remains is to express the rest-frame acceleration \(a'\) through laboratory quantities. Two cases matter.

Acceleration across the velocity (a magnet bending the orbit). In the laboratory a particle with velocity \(v\) crosses a field \(B\); the force is \(qvB\) and, since the field does no work, \(\gamma\) is constant and the acceleration is \(a = qvB/(\gamma m)\). In the rest frame there is an electric field \(\mathcal{E}' = \gamma vB\) across the motion, the force is \(q\gamma vB\), and the acceleration is \(a' = \gamma qvB/m = \gamma^2 a\).

Acceleration along the velocity (a linear accelerator). A force along the motion is the same in both frames, \(F' = F\), so \(a' = F/m\). In the laboratory \(F = dp/dt = \gamma^3 m\,dv/dt\), so \(a = F/(\gamma^3 m)\) and \(a' = \gamma^3 a\).

The general result, which contains both cases, is Liénard’s formula:

\[ P = \frac{q^2\gamma^6}{6\pi\varepsilon_0 c^3}\left[a^2 - \frac{|\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{a}|^2}{c^2}\right] \]

Landau and Lifshitz obtain it from the four-momentum radiated, written with the four-acceleration (section 73, equations 73.3 to 73.7); Tong derives it from the Liénard-Wiechert fields (section 7.4.4).

Written with the force, the two cases differ sharply:

\[ P_\perp = \frac{q^2\gamma^4 a^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0 c^3} = \frac{q^2\gamma^2 F_\perp^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0 m^2 c^3}, \qquad P_\parallel = \frac{q^2 F_\parallel^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0 m^2 c^3} \]

For the same force a transverse push radiates \(\gamma^2\) times more. A linear accelerator barely radiates: the ratio of power radiated to power gained, \(P_\parallel/(F v)\), is about \(\frac{2}{3}r_e F/(m_e c^2)\) for an electron, where \(r_e = e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0m_ec^2) = 2.818\times10^{-15}\) m is the classical electron radius (the distance at which the Coulomb energy of two electrons equals one electron’s rest energy; it is a convenient length, not the electron’s size). To see it, divide \(P_\parallel\) by the power \(Fv\) the force delivers, with \(v \approx c\): \(P_\parallel/(Fc) = e^2F/(6\pi\varepsilon_0m_e^2c^4) = \tfrac23\cdot\tfrac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0m_ec^2}\cdot\tfrac{F}{m_ec^2}\). At 20 MV/m it is \(7.4\times10^{-14}\). Landau and Lifshitz make the same remark: motion along an electric field is the one case in which the radiation does not grow with energy. Bending is what costs.

3. Energy lost per turn

On a circle of radius \(\rho\) at speed \(v = \beta c\) the acceleration is \(a = v^2/\rho = \beta^2c^2/\rho\) (F2, section 4), across the velocity. The transverse formula gives

\[ P = \frac{q^2 c\,\beta^4\gamma^4}{6\pi\varepsilon_0\rho^2} \]

A turn on a circle of radius \(\rho\) takes \(2\pi\rho/(\beta c)\), so the energy lost per turn is

\[ U_0 = P\,\frac{2\pi\rho}{\beta c} = \frac{q^2\beta^3\gamma^4}{3\varepsilon_0\rho} \]

This is the formula quoted in chapter 11 and chapter 15.

Practical form. For \(\beta \approx 1\) write \(\gamma = E/(mc^2)\) and use the classical radius, \(q^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) = r\,mc^2\) (with \(r = r_e\) for the electron, \(r_p\) for the proton). Then \(q^2/(3\varepsilon_0) = \frac{4\pi}{3}r\,mc^2\) and

\[ U_0 = C_\gamma\frac{E^4}{\rho}, \qquad C_\gamma = \frac{4\pi}{3}\frac{r}{(mc^2)^3} \]

For electrons, with \(r_e = 2.818\times10^{-15}\) m and \(m_ec^2 = 0.000511\) GeV, \(C_\gamma = \tfrac{4\pi}{3}\times2.818\times10^{-15}/(0.000511)^3 = 8.846\times10^{-5}\) m/GeV\(^3\) (here \(C_\gamma E^4/\rho\) comes out in GeV when \(E\) is in GeV), which gives the practical form

\[ U_0\,[\mathrm{keV}] = 88.46\,\frac{E^4\,[\mathrm{GeV}]}{\rho\,[\mathrm{m}]} \]

A 1 GeV electron on a 1 m radius loses 88.46 keV a turn. The power drawn from the RF by a beam of current \(I\) is \(U_0 I/e\), so in practical units \(P\,[\mathrm{kW}] = U_0\,[\mathrm{keV}]\times I\,[\mathrm{A}]\).

Real rings have straight sections. The radiation comes only from the bends. Then \(P(s) \propto 1/\rho(s)^2\) and the loss per turn is an integral round the ring:

\[ U_0 = \frac{C_\gamma E^4}{2\pi}\,I_2, \qquad I_2 = \oint\frac{ds}{\rho^2} \]

\(I_2\) (m\(^{-1}\)) is the second synchrotron radiation integral. If all dipoles have the same radius (an isomagnetic ring), the bends together make a full circle of length \(2\pi\rho\) and \(I_2 = 2\pi/\rho\), giving back \(U_0 = C_\gamma E^4/\rho\). The straight sections do not change \(U_0\); they lengthen \(T_0\) and so lower the average power per particle.

The mass. At the same energy and radius, \(U_0 \propto 1/m^4\). A proton radiates \((m_p/m_e)^4 = 1.14\times10^{13}\) times less than an electron. This is why the wall in this chapter is an electron wall; section 13 comes back to protons.

Worked number: MAX IV. The MAX IV 3 GeV ring in Lund (Tavares and colleagues, Journal of Synchrotron Radiation 21, 862, 2014) has circumference 528 m and a bare-lattice loss of 363.8 keV a turn. If its dipoles were all alike, \(\rho = 88.46\times3^4/363.8 = 19.7\) m and \(B = 3.3356\times3/19.7 = 0.51\) T. At its design current of 500 mA the dipoles alone radiate \(363.8\times0.5 = 182\) kW. With insertion devices (the wigglers and undulators of section 11, magnets placed in the straight sections to make more light) the paper’s study of the harmonic cavities (extra cavities at three times the RF frequency, section 12) takes 856 keV a turn (428 kW), and its table for the fully equipped ring gives 1000 keV (500 kW).

4. The opening angle

In the rest frame the radiation has the dipole pattern about the acceleration, which here is radial. Half of it goes into the forward hemisphere. A photon emitted at angle \(\theta'\) to the direction of motion in the rest frame appears in the laboratory at angle \(\theta\) given by the aberration formula

\[ \tan\theta = \frac{\sin\theta'}{\gamma(\cos\theta' + \beta)} \]

A photon emitted sideways, \(\theta' = 90°\), arrives at \(\tan\theta = 1/(\beta\gamma)\). Everything emitted into the forward hemisphere is folded into a cone of half-angle about \(1/\gamma\), and the Doppler shift gives the forward photons most of the energy. The light is a narrow searchlight pointing along the orbit. As the electron goes round, the searchlight sweeps the plane of the ring, so a bending magnet gives a fan of light, wide horizontally and about \(1/\gamma\) high.

(a) In the instantaneous rest frame, Larmor’s doughnut: no radiation along \(\mathbf{a}\), equal amounts forwards and backwards. At MAX IV, \(\gamma = 5871\) and \(1/\gamma = 0.170\) mrad; at 10 m from the source the fan is about 3.4 mm high (twice \(10\ \mathrm{m}\times0.170\) mrad). At LEP’s 104 GeV, \(1/\gamma = 4.9\) µrad. At General Electric’s 70 MeV, \(1/\gamma\) was 7.3 mrad.

5. The critical frequency

An observer in the plane of the orbit sees light only while the searchlight points at them: while the electron’s direction lies within about \(\pm1/\gamma\) of the line of sight. In that time the electron travels an arc of length about \(2\rho/\gamma\), taking \(\Delta t_e = 2\rho/(\gamma\beta c)\). The light it emits at the start of that arc has a head start: it travels the (nearly equal) chord at speed \(c\) while the electron travels the arc at \(\beta c\). The pulse the observer receives is shortened to

\[ \Delta t_{obs} \approx \Delta t_e\,(1-\beta) \approx \frac{2\rho}{\gamma c}\cdot\frac{1}{2\gamma^2} = \frac{\rho}{\gamma^3 c} \]

using \(1 - \beta \approx 1/(2\gamma^2)\). (The arc is longer than the chord by about \(\rho(2/\gamma)^3/24\), which adds \(\rho/(3\gamma^3 c)\), a term of the same order; the estimate is good only to a factor of order one.)

Figure 20.4. Why the pulse is short (not to scale: the angle \(1/\gamma\) is drawn as 12°, and is 0.17 mrad at 3 GeV). The electron runs anticlockwise round a circle of radius \(\rho\) about \(O\). Its light (blue) lies within about \(1/\gamma\) of its direction of motion, so a distant observer on the dashed line is lit only while the electron goes from \(A\) to \(B\), through an angle \(2/\gamma\) and an arc \(2\rho/\gamma\). The light from \(A\) races ahead along almost the same path, and the electron, only slightly slower, chases it; the light from \(B\) arrives just \(\rho/(\gamma^3c)\) after the light from \(A\).

A pulse of duration \(\Delta t\) contains frequencies up to about \(1/\Delta t\) (Fourier’s idea, and its continuation in chapter 8): to build a sharp feature out of sine waves one needs waves that change as fast as the feature does. The angular revolution frequency is \(c/\rho\) for a full ring of radius \(\rho\); the light reaches \(\gamma^3\) times higher. The exact calculation fixes the factor, and the conventional definition is

\[ \omega_c = \frac{3}{2}\frac{\gamma^3 c}{\rho}, \qquad \varepsilon_c = \hbar\omega_c = \frac{3\hbar c\gamma^3}{2\rho} \]

Here \(\hbar = h/2\pi = 1.0546\times10^{-34}\) J s is the reduced Planck constant, and \(\hbar\omega\) is the energy of a photon of angular frequency \(\omega\) (F7, section 4). The practical forms for electrons, with \(E\) in GeV, \(\rho\) in metres and \(B\) in tesla (using \(\rho = 3.3356\,E/B\), the rigidity \(B\rho = p/e\) with \(p \approx E/c\)):

\[ \varepsilon_c\,[\mathrm{keV}] = 2.218\,\frac{E^3}{\rho} = 0.665\,E^2 B, \qquad \lambda_c = \frac{2\pi c}{\omega_c},\quad \lambda_c\,[\mathrm{nm}] = 0.559\,\frac{\rho}{E^3} \]

Worked numbers. MAX IV’s dipoles at \(\rho = 19.7\) m give \(\varepsilon_c = 3.04\) keV, \(\lambda_c = 0.41\) nm: soft X-rays, with useful flux to several times that. LEP at 104 GeV on 3026 m gave 825 keV, gamma rays. The 7 TeV protons of the LHC on 2804 m give 44 eV, ultraviolet.

Pre-requisite (continued): Special functions and integrals (Bessel, Gaussian, error function)

Pre-requisite. Chapter 2 gave the Gaussian integral and its relatives, and chapter 14 met the modified Bessel equation and its growing solution \(I_n\). This section takes up the other solution, the modified Bessel function of the second kind \(K_\nu\), which falls off away from the origin. It shows where \(K_{1/3}\) and \(K_{2/3}\) come from in synchrotron radiation, gives the gamma function, derives how \(K_\nu\) behaves for small and large arguments, and uses one standard integral to find the moments of the synchrotron spectrum. Section 6 uses \(K_{5/3}\) and its integral for the universal spectrum. Taught from S. Y. Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 4, section I.3 (equations 4.43 to 4.55, the Bessel-function integrals and their asymptotic forms), and Landau and Lifshitz, volume 2, §74 (magnetic bremsstrahlung, from the German for “braking radiation”, their name for synchrotron radiation, where the same integrals appear as Airy functions).

Where the functions come from. An observer in the plane of a ring sees a pulse from each electron as its searchlight sweeps past (section 5). To find the pulse’s spectrum one integrates the radiation field times \(e^{i\omega t}\) over the observer’s time, and the phase of that integrand is set by the retarded time. Along a circle of radius \(\rho\), the observer’s time \(t\) and the electron’s time \(t'\) near the moment it points at the observer are related by (Lee, equation 4.43)

\[ \omega\left(t' - \frac{\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{r}(t')}{c}\right) \approx \frac{\omega}{2}\left[\left(\frac{1}{\gamma^2} + \psi^2\right)t' + \frac{c^2t'^3}{3\rho^2}\right] \]

with \(\psi\) the observer’s angle above the orbit plane. The linear term is the squeezing factor \(\tfrac12(1/\gamma^2 + \psi^2)\) of the Lorentz section; the cubic term appears because the direction of motion turns away from the observer. After scaling the time, the integrals to be done are

\[ \int_0^\infty\cos\left[\tfrac32\xi\left(u + \tfrac13u^3\right)\right]du = \frac{K_{1/3}(\xi)}{\sqrt3}, \qquad \int_0^\infty u\sin\left[\tfrac32\xi\left(u + \tfrac13u^3\right)\right]du = \frac{K_{2/3}(\xi)}{\sqrt3} \]

with \(\xi = (\omega/2\omega_c)(1 + \gamma^2\psi^2)^{3/2}\). The first integral comes from the vertical component of the field, the second from the horizontal one. These identities are taken as given here (Lee, equations 4.44 and 4.45); a numerical check, integrating along a ray in the complex plane where the oscillation becomes a decay, reproduces both to twelve digits at \(\xi = 0.5\), 1 and 2. The two functions on the right are the modified Bessel functions of the second kind of orders \(\tfrac13\) and \(\tfrac23\).

The modified Bessel functions. Chapter 14 met the equation

\[ x^2y'' + xy' - \left(x^2 + \nu^2\right)y = 0 \]

for whole-number orders. The order \(\nu\) may be any real number. Of its two independent solutions, \(I_\nu(x)\) grows like \(e^x\) for large \(x\) and \(K_\nu(x)\) decays like \(e^{-x}\); \(K_\nu\) is infinite at \(x = 0\). A form convenient for computing and for approximations is the integral

\[ K_\nu(x) = \int_0^\infty e^{-x\cosh t}\cosh(\nu t)\,dt \qquad (x > 0) \]

(checked numerically against a library routine for \(\nu = \tfrac13\), \(\tfrac23\), \(\tfrac53\) and \(x = 0.1\), 1, 3: agreement to 14 digits). It shows at once that \(K_\nu\) is positive, decreasing and the same for \(\nu\) and \(-\nu\). Neighbouring orders are tied by the recurrence

\[ K_{\nu+1}(x) = K_{\nu-1}(x) + \frac{2\nu}{x}K_\nu(x) \]

which with \(\nu = \tfrac23\) gives \(K_{5/3} = K_{1/3} + \tfrac{4}{3x}K_{2/3}\), the function of section 6. At \(x = 1.3\) both sides are 0.61284.

The gamma function. The small-argument behaviour needs the gamma function,

\[ \Gamma(s) = \int_0^\infty u^{s-1}e^{-u}\,du \qquad (s > 0) \]

Integrating by parts gives \(\Gamma(s + 1) = s\,\Gamma(s)\), and \(\Gamma(1) = 1\), so \(\Gamma(n + 1) = n!\): it extends the factorial to any positive number. One more property, \(\Gamma(s)\Gamma(1 - s) = \pi/\sin(\pi s)\), is used below without proof. Values needed here: \(\Gamma(\tfrac13) = 2.6789\), \(\Gamma(\tfrac23) = 1.3541\), \(\Gamma(\tfrac53) = \tfrac23\Gamma(\tfrac23) = 0.9027\), and \(\Gamma(\tfrac13)\Gamma(\tfrac23) = 2\pi/\sqrt3 = 3.6276\).

Large argument. For large \(x\) the factor \(e^{-x\cosh t}\) kills everything except small \(t\). There \(\cosh t \approx 1 + t^2/2\) and \(\cosh\nu t \approx 1\), so

\[ K_\nu(x) \approx e^{-x}\int_0^\infty e^{-xt^2/2}\,dt = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2x}}\;e^{-x} \]

using the Gaussian integral. The order has dropped out. For \(K_{5/3}\) the ratio of exact to approximate is 2.38 at \(x = 1\), 1.26 at 5 and 1.13 at 10: the approximation is good to tens of per cent once \(x\) is several units, and improves slowly.

Small argument. For small \(x\) the integral reaches out to large \(t\) before \(e^{-x\cosh t}\) cuts it off. There \(\cosh t \approx e^t/2\) and \(\cosh\nu t \approx e^{\nu t}/2\), so with \(u = (x/2)e^t\), \(du = u\,dt\),

\[ K_\nu(x) \approx \frac12\int e^{-(x/2)e^t}e^{\nu t}\,dt = \frac12\left(\frac{2}{x}\right)^\nu\int_{x/2}^\infty u^{\nu-1}e^{-u}\,du \approx \frac12\,\Gamma(\nu)\left(\frac{2}{x}\right)^\nu \]

for \(\nu > 0\). For \(K_{5/3}\) it is good to 0.004 per cent at \(x = 0.01\) and 0.4 per cent at \(x = 0.1\).

The integral of \(K_{5/3}\). Section 6 needs \(\int_x^\infty K_{5/3}(t)\,dt\). It has no closed form, but both its limits follow from the two approximations:

  • small \(x\): \(\int_x^\infty \tfrac12\Gamma(\tfrac53)\,2^{5/3}t^{-5/3}\,dt\) is dominated by its lower end and gives \(\tfrac34\,\Gamma(\tfrac53)\,2^{5/3}\,x^{-2/3} = 2.150\,x^{-2/3}\);
  • large \(x\): \(\int_x^\infty\sqrt{\pi/2t}\,e^{-t}\,dt \approx \sqrt{\pi/2x}\;e^{-x}\), since \(e^{-t}\) falls much faster than \(t^{-1/2}\) changes.

Multiplying by \(\tfrac{9\sqrt3}{8\pi}x\) gives the two limits of the spectrum quoted in section 6: \(S(x) \approx \tfrac{9\sqrt3}{8\pi}\cdot2.150\,x^{1/3} = 1.333\,x^{1/3}\) and \(S(x) \approx \tfrac{9\sqrt3}{8\pi}\sqrt{\pi/2}\,\sqrt{x}\,e^{-x} = 0.777\sqrt{x}\,e^{-x}\). Numerically, \(\int_x^\infty K_{5/3}\,dt = 8.18\) at \(x = 0.1\), 0.651 at \(x = 1\) and 0.0132 at \(x = 4\).

One standard integral. The moments of \(K_\nu\) are known exactly:

\[ \int_0^\infty x^\mu K_\nu(x)\,dx = 2^{\mu-1}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1 + \mu + \nu}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1 + \mu - \nu}{2}\right) \qquad (\mu + 1 > |\nu|) \]

It is quoted here and checked: for \(\nu = \tfrac53\) and \(\mu = 1\), 2, 3 the numerical integrals are 5.23599, 3.22453 and 6.39954, as the formula gives. With it the integrals of the spectrum need only one trick, swapping the order of integration:

\[ \int_0^\infty x^k\left[\int_x^\infty K_{5/3}(t)\,dt\right]dx = \int_0^\infty K_{5/3}(t)\left[\int_0^t x^k\,dx\right]dt = \frac{1}{k + 1}\int_0^\infty t^{k+1}K_{5/3}(t)\,dt \]

(the region is \(0 < x < t\), described either way round).

At work: the spectrum is normalised. With \(k = 1\) the area under \(S\) is \(\tfrac{9\sqrt3}{8\pi}\cdot\tfrac12\cdot2\,\Gamma(\tfrac73)\Gamma(\tfrac23)\). Since \(\Gamma(\tfrac73) = \tfrac43\cdot\tfrac13\,\Gamma(\tfrac13)\) and \(\Gamma(\tfrac13)\Gamma(\tfrac23) = 2\pi/\sqrt3\), this is \(\tfrac{9\sqrt3}{8\pi}\cdot\tfrac49\cdot\tfrac{2\pi}{\sqrt3} = 1\) exactly: the constant \(\tfrac{9\sqrt3}{8\pi}\) in section 6 was chosen so that \(S\) shares out the total power and nothing more.

Pre-requisite (continued): Angular distribution and spectrum of radiation

Pre-requisite. Chapter 8 defined the energy radiated per unit frequency and per unit solid angle, \(d^2W/(d\omega\,d\Omega)\), obtained it from the radiation field with Parseval’s theorem, showed that a fast particle’s light lies within about \(1/\gamma\) of its direction, and turned energy into photons with \(dN = dW/(\hbar\omega)\). This section applies those tools to an electron on a circle. It states Schwinger’s result for the angle and frequency distribution, checks that it integrates to section 6’s universal spectrum, and from the spectrum derives the number of photons, their mean energy and mean square energy, and the flux of a bending magnet in the units beamlines use. Section 6 quotes these numbers, and section 9 builds the beam’s equilibrium on the mean square photon energy. Taught from S. Y. Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 4, sections I.3 (frequency and angular distribution, equations 4.46 to 4.61) and I.4 (quantum fluctuation, equations 4.62 to 4.67), and Landau and Lifshitz, volume 2, §74 (magnetic bremsstrahlung).

The distribution in angle and frequency. For an electron on a circle of radius \(\rho\), observed at an angle \(\psi\) above the orbit plane, the energy radiated per pass per unit angular frequency per unit solid angle is (Lee, equation 4.49; Landau and Lifshitz, §74)

\[ \frac{d^2W}{d\omega\,d\Omega} = \frac{3e^2}{16\pi^3\varepsilon_0c}\,\gamma^2\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_c}\right)^2\left(1 + \gamma^2\psi^2\right)^2\left[K_{2/3}^2(\xi) + \frac{\gamma^2\psi^2}{1 + \gamma^2\psi^2}\,K_{1/3}^2(\xi)\right], \qquad \xi = \frac{\omega}{2\omega_c}\left(1 + \gamma^2\psi^2\right)^{3/2} \]

in J s per steradian, with \(\omega_c = 3\gamma^3c/(2\rho)\) as in section 5. It is the square of the Fourier transform of the radiation field, with the transform done by the two integrals of the previous section. The first term in the bracket is the field polarised in the orbit plane, the second the field perpendicular to it; section 7’s pre-requisite returns to them.

Read off three things.

  • The angle and the frequency are tied together through \(\xi\). Since \(K_\nu(\xi)\) dies as \(e^{-\xi}\), light at frequency \(\omega\) appears only where \(\xi \lesssim 1\), that is \((1 + \gamma^2\psi^2)^{3/2} \lesssim 2\omega_c/\omega\). High frequencies stay within \(1/\gamma\) of the plane; low frequencies spread to angles of order \((1/\gamma)(\omega_c/\omega)^{1/3}\).
  • In the plane, \(\psi = 0\), only the in-plane polarisation remains.
  • The horizontal angle does not appear. Every direction in the orbit plane is equivalent, so a full turn sends the same light into every horizontal angle.

Check: from the angles back to the spectrum. A full turn spreads its light over the \(2\pi\) of horizontal angle, so the energy per turn per unit \(\omega\) per unit vertical angle is \(2\pi\,d^2W/(d\omega\,d\Omega)\). Integrate over \(\psi\) and divide by the time spent bending, \(2\pi\rho/c\), to get the power per unit \(\omega\). Numerically, at 3 GeV on \(\rho = 19.7\) m, this gives \(1.2346\times10^{-26}\) W per rad/s at \(\omega = \omega_c\) and \(1.5508\times10^{-26}\) W per rad/s at \(0.1\,\omega_c\); section 6’s \((P/\omega_c)\,S(\omega/\omega_c)\) gives the same numbers to four or five digits. The rms vertical angle of the light, also computed from the formula, is \(0.57/\gamma\) at \(\omega_c\), \(1.47/\gamma\) at \(0.1\,\omega_c\) and \(3.3/\gamma\) at \(0.01\,\omega_c\): at MAX IV, 0.098, 0.25 and 0.56 mrad.

From power to photons. The power per unit angular frequency is \(dP/d\omega = (P/\omega_c)\,S(\omega/\omega_c)\). Each photon carries \(u = \hbar\omega\), so the number emitted per second per unit \(\omega\) is \((dP/d\omega)/(\hbar\omega)\). Write \(x = \omega/\omega_c\) and integrate:

\[ \dot N = \int_0^\infty\frac{P}{\hbar\omega_c}\,\frac{S(x)}{x}\,dx = \frac{P}{\varepsilon_c}\int_0^\infty\frac{S(x)}{x}\,dx = \frac{15\sqrt3}{8}\,\frac{P}{\varepsilon_c} \]

where \(\varepsilon_c = \hbar\omega_c\) and the integral is exercise K2 of the previous section. The low-frequency end, where \(S \propto x^{1/3}\), gives \(S/x \propto x^{-2/3}\), which is integrable: there are many soft photons, but not infinitely many.

The moments of the photon energy follow in the same way. The total power is \(\dot N\langle u\rangle\), so

\[ \langle u\rangle = \frac{P}{\dot N} = \frac{8}{15\sqrt3}\,\varepsilon_c = 0.3079\,\varepsilon_c \]

and

\[ \dot N\langle u^2\rangle = \int_0^\infty\hbar\omega\,\frac{dP}{d\omega}\,d\omega = P\,\varepsilon_c\int_0^\infty x\,S(x)\,dx = \frac{55\sqrt3}{72}\,P\,\varepsilon_c \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \langle u^2\rangle = \frac{11}{27}\,\varepsilon_c^2 \]

The last integral comes from the standard integral with \(k = 2\): \(\tfrac{9\sqrt3}{8\pi}\cdot\tfrac13\cdot4\,\Gamma(\tfrac{17}{6})\Gamma(\tfrac76) = \tfrac{55\sqrt3}{72} = 1.3231\), confirmed numerically. The rms photon energy, \(\sqrt{11/27}\,\varepsilon_c = 0.638\,\varepsilon_c\), is twice the mean: the spectrum has a long tail of hard photons.

Photons per turn. Multiply \(\dot N\) by the time spent bending in one turn, \(2\pi\rho/c\), and use \(P = e^2c\gamma^4/(6\pi\varepsilon_0\rho^2)\) and \(\varepsilon_c = 3\hbar c\gamma^3/(2\rho)\):

\[ N_{turn} = \frac{15\sqrt3}{8}\cdot\frac{e^2c\gamma^4}{6\pi\varepsilon_0\rho^2}\cdot\frac{2\rho}{3\hbar c\gamma^3}\cdot\frac{2\pi\rho}{c} = \frac{5\pi}{\sqrt3}\,\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c}\,\gamma = \frac{5\pi}{\sqrt3}\,\alpha\gamma \]

with \(\alpha = e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c) = 1/137.036\). The radius has cancelled: the number of photons per turn depends only on the energy. The product \(\dot N\langle u^2\rangle\), which section 9 needs, becomes with \(\varepsilon_c = 3\hbar c\gamma^3/(2\rho)\) and \(E = \gamma m_ec^2\):

\[ \dot N\langle u^2\rangle = \frac{55\sqrt3}{72}\cdot\frac{3\hbar c\gamma^3}{2\rho}\,P = 2\,C_q\gamma^2\,\frac{E\,P}{\rho}, \qquad C_q = \frac{55}{32\sqrt3}\,\frac{\hbar}{m_ec} = 3.832\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{m} \]

which is section 9’s starting point.

At work: MAX IV. At 3 GeV, \(\gamma = 5871\) and \(\varepsilon_c = 3.040\) keV (on \(\rho = 19.7\) m). Each electron emits \(\tfrac{5\pi}{\sqrt3}\alpha\gamma = 388.5\) photons a turn, of mean energy \(0.3079\times3.040 = 0.936\) keV, and \(388.5\times0.936\) keV \(= 363.7\) keV is the loss per turn. An electron emits \(2.2\times10^8\) photons a second.

The flux of a bending magnet. Beamlines count photons per second, per milliradian of horizontal angle, in a bandwidth of 0.1 per cent. A current \(I\) (A) holds electrons that together make \(I/e\) electron-turns per second. One turn sends a fraction \(\Delta\theta/(2\pi)\) of its light into a horizontal angle \(\Delta\theta\), and in a band \(\Delta\omega = 10^{-3}\omega\) it holds \(T_{bend}\,(dP/d\omega)\,\Delta\omega/(\hbar\omega)\) photons, where \(T_{bend} = 2\pi\rho/c\). With \(T_{bend}P = U_0\) and \(U_0/\varepsilon_c = \tfrac{8\pi}{9}\alpha\gamma\) (which you can check from the formulas above), this is

\[ \frac{d\dot N}{d\theta} = \frac{I}{e}\cdot\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{8\pi}{9}\,\alpha\gamma\cdot10^{-3}\,S\!\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_c}\right) = \frac{\sqrt3}{2\pi}\,\alpha\gamma\,\frac{I}{e}\,10^{-3}\,y\int_y^\infty K_{5/3}(t)\,dt \]

per radian, with \(y = \omega/\omega_c\). Per milliradian, for \(E\) in GeV and \(I\) in amperes, it is \(2.46\times10^{13}\,E\,I\,y\int_y^\infty K_{5/3}\,dt\) photons s\(^{-1}\) mrad\(^{-1}\) (0.1 per cent bandwidth)\(^{-1}\), Lee’s equation 4.61.

At work. MAX IV’s dipoles at 500 mA give, at the critical energy (3.04 keV, where \(y\int_y^\infty K_{5/3} = 0.651\)), \(2.46\times10^{13}\times3\times0.5\times0.651 = 2.4\times10^{13}\) photons per second per milliradian per 0.1 per cent bandwidth; \(3.0\times10^{13}\) at 0.30 keV; \(1.9\times10^{12}\) at 12.2 keV (\(4\varepsilon_c\)).

6. The spectrum: Schwinger’s universal function

The pulse shape, and so the spectrum, follow from the Liénard-Wiechert fields along a circular orbit. The result, which Landau and Lifshitz derive in section 74 (Synchrotron radiation (magnetic bremsstrahlung), equations 74.13 to 74.18) and Schwinger gave in 1949, is quoted here: the power per unit angular frequency, integrated over angle, is

\[ \frac{dP}{d\omega} = \frac{P}{\omega_c}\,S\!\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_c}\right), \qquad S(x) = \frac{9\sqrt{3}}{8\pi}\,x\int_x^\infty K_{5/3}(t)\,dt \]

where \(K_{5/3}\) is a modified Bessel function of the second kind. The shape \(S(x)\) is the same for every ring and every energy; only the scale \(\omega_c\) and the total \(P\) change. Its properties, computed numerically:

  • \(\int_0^\infty S(x)\,dx = 1\), as it must, and half the power lies below \(\omega_c\) and half above (0.500005 below: not an exact identity, but half to five decimal places). This is a convenient meaning for \(\omega_c\).
  • The peak of \(S\) is at \(x = 0.29\), with \(S = 0.57\).
  • At low frequency \(S(x) \approx 1.333\,x^{1/3}\): the spectrum falls slowly towards the infrared, so a ring is a good infrared source too.
  • At high frequency \(S(x) \approx 0.777\,\sqrt{x}\,e^{-x}\): it is cut off exponentially. Only 3.6 per cent of the power lies above \(4\omega_c\), and 0.012 per cent above \(10\omega_c\).

Figure 20.5. The universal spectrum \(S(x)\), \(x = \omega/\omega_c\), on logarithmic axes, computed from its definition (the integral of \(K_{5/3}\) done numerically). It rises slowly as \(1.333\,x^{1/3}\) towards the infrared (blue dashes), peaks at \(x = 0.29\), and beyond \(\omega_c\) falls as \(0.777\sqrt{x}\,e^{-x}\) (black dashes), losing a factor of about \(e\) for each further \(\omega_c\). Half the power lies on each side of \(\omega_c\). At MAX IV \(\omega_c\) corresponds to 3.04 keV, so the axis runs from 3 eV to 30 keV.

Photons. The light arrives as photons. Dividing the spectrum by \(\hbar\omega\) and integrating, the mean photon energy is \(\langle u\rangle = \frac{8}{15\sqrt{3}}\varepsilon_c = 0.308\,\varepsilon_c\), the mean square is \(\langle u^2\rangle = \frac{11}{27}\varepsilon_c^2\), and the number emitted per turn is \(\frac{5\pi}{\sqrt{3}}\alpha\gamma = 0.0662\,\gamma\) (with \(\alpha = e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c) = 1/137.036\) the fine-structure constant, a pure number that measures the strength of the electromagnetic force). For MAX IV: 389 photons a turn, of mean energy 0.94 keV, and \(389\times0.94\) keV is indeed the 363.8 keV loss. The loss is lumpy, a few hundred random photons a turn, and that randomness is what sets the beam’s size (section 9).

Limits. The classical result needs \(\varepsilon_c \ll E\), which holds in every accelerator so far (LEP: 825 keV against 104 GeV). It assumes \(\gamma \gg 1\), an orbit much longer than the formation length \(\rho/\gamma\) (the length of arc, of order \(2\rho/\gamma\), over which one pulse is made, section 5), and a uniform field over that length. Where the field changes within \(\rho/\gamma\) (dipole ends, short bends) the spectrum is modified.

Pre-requisite (continued): Maxwell’s equations and the wave equation

Pre-requisite. Chapter 13 derived the wave equation from Maxwell’s equations, and chapter 14 used them in integral form. This section adds the polarisation of a light wave: the direction in which its electric field points and how that direction moves during a period. Two components of the field across the direction of travel, in phase, give linear polarisation; a quarter period apart, elliptical or circular. Section 7 uses this to describe the polarisation of synchrotron light above, in and below the orbit plane. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, volume 2, §48 (monochromatic plane waves, the polarisation ellipse) and §50 (partially polarised light), and Tong, Electromagnetism, §4.3.2 (polarisation).

Two components. A plane wave of angular frequency \(\omega\) travelling along \(z\) has its electric field in the \(xy\) plane: Maxwell’s equations in empty space force \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{z}} = 0\). At a fixed point, each of the two components oscillates at \(\omega\) with its own amplitude and phase. Choose the time origin so that the \(x\) component has phase zero:

\[ \mathcal{E}_x = \mathcal{E}_{0x}\cos\omega t, \qquad \mathcal{E}_y = \mathcal{E}_{0y}\cos(\omega t - \delta) \]

with amplitudes \(\mathcal{E}_{0x}\), \(\mathcal{E}_{0y}\) (V/m) and a phase difference \(\delta\). The tip of \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) traces a curve in the \(xy\) plane, once every period. Eliminating \(t\) (write \(\cos(\omega t - \delta) = \cos\omega t\cos\delta + \sin\omega t\sin\delta\), solve for \(\sin\omega t\), and use \(\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1\)):

\[ \left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_x}{\mathcal{E}_{0x}}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_y}{\mathcal{E}_{0y}}\right)^2 - 2\,\frac{\mathcal{E}_x\mathcal{E}_y}{\mathcal{E}_{0x}\mathcal{E}_{0y}}\cos\delta = \sin^2\delta \]

an ellipse, the polarisation ellipse (Landau and Lifshitz, equation 48.10).

  • \(\delta = 0\) or \(\pi\): the ellipse collapses to a line, \(\mathcal{E}_y/\mathcal{E}_x = \pm \mathcal{E}_{0y}/\mathcal{E}_{0x}\). The field swings back and forth along a fixed direction: linear polarisation.
  • \(\delta = \pm\pi/2\): the ellipse has its axes along \(x\) and \(y\), \((\mathcal{E}_x/\mathcal{E}_{0x})^2 + (\mathcal{E}_y/\mathcal{E}_{0y})^2 = 1\). One component peaks when the other is zero: two oscillations a quarter period apart make elliptical polarisation.
  • \(\delta = \pm\pi/2\) and \(\mathcal{E}_{0x} = \mathcal{E}_{0y}\): a circle. The field keeps its size and turns at \(\omega\): circular polarisation.

The sign of \(\delta\) fixes the sense of rotation. With \(\delta = +\pi/2\), at \(\omega t = 0\) the field points along \(+x\) and a quarter period later along \(+y\): it turns from \(x\) to \(y\). Which sense is called “right” differs between optics and particle physics, so it is safest to state the rotation, or the photon’s helicity, explicitly.

Measuring it. The energy flux is \(\varepsilon_0c\) times the mean of \(\mathcal{E}_x^2 + \mathcal{E}_y^2\), so the two components carry \(\tfrac12\varepsilon_0cE_{0x}^2\) and \(\tfrac12\varepsilon_0cE_{0y}^2\): the power splits between them. Two numbers describe the shape. The degree of linear polarisation and of circular polarisation are

\[ P_l = \frac{\mathcal{E}_{0x}^2 - \mathcal{E}_{0y}^2}{\mathcal{E}_{0x}^2 + \mathcal{E}_{0y}^2}, \qquad P_c = \frac{2\mathcal{E}_{0x}\mathcal{E}_{0y}\sin\delta}{\mathcal{E}_{0x}^2 + \mathcal{E}_{0y}^2} \]

(\(P_l\) is written for an ellipse with axes along \(x\) and \(y\)). For a single wave of fixed amplitudes and phase, \(P_l^2 + P_c^2 = 1\) when \(\delta = \pm\pi/2\). Light made of many waves whose shapes differ is partially polarised, and then \(P_l^2 + P_c^2 < 1\) (Landau and Lifshitz, §50).

Adding sources. Many electrons in a bunch emit with random phases, so their powers add, not their fields (the pre-requisite before section 11). But each electron seen from the same direction sends light of the same polarisation shape, so the sum keeps that shape. A beam’s synchrotron light is therefore polarised, not a jumble, as long as one looks in a well-defined direction.

Synchrotron light. Section 7’s statements come from the bracket of the angular distribution: the field in the orbit plane is proportional to \(K_{2/3}(\xi)\), the field perpendicular to it to \(\gamma\psi\,K_{1/3}(\xi)/\sqrt{1 + \gamma^2\psi^2}\), and the two are a quarter period apart (one comes from a sine integral, the other from a cosine). So:

  • in the plane, \(\psi = 0\), the vertical part vanishes and the light is linearly polarised in the plane;
  • above it the light is elliptical, with \(\delta = \pm\pi/2\);
  • below it \(\psi\) changes sign, the vertical field changes sign, and the light turns the other way.

At work. At \(\omega = \omega_c\) the degree of circular polarisation is 0.39 at \(\gamma\psi = 0.25\), 0.67 at \(\gamma\psi = 0.5\) and 0.91 at \(\gamma\psi = 1\). The price is intensity: at \(\gamma\psi = 1\) only 28 per cent of the on-axis flux remains, and at \(\gamma\psi = 2\) almost none. At \(0.1\,\omega_c\), where the light spreads wider, \(P_c = 0.77\) at \(\gamma\psi = 1\) with the flux still 9 per cent above its on-axis value. Integrated over all angles and frequencies, the in-plane component carries \(\tfrac78\) of the power and the perpendicular one \(\tfrac18\) (Lee, equation 4.53; numerically 0.8749 and 0.1251). At a single frequency the perpendicular share is 24 per cent at \(0.01\,\omega_c\), 12 per cent at \(\omega_c\) and 6 per cent at \(4\,\omega_c\).

7. Polarisation

In the plane of the orbit the electric field of the light lies in that plane, along the acceleration: the light is linearly polarised horizontally. Above and below the plane a vertical component appears, a quarter period out of phase, so the light is elliptically polarised, with opposite handedness above and below. Integrated over angle and frequency, seven-eighths of the power is in the horizontal (σ) polarisation and one-eighth in the vertical (π). The vertical component is relatively stronger at long wavelengths, where the cone is wider. Experimenters who want circular light take it from just above or below the plane, or use an undulator built for it.

Pre-requisite (continued): Matrices, determinants, eigenvalues

Pre-requisite. Chapter 8 showed that the determinant of a transfer matrix is the factor by which it multiplies areas in phase space, and chapter 14 added eigenvalues and eigenvectors. This section joins the two for a ring with damping: the determinant is the product of the eigenvalues, the eigenvalues of a damped oscillation shrink by \(e^{-T_0/\tau}\) a turn, and so the determinant of the one-turn matrix fixes the sum of all the damping rates. A rule for the determinant of a matrix close to the unit matrix, \(\det(I + \epsilon A) \approx 1 + \epsilon\,\mathrm{tr}\,A\), then shows why the sum cannot depend on the lattice. Section 8 uses this for Robinson’s theorem. Taught from S. Y. Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, section II.1 (the transfer matrix and the stability of betatron motion) and chapter 4, section II.2 (damping of betatron motion), and Chao and Tigner’s Handbook, section 2.2.6 (orbital eigenanalysis for electron storage rings).

Determinant and eigenvalues. For a \(2\times2\) matrix \(M\) with eigenvalues \(\lambda_1\), \(\lambda_2\), the characteristic polynomial is \(\det(M - \lambda I) = \lambda^2 - (\mathrm{tr}\,M)\lambda + \det M = (\lambda - \lambda_1)(\lambda - \lambda_2)\). Comparing coefficients, \(\det M = \lambda_1\lambda_2\) and \(\mathrm{tr}\,M = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2\). The same holds for any size: the determinant is the product of all the eigenvalues, the trace their sum.

A damped oscillation, one turn at a time. Without damping, the one-turn matrix of a stable oscillation has eigenvalues \(e^{\pm i\mu}\), with \(\mu\) the phase advance per turn (\(2\pi\) times the tune), and determinant 1. With a little damping each eigenvector also shrinks every turn, by the factor \(e^{-T_0/\tau}\), where \(T_0\) is the revolution time and \(\tau\) the damping time of the amplitude. The eigenvalues become \(e^{-T_0/\tau \pm i\mu}\), and

\[ \det M = e^{-2T_0/\tau} \]

The area of the phase-space ellipse shrinks by this factor each turn, which is the statement that the emittance, an area, damps at twice the rate of the amplitude. Reading it backwards, \(T_0/\tau = -\tfrac12\ln\det M \approx \tfrac12(1 - \det M)\) for weak damping.

At work: vertical damping in MAX IV. Let the one-turn vertical matrix be a betatron rotation \(R\) (determinant 1) followed by the effect of radiation and RF: the angle \(y'\) is multiplied by \(1 - U_0/E\) and the position \(y\) unchanged (section 8). That second matrix is \(D = \mathrm{diag}(1,\ 1 - U_0/E)\), with determinant \(1 - U_0/E\). So \(\det(DR) = 1 - U_0/E\), both eigenvalues have modulus \(\sqrt{1 - U_0/E}\), and

\[ \frac{T_0}{\tau_y} = \frac{U_0}{2E}, \qquad \tau_y = \frac{2E\,T_0}{U_0} \]

With \(U_0/E = 3.638\times10^5/3\times10^9 = 1.213\times10^{-4}\), \(\tau_y = 16\,490\) turns, 29.0 ms, as section 8 finds by averaging. (A direct numerical calculation of the eigenvalues of \(DR\), at a tune of 0.28, gives the same moduli, \(\sqrt{1 - U_0/E}\), to all digits.)

A matrix close to the unit matrix. Over a short length \(ds\) of ring the transfer matrix is \(I + A(s)\,ds\), where \(A\) holds the local focusing and, with radiation, the local damping. The determinant of such a matrix is

\[ \det(I + \epsilon A) = 1 + \epsilon\,\mathrm{tr}\,A + O(\epsilon^2) \]

For \(2\times2\): \((1 + \epsilon a_{11})(1 + \epsilon a_{22}) - \epsilon^2a_{12}a_{21} = 1 + \epsilon(a_{11} + a_{22}) + O(\epsilon^2)\). For any size, expanding the determinant as a sum of products, every product other than that of the diagonal elements contains at least two off-diagonal factors, each of order \(\epsilon\). Only the diagonal survives at first order. (A \(3\times3\) example with \(\epsilon = 10^{-3}\) gives 0.9996006 against \(1 + \epsilon\,\mathrm{tr}\,A = 0.9996\).)

The off-diagonal elements do not count. Focusing, the coupling of planes, and dispersion that mixes energy into position all sit off the diagonal, so they change no determinant to first order. Only terms that shrink a coordinate in proportion to itself, on the diagonal, change areas.

The sum of the damping rates. Now the whole ring, in six dimensions: \((x, x', y, y', z, \delta)\). Its one-turn matrix is the product of all the short pieces, and the determinant of a product is the product of determinants:

\[ \det M_{turn} = \prod_{\text{pieces}}\left(1 + \mathrm{tr}\,A\;ds\right) \approx \exp\left(\oint\mathrm{tr}\,A\;ds\right) \]

The three oscillations (horizontal, vertical, longitudinal) have eigenvalue pairs \(e^{-T_0/\tau_i \pm i\mu_i}\), so \(\det M_{turn} = e^{-2T_0\sum_i1/\tau_i}\). Hence

\[ \sum_{i = x, y, E}\frac{1}{\tau_i} = -\frac{1}{2T_0}\oint\mathrm{tr}\,A\;ds \]

The sum of the damping rates is fixed by the diagonal of the local matrices alone, whatever the focusing and dispersion do. What is on the diagonal? Radiation removes a fraction \(u/E\) of each momentum component, and the RF restores only the longitudinal one, so \(x'\) and \(y'\) each shrink by \(U_0/E\) per turn. In the longitudinal plane the energy deviation \(\delta\) decays because a more energetic electron radiates more, \(dU/dE = 2U_0/E\) at fixed field. So the one-turn determinant is

\[ \det M_{turn} = \left(1 - \frac{U_0}{E}\right)\left(1 - \frac{U_0}{E}\right)\left(1 - \frac{2U_0}{E}\right) \approx 1 - \frac{4U_0}{E}, \qquad \sum_i\frac{1}{\tau_i} = \frac{4U_0}{2E\,T_0} \]

which is \(J_x + J_y + J_E = 4\) in section 8’s notation. Dispersion in the dipoles (section 8’s \(\mathcal{D}\)) moves damping from one plane to another through off-diagonal coupling, but cannot change the total. This is the structure of Robinson’s argument; his paper does the bookkeeping with the full six-dimensional matrix, including the dependence of the radiation on position in a gradient dipole.

At work. For MAX IV, \(4U_0/E = 4.85\times10^{-4}\) per turn, so the three rates add to \(4.85\times10^{-4}/(2T_0) = 138\) s\(^{-1}\). With \(J_x = 1.847\), \(J_y = 1\), \(J_E = 1.153\): \(1/15.7 + 1/29.0 + 1/25.2\) ms\(^{-1}\) \(= 0.0637 + 0.0344 + 0.0397 = 0.138\) ms\(^{-1}\).

Exercise D1. Check that \(\det(I + \epsilon A) \approx 1 + \epsilon\,\mathrm{tr}\,A\) for \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -k & 0 \end{pmatrix}\), a short piece of focusing, and say what this means for phase-space area.

Answer

\(\det\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \epsilon \\ -k\epsilon & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 1 + k\epsilon^2\), and \(\mathrm{tr}\,A = 0\). To first order the determinant is 1: focusing preserves area, as Liouville’s theorem requires. (The exact transfer matrix of a short quadrupole has determinant exactly 1; the \(\epsilon^2\) here is an artefact of truncating it.)

Exercise D2. A ring has \(U_0/E = 10^{-3}\) per turn. If a gradient in the dipoles makes \(J_x = 2.5\), what are \(J_E\) and the three damping times in turns?

Answer

\(J_E = 4 - 2.5 - 1 = 0.5\). With \(\tau_i = 2E/(J_iU_0)\) turns: \(\tau_x = 800\), \(\tau_y = 2000\), \(\tau_E = 4000\) turns. Faster horizontal damping is bought with slower longitudinal damping; pushing \(J_x\) above 3 would make \(J_E\) negative and the synchrotron motion unstable.

8. Radiation damping

Now what the radiation does to the beam. An electron ring is far above transition (\(\gamma \gg \gamma_t\): a more energetic electron takes longer to go round, because its longer path matters more than its negligible gain in speed), and in it each electron oscillates about the closed orbit transversely (betatron oscillations, chapter 16) and in energy and phase (synchrotron oscillations, chapter 19). Without radiation, Liouville’s theorem keeps the phase-space area of these oscillations fixed. Radiation is not Hamiltonian (it is not the motion in fixed fields that Liouville’s theorem covers, because the photons carry momentum away): it is a friction, and it shrinks them.

Vertical: the simplest case. A relativistic electron emits its photons within \(1/\gamma\) of its direction of motion, so on average each photon carries momentum exactly along the electron’s momentum. The momentum loses a fraction \(u/E\) of all its components; the direction does not change. If the electron moves at a small angle \(y'\) to the orbit, its vertical momentum \(p y'\) shrinks with the total. The RF cavity then puts the energy back, but its field is along the axis: it restores only the longitudinal momentum. After a turn the electron has its energy back and its vertical angle reduced:

\[ y' \to y'\left(1 - \frac{U_0}{E}\right) \]

What does this do to the amplitude? The Courant-Snyder invariant (chapter 16, section 7) is \(A^2 = \gamma_y y^2 + 2\alpha_y yy' + \beta_y y'^2\): with the optical functions \(\alpha_y\), \(\beta_y\) (m) and \(\gamma_y = (1 + \alpha_y^2)/\beta_y\) of the lattice at that point, this combination stays the same turn after turn while nothing disturbs the motion, and \(A\) (m\(^{1/2}\)) measures the size of the oscillation, which reaches \(A\sqrt{\beta_y}\) at most. A change \(\Delta y' = -y'U_0/E\) changes it by \(\Delta(A^2) = -2(U_0/E)(\alpha_y yy' + \beta_y y'^2)\). Averaged over the phase of the oscillation, \(\langle\alpha_y yy' + \beta_y y'^2\rangle = A^2/2\) (exercise 9 at the end of the chapter does this average). So \(\Delta(A^2) = -(U_0/E)A^2\) per turn, the amplitude \(A\) falls by the fraction \(U_0/(2E)\) per turn, and

\[ \tau_y = \frac{2E\,T_0}{U_0} \]

is the e-folding time of the amplitude (the emittance, which goes as \(A^2\), falls twice as fast). The damping is set by how often the beam’s energy is replaced: \(E/U_0\) turns, times two.

Longitudinal. Suppose an electron has energy \(E + \Delta E\), a relative energy deviation \(\delta = \Delta E/E\). It radiates more: \(P \propto \gamma^4/\rho^2\) (section 3), and in a fixed field \(\rho = p/(eB) \propto E/B\), so \(P \propto E^4B^2/E^2 = E^2B^2\). Over the same path the energy lost goes as \(E^2\) too, and \(dU/dE = 2U_0/E\) from this effect alone. Its excess energy decays. With the RF restoring force this makes the synchrotron oscillation, of angular frequency \(\Omega_s\), a damped oscillator, \(\ddot\delta + 2\alpha_E\dot\delta + \Omega_s^2\delta = 0\), whose amplitude falls as \(e^{-\alpha_Et}\), with \(\alpha_E = (dU/dE)/(2T_0)\) (the extra loss \((dU/dE)\,\Delta E\) each turn of \(T_0\) shrinks the energy deviation at the rate \((dU/dE)/T_0\); it acts on only one of the oscillation’s two coordinates, so, as for \(y'\) above, the amplitude falls at half that rate) (Wolski derives this from the longitudinal equations of motion, his equations 72 to 88). With \(dU/dE = 2U_0/E\), \(\tau_E = 1/\alpha_E = ET_0/U_0\), half the vertical time.

Horizontal, and the dispersion term. In the horizontal plane the same reasoning gives the same damping, but there is an extra effect. An electron that loses energy \(u\) at a point of dispersion \(D_x\) (m; chapter 16, section 10: the closed orbit of a particle with relative energy deviation \(\delta\) lies at \(x = D_x\delta\)) has its closed orbit moved by \(-D_x u/E\); its betatron coordinate, measured from that new orbit, jumps by the opposite amount. Averaged over many emissions this couples horizontal and longitudinal motion. And an electron with energy deviation \(\delta\) travels at \(x = D_x\delta\), where in a dipole with a gradient it sees a different field and a different path length, so it radiates a different amount. Both effects are summed by the fourth synchrotron radiation integral (Sands, 1970; Wolski, equations 57 to 66, 87 and 122):

\[ \mathcal{D} = \frac{I_4}{I_2}, \qquad I_4 = \oint\frac{D_x}{\rho}\left(\frac{1}{\rho^2} + 2k\right)ds \]

with \(k\) the normalised quadrupole strength inside the dipole (m\(^{-2}\); the field gradient divided by the rigidity, \(k = (\partial B_y/\partial x)/(B\rho)\), as in chapter 16, section 2). \(I_4\), like \(I_2\), is fixed by the lattice: it is computed once the optics are known. Its sign carries the physics. An electron with more energy than the design sits outward, where \(D_x > 0\). If the field is weaker there, as in a dipole whose gradient focuses vertically (\(k < 0\) here), it radiates less than its extra energy alone would make it, so the longitudinal damping falls; when \(2|k| > 1/\rho^2\), \(I_4\) is negative, and Robinson’s theorem below hands the lost damping to the horizontal plane. The damping partition numbers are then

\[ J_x = 1 - \mathcal{D}, \qquad J_y = 1, \qquad J_E = 2 + \mathcal{D} \]

and all three damping times take one form:

\[ \tau_i = \frac{2E\,T_0}{J_i\,U_0}, \qquad i = x, y, E \]

Robinson’s theorem. Add the partition numbers: \(J_x + J_y + J_E = 4\), whatever the lattice. Kenneth Robinson proved in 1958 (Physical Review 111, 373) that the sum of the three damping rates is fixed, independent of the focusing structure and of how the RF is arranged, by evaluating the determinant of the transfer matrix of a short piece of orbit with radiation included. The total damping, \(\sum 1/\tau_i = 4U_0/(2ET_0)\), can be moved between planes but not increased. In a ring of separated-function magnets (dipoles with no gradient), \(I_4\) is small, \(\mathcal{D} \approx 0\), and \(J_x \approx 1\), \(J_E \approx 2\). A gradient in the dipoles that focuses vertically (\(k < 0\) in the convention above) makes \(\mathcal{D}\) negative, so it raises \(J_x\) (faster horizontal damping, smaller emittance, section 9) at the price of a smaller \(J_E\) (larger energy spread). If \(J_x\) is pushed below zero the horizontal motion is anti-damped, so the gradients in an electron ring’s dipoles are a choice made with the partition numbers in view.

Worked numbers: MAX IV. \(E = 3\) GeV, \(T_0 = 528\ \mathrm{m}/c = 1.761\) µs, \(U_0 = 363.8\) keV and, from the paper, \(J_x = 1.847\), so \(J_E = 4 - 1.847 - 1 = 1.153\). Then

\[ \tau_x = 15.7\ \mathrm{ms},\quad \tau_y = 29.0\ \mathrm{ms},\quad \tau_E = 25.2\ \mathrm{ms} \]

about 8,900, 16,500 and 14,300 turns. MAX IV’s dipoles all carry a vertically focusing gradient, which is why \(J_x\) is nearly two. With all insertion devices closed (their magnet jaws brought to the smallest gap, so their field is strongest) \(U_0\) more than doubles and the damping is correspondingly faster.

Adiabatic damping is a different thing. In a linear accelerator or a ramping synchrotron, \(y'\) also shrinks as \(p\) grows, but there the energy is never taken away again, so the normalised emittance \(\beta\gamma\varepsilon\) stays constant (chapter 16). Radiation damping reduces the normalised emittance itself, at constant energy. It is the reason an electron beam forgets where it was injected: after a few damping times, nothing of its injected emittance remains.

Pre-requisite (continued): Diffusion and Fokker-Planck

Pre-requisite. Chapter 3 showed that in a random walk the mean square displacement grows in proportion to the number of steps, \(\langle R^2\rangle = N\langle\ell^2\rangle\), and announced that random kicks against a steady restoring pull lead to an equilibrium. This section works that equilibrium out for an oscillator: random kicks make the mean square amplitude grow at a steady rate, damping removes it in proportion to itself, and the two balance at \(\langle A^2\rangle = D\tau/2\), approached exponentially. Section 9 uses it, with the photons of section 6 as the kicks and radiation damping as the pull, to find the natural energy spread and emittance. Taught from S. Y. Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 4, sections II.4 (radiation excitation and equilibrium energy spread) and II.5 (the radial bunch width), and Tong, Fluid Mechanics, chapter 7 (the random walk, in the chapter’s opening pages, and §7.2 and §7.3, the Langevin and Fokker-Planck equations, where friction against random kicks settles into an equilibrium distribution).

A kicked oscillator. Take an oscillation \(x = A\cos\phi\), \(x'/\Omega = -A\sin\phi\), with \(\Omega\) its angular frequency: in the scaled phase plane \((x, x'/\Omega)\) the particle runs round a circle of radius \(A\). Now let it receive, at random moments, small kicks \(\Delta\) to one coordinate, say \(x\), each with zero mean. A kick moves the point from \((x, \cdot)\) to \((x + \Delta, \cdot)\), so

\[ A^2 \to (x + \Delta)^2 + (x'/\Omega)^2 = A^2 + 2x\Delta + \Delta^2 \]

Average over many kicks. The phase at which a kick arrives is unrelated to its sign, so \(\langle x\Delta\rangle = 0\), and each kick adds \(\langle\Delta^2\rangle\) to \(\langle A^2\rangle\) on average. With \(\dot N\) kicks per second,

\[ \frac{d\langle A^2\rangle}{dt}\bigg|_{kicks} = \dot N\langle\Delta^2\rangle \equiv D \]

\(D\) is the diffusion rate of the squared amplitude (units of \(A^2\) per second). It is the random walk of chapter 3: the mean square grows linearly in time, the rms only as \(\sqrt t\). Two points matter. A kick of zero mean still raises the mean square, because \(\Delta^2\) is never negative. And although the kick hits one coordinate, the particle goes on round its circle, so the gain is shared between the two: averaged round the circle, \(\langle x^2\rangle = \langle A^2\rangle/2\).

Damping. Let the amplitude also decay, \(dA/dt = -A/\tau\), with \(\tau\) the damping time of the amplitude. Then \(dA^2/dt = 2A\,dA/dt = -2A^2/\tau\): the square damps twice as fast. Together,

\[ \frac{d\langle A^2\rangle}{dt} = -\frac{2}{\tau}\,\langle A^2\rangle + D \]

a first-order linear differential equation with constant coefficients (F1, section 10). Write \(Y = \langle A^2\rangle - D\tau/2\); then \(dY/dt = -(2/\tau)Y\), whose solution is \(Y_0e^{-2t/\tau}\). So, starting from \(\langle A^2\rangle_0\),

\[ \langle A^2\rangle(t) = \frac{D\tau}{2} + \left(\langle A^2\rangle_0 - \frac{D\tau}{2}\right)e^{-2t/\tau} \]

Whatever the start, the beam forgets it in a few damping times and settles at

\[ \langle A^2\rangle_{eq} = \frac{D\,\tau}{2}, \qquad \langle x^2\rangle_{eq} = \frac{D\,\tau}{4} \]

The equilibrium grows with the strength of the kicks and with the damping time: weak damping lets the random walk go further before it is pulled back.

Figure 20.6. Random kicks against damping, from the solution above, with \(\langle A^2\rangle\) in units of its equilibrium \(D\tau/2\) and time in damping times. A beam injected with no spread (red) grows towards equilibrium as \(1 - e^{-2t/\tau}\); a beam injected four times too large (blue) shrinks towards the same value. Both forget their start in a few \(\tau\). Without damping (dashed) the random walk would grow without limit, passing the equilibrium value at \(t = \tau/2\).

The shape of the equilibrium. In equilibrium each particle’s \(x\) is a sum of many small, independent kicks, each partly damped away since. By the central limit theorem such a sum is Gaussian, whatever the distribution of single kicks. So the equilibrium beam is a Gaussian in each coordinate, of rms \(\sqrt{D\tau/4}\). The equation for the whole distribution, of which this is the second moment, is the Fokker-Planck equation; its steady solution is this Gaussian (Lee, chapter 4, section II.4, part D).

At work: the energy spread of MAX IV. The kicks are photons. Each changes the relative energy deviation \(\delta\) by \(-u/E\); the mean loss is replaced by the RF, and what remains is the fluctuation. From the photon statistics, an electron emits 388.5 photons a turn with \(\langle u^2\rangle = \tfrac{11}{27}\varepsilon_c^2 = 3.77\) keV\(^2\). So, per turn,

\[ D = 388.5\times\frac{3.77\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{eV}^2}{(3\times10^9\ \mathrm{eV})^2} = 1.63\times10^{-10} \]

The longitudinal damping time is \(\tau_E = 2E/(J_EU_0) = 14\,300\) turns. The equilibrium is \(\sigma_\delta^2 = D\tau_E/4 = 5.81\times10^{-7}\), so \(\sigma_\delta = 7.6\times10^{-4}\), as section 9 finds. A single photon moves \(\delta\) by about \(6\times10^{-7}\) (rms) and a turn’s worth of photons by \(1.3\times10^{-5}\); it takes thousands of turns of random walk, held in check by the damping, to build the spread of \(7.6\times10^{-4}\).

A quick simulation confirms the bookkeeping: 4000 particles rotating in phase space, damped by \(e^{-1/\tau}\) per turn with \(\tau = 2000\) turns and kicked with \(D = 4/\tau\) per turn, settle at an rms of 1.007, against the predicted 1.

Exercise Q1. A beam is injected into a ring with almost no energy spread. How many damping times until its spread squared reaches 90 per cent of the equilibrium value?

Answer

\(\langle A^2\rangle = (D\tau/2)(1 - e^{-2t/\tau})\), which reaches 0.9 of equilibrium when \(e^{-2t/\tau} = 0.1\), \(t = \tfrac12\tau\ln10 = 1.15\tau\). For MAX IV’s longitudinal plane, 29 ms.

Exercise Q2. Without damping, how long would the random walk take to reach the equilibrium spread? Why is the answer shorter than the time to reach 90 per cent with damping?

Answer

\(\langle A^2\rangle = Dt\) equals \(D\tau/2\) at \(t = \tau/2\). With damping the growth is \(D\) at first and slows as \(\langle A^2\rangle\) rises, because the damping removes more as there is more to remove, so equilibrium is approached only exponentially.

Exercise Q3. At fixed bending radius, how does the equilibrium energy spread depend on the beam energy? Use \(D \propto N_{turn}\langle u^2\rangle/E^2\) and \(\tau_E \propto E/U_0\) (in turns).

Answer

\(N_{turn} \propto \gamma\), \(\langle u^2\rangle \propto \varepsilon_c^2 \propto \gamma^6\), so \(D \propto \gamma\cdot\gamma^6/\gamma^2 = \gamma^5\). \(U_0 \propto \gamma^4\), so \(\tau_E \propto \gamma/\gamma^4 = \gamma^{-3}\) turns. Then \(\sigma_\delta^2 \propto D\tau_E \propto \gamma^2\), and \(\sigma_\delta \propto \gamma\): section 9’s \(\sigma_\delta^2 = C_q\gamma^2/(J_E\rho)\).

9. Quantum excitation and the equilibrium beam

If damping were the whole story, an electron beam would shrink to nothing. It does not, because the energy is lost in random photons (section 6). Each emission is a small random kick, and the beam does a random walk that the damping holds in check. The equilibrium is the natural beam.

Energy spread. Treat the synchrotron oscillation as a point moving round a circle in a scaled phase plane, with radius \(A\) (in units of \(\delta\)). A photon of energy \(u\) changes \(\delta\) by \(-u/E\) and \(A^2\) on average by \((u/E)^2\), while the damping removes \(A^2\) at the rate \(2/\tau_E\). Averaged over the oscillation, \(\langle\delta^2\rangle = \langle A^2\rangle/2\). Let \(\dot N\langle u^2\rangle\) be the rate of emission times the mean square photon energy, averaged round the ring (J\(^2\)/s). Then

\[ \frac{d\langle A^2\rangle}{dt} = -\frac{2}{\tau_E}\langle A^2\rangle + \frac{\dot N\langle u^2\rangle}{E^2}, \qquad \sigma_\delta^2 = \frac{\langle A^2\rangle}{2} = \frac{\tau_E\,\dot N\langle u^2\rangle}{4E^2} \]

From the spectrum (section 6), at a point of bending radius \(\rho\), \(\dot N\langle u^2\rangle = 2C_q\gamma^2 E P/\rho\) (Wolski, equation 102), with

\[ C_q = \frac{55}{32\sqrt{3}}\frac{\hbar}{m_e c} = 3.832\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{m} \]

Average this round the ring. The electron spends a time \(ds/c\) on each length \(ds\), and there radiates \(P(s) = e^2c\gamma^4/(6\pi\varepsilon_0\rho^2)\) (section 3), so over one turn of \(T_0\)

\[ \overline{\dot N\langle u^2\rangle} = \frac{1}{T_0}\oint 2C_q\gamma^2E\,\frac{P(s)}{\rho}\,\frac{ds}{c} = \frac{2C_q\gamma^2E}{T_0}\cdot\frac{e^2\gamma^4}{6\pi\varepsilon_0}\,I_3, \qquad I_3 = \oint\frac{ds}{|\rho|^3} \]

the third synchrotron radiation integral (m\(^{-2}\)). In the same way \(U_0 = \oint P\,ds/c = (e^2\gamma^4/6\pi\varepsilon_0)\,I_2\), so \(\tau_E = 2ET_0/(J_EU_0) = 2ET_0\cdot6\pi\varepsilon_0/(J_Ee^2\gamma^4I_2)\). Put both into \(\sigma_\delta^2 = \tau_E\overline{\dot N\langle u^2\rangle}/(4E^2)\): the factors \(T_0\), \(e^2\gamma^4/(6\pi\varepsilon_0)\) and \(E^2\) all cancel, leaving

\[ \sigma_\delta^2 = C_q\gamma^2\,\frac{I_3}{J_E\,I_2} \]

For an isomagnetic ring \(I_3/I_2 = 1/\rho\) and \(\sigma_\delta^2 = C_q\gamma^2/(J_E\rho)\). The energy spread depends only on the energy and the bending radius: it grows as \(\gamma/\sqrt{\rho}\).

Worked number: MAX IV. With \(\gamma = 5871\), \(J_E = 1.153\) and \(\rho = 19.7\) m, \(\sigma_\delta = 7.6\times10^{-4}\). The published bare-lattice value is \(7.69\times10^{-4}\). Read backwards, the published energy spread implies \(\rho = 19.4\) m, consistent with the 19.7 m from the energy loss; the small difference is because MAX IV’s dipoles are not all alike.

Emittance. In the horizontal plane the kick acts through dispersion. A photon emitted where the dispersion is \(D_x\) and its slope \(D_x'\) moves the closed orbit by \((-D_x u/E, -D_x' u/E)\) in position and angle, so the betatron coordinates measured from the new orbit jump by the opposite amounts. With the Courant-Snyder action \(J_\beta\) (half the Courant-Snyder invariant, \(J_\beta = A^2/2\), as in chapter 16’s pre-requisite on action-angle variables; its average over the beam is the emittance, \(\varepsilon = \langle J_\beta\rangle\)), the jump changes \(2J_\beta\) on average by \(\mathcal{H}_x(u/E)^2\), where

\[ \mathcal{H}_x = \gamma_x D_x^2 + 2\alpha_x D_x D_x' + \beta_x D_x'^2 \]

is the curly-H function (m): the Courant-Snyder invariant of the dispersion itself. The same balance of random walk and damping, at rate \(2/\tau_x\) for the emittance, gives

\[ \frac{d\varepsilon}{dt} = -\frac{2}{\tau_x}\varepsilon + \frac{\langle\mathcal{H}_x\,\dot N\langle u^2\rangle\rangle}{2E^2} \]

and, in equilibrium, \(\varepsilon = \tau_x\langle\mathcal{H}_x\dot N\langle u^2\rangle\rangle/(4E^2)\). The average is the one just done for the energy spread with an extra \(\mathcal{H}_x\) inside the integral, so \(I_3\) becomes \(I_5\) below, and \(\tau_x\) brings \(J_x\) where \(\tau_E\) brought \(J_E\):

\[ \varepsilon_{x0} = C_q\gamma^2\,\frac{I_5}{J_x I_2}, \qquad I_5 = \oint\frac{\mathcal{H}_x}{|\rho|^3}\,ds \]

This is the natural emittance (m rad), written \(\varepsilon_{x0}\) to keep it apart from the permittivity of free space \(\varepsilon_0\). Wolski (equations 98 to 124) and Sands (1970) carry the derivation through. The physical content: to make the emittance small, make \(\mathcal{H}_x\) small where the electrons radiate, that is keep the dispersion and its slope small inside the dipoles.

The vertical emittance has no dispersion in an ideal flat ring, and the only excitation is the photon’s tiny angle, \(1/\gamma\); this quantum limit is far below anything measured. In practice the vertical emittance comes from coupling and stray vertical dispersion caused by errors (chapter 18), and is usually quoted as a percentage of the horizontal, the coupling.

10. How emittance scales: lattices

Write the dipole length as \(L = \rho\theta\), with \(\theta\) the bending angle of each dipole. Inside a dipole the dispersion grows from whatever it was at the entrance by roughly \(\rho\theta^2/2\), and its slope by \(\theta\). Optimising \(\beta_x\), \(\alpha_x\), \(D_x\) and \(D_x'\) at the dipole entrance to minimise \(I_5\) shows that the best possible \(\langle\mathcal{H}_x\rangle\) in a dipole scales as \(\rho\theta^3\). The scaling can be seen without the optimisation. Inside a well-designed dipole every length is of the size of the dipole: \(\beta_x \sim L = \rho\theta\), \(D_x \sim \rho\theta^2\), \(D_x' \sim \theta\). Then the two kinds of term in \(\mathcal{H}_x\) are \(D_x^2/\beta_x \sim \rho^2\theta^4/(\rho\theta) = \rho\theta^3\) and \(\beta_xD_x'^2 \sim \rho\theta\cdot\theta^2 = \rho\theta^3\), the same. In an isomagnetic ring \(I_5/I_2 = \langle\mathcal{H}_x\rangle/\rho \sim \theta^3\) (the \(1/\rho^3\) in \(I_5\) against the \(1/\rho^2\) in \(I_2\)), and so

\[ \varepsilon_{x0} = F\,\frac{C_q\gamma^2\theta^3}{J_x} \]

for small \(\theta\), with \(F\) a number fixed by the type of lattice. The lattices differ in how they arrange dipoles and quadrupoles into the cell that repeats round the ring. A FODO cell (chapter 16, section 6) alternates focusing and defocusing quadrupoles with dipoles between them, and its dispersion never vanishes. An achromat is a cell whose dispersion and its slope are both zero at its ends, so that the straight sections between cells have none: particles of every energy leave it on the same orbit (“achromatic”, without colour, as in optics). The double-bend achromat (DBA) does it with two dipoles and a focusing quadrupole between them; the multi-bend achromat (MBA) with \(M\) dipoles (figure 20.7); and the theoretical minimum emittance (TME) cell gives up the zero dispersion at the ends to make \(\mathcal{H}_x\) as small as possible in its one dipole. Wolski derives (section 3, table 1):

Two scalings are the whole story of light sources. At fixed lattice, \(\varepsilon_{x0} \propto \gamma^2\): doubling the energy quadruples the emittance. At fixed energy, \(\varepsilon_{x0} \propto \theta^3 \propto N_d^{-3}\), where \(N_d = 2\pi/\theta\) is the number of dipoles: doubling the number of dipoles divides the emittance by eight. An achromat has zero dispersion in its straight sections, where the insertion devices go (section 11), so that their radiation does not add to \(I_5\) and their source points do not move with energy. The price of more dipoles is a longer ring or shorter, stronger magnets (chapter 17), stronger focusing, larger natural chromaticity (the spread of tune with energy, chapter 16, section 12) and stronger sextupoles to correct it, and so a smaller dynamic aperture, the region of amplitudes in which particles survive the sextupoles’ nonlinear kicks (chapter 18).

Worked numbers. The original ESRF (6 GeV, 844 m) had 32 double-bend cells, 64 dipoles, so \(\theta = 2\pi/64\); the DBA minimum is \(0.0645\,C_q\gamma^2\theta^3 = 3.2\) nm rad with \(J_x = 1\), and the machine ran at 4 nm rad (Revol and colleagues, IPAC 2022). The ESRF-EBS kept the 32 cells but put seven dipoles in each, 224 dipoles; the 7BA minimum with \(J_x = 1\) is 33 pm rad, and the machine runs at 133 pm rad. MAX IV’s 20 seven-bend achromats (140 dipoles, average 2.57°) give a minimum of 18.5 pm rad with its \(J_x = 1.847\), against its 328 pm rad. Real lattices sit some factor above the minimum because dynamic aperture and injection need weaker focusing than the ideal (Wolski, section 3.4); the scaling with \(\theta^3\) is what holds.

Pre-requisite: Coherent and incoherent radiation

Pre-requisite. This section shows when radiation from several sources adds in power and when it adds in field. Waves with random phases add their powers, so \(N\) emitters give \(N\) times the power of one; waves in step add their fields, and give \(N^2\) times. It applies the idea three times: to the electrons of a bunch (incoherent at X-ray wavelengths, coherent at wavelengths longer than the bunch), to the successive periods of an undulator (whose waves interfere into narrow lines at harmonics of one frequency), and to the light of a small source (which cannot be narrower in angle than diffraction allows, giving the photon emittance \(\lambda/4\pi\) and the idea of transverse coherence). Section 11 uses all three for wigglers, undulators and brightness. Taught from Chao and Tigner’s Handbook, sections 3.1.2 (diffraction limit and brightness), 3.1.3 (coherent radiation) and 3.1.6 (undulator and wiggler radiation), and Landau and Lifshitz, volume 2, §61 (Fraunhofer diffraction).

Fields add, powers do not. Maxwell’s equations are linear, so the fields of two sources add. The power is proportional to the square of the field. Two waves of equal amplitude \(\mathcal{E}\) at one frequency, with phases differing by \(\Delta\varphi\), give at a point

\[ |\mathcal{E}_1 + \mathcal{E}_2|^2 = \mathcal{E}^2 + \mathcal{E}^2 + 2\mathcal{E}^2\cos\Delta\varphi \]

In step (\(\Delta\varphi = 0\)) this is \(4\mathcal{E}^2\), twice the sum of the separate powers. Opposite (\(\Delta\varphi = \pi\)) it is zero. If the phase difference is random, the cosine averages to zero and the result is \(2\mathcal{E}^2\): the powers simply add.

For \(N\) sources with phases \(\varphi_k\),

\[ \left|\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathcal{E}\,e^{i\varphi_k}\right|^2 = \mathcal{E}^2\left[N + \sum_{j\ne k}\cos(\varphi_j - \varphi_k)\right] \]

The first term is the \(N\) squares; the second holds \(N(N - 1)\) cross terms. With random phases the cross terms average to zero and the power is \(N \mathcal{E}^2\): incoherent addition. With all phases equal every cross term is 1 and the power is \(N^2\mathcal{E}^2\): coherent addition. For a bunch of \(10^{10}\) electrons the difference is a factor of \(10^{10}\). (Random phases give \(N\) only on average. A simulation of 2000 sets of random phases gives a mean of \(N\) for \(N\) = 10 to \(10^4\), but individual sets scatter about it by about 100 per cent: the speckle seen when light of one frequency from many random emitters is looked at in one direction. Averaging over frequencies, directions or bunches smooths it out.)

A bunch of electrons: the form factor. Every electron in a bunch makes the same pulse of synchrotron light; they differ only in when. An electron a distance \(z_k\) ahead of the bunch centre sends its pulse earlier by \(z_k/c\), which at angular frequency \(\omega\) is a phase \(\omega z_k/c = 2\pi z_k/\lambda\). The cross terms average, over the random positions in a bunch of line density \(\psi(z)\) (normalised to unit area), to the square of

\[ g(\lambda) = \int\psi(z)\,e^{2\pi iz/\lambda}\,dz \]

the form factor, a Fourier transform of the bunch shape. The power radiated at that wavelength by \(N\) electrons, each of which alone gives \(p(\lambda)\), is (Chao and Tigner, §3.1.3)

\[ P(\lambda) = p(\lambda)\,N\left[1 + (N - 1)\,|g(\lambda)|^2\right] \]

For a Gaussian bunch of rms length \(\sigma_z\) chapter 8’s transform gives \(g = e^{-(2\pi\sigma_z/\lambda)^2/2}\), so \(|g|^2 = e^{-(2\pi\sigma_z/\lambda)^2}\). At wavelengths much shorter than the bunch it is zero to any precision, and the radiation is incoherent. At wavelengths longer than about \(2\pi\sigma_z\), \(|g|^2\) approaches 1 and the bunch radiates as one charge \(Ne\).

At work. A MAX IV bunch holds about \(3.1\times10^{10}\) electrons (500 mA in 176 bunches). With its natural length of 10.1 mm the coherent term equals the incoherent one, \(N|g|^2 = 1\), at \(\lambda = 2\pi\sigma_z/\sqrt{\ln N} = 13\) mm; at X-ray wavelengths it is zero. Such long waves are also cut off by the metal chamber round the beam (for a 22 mm chamber on this radius, Chao and Tigner’s shielding estimate gives about 1.5 mm), so a stored beam of this length does not radiate coherently. A linac bunch for a free-electron laser is different. With 100 pC (\(6.2\times10^8\) electrons) compressed to \(\sigma_z = 10\) µm, the coherent enhancement \(N|g|^2\) is \(4\times10^8\) at \(\lambda = 100\) µm (3 THz), \(1.3\times10^8\) at 50 µm, and \(4\times10^{-9}\), that is nothing, at 10 µm. Coherent terahertz light from such bunches is used to measure their length; coherent X-rays need the bunch to be structured on the X-ray wavelength itself, which is what a free-electron laser does (chapter 26).

One electron, many periods: interference into lines. In an undulator one electron makes a short wave each period, and the \(N\) periods send \(N\) copies of the same wave, each later than the one before by a fixed delay \(T_1\). The delay is set by how far the electron falls behind its light in one period: one wavelength \(\lambda_1\) on axis, so \(T_1 = \lambda_1/c = 2\pi/\omega_1\) (section 11 derives \(\lambda_1\)). At angular frequency \(\omega\) successive copies differ in phase by \(\varphi = \omega T_1 = 2\pi\omega/\omega_1\), and the total field is the field of one period times a geometric series:

\[ \sum_{k=0}^{N-1}e^{ik\varphi} = \frac{1 - e^{iN\varphi}}{1 - e^{i\varphi}}, \qquad \left|\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}e^{ik\varphi}\right|^2 = \frac{\sin^2(N\varphi/2)}{\sin^2(\varphi/2)} \]

The first form is the sum of a geometric series: if \(S = 1 + z + \dots + z^{N-1}\) with \(z = e^{i\varphi}\), then \(S - zS = 1 - z^N\) (every other term cancels), so \(S = (1 - z^N)/(1 - z)\) (chapter 17 does the same). The second uses \(|1 - e^{i\theta}| = 2|\sin(\theta/2)|\), which follows from \(1 - e^{i\theta} = e^{i\theta/2}(e^{-i\theta/2} - e^{i\theta/2}) = -2i\,e^{i\theta/2}\sin(\theta/2)\) with Euler’s formula. This is the function of a diffraction grating (F5, section 15). Where \(\varphi\) is a multiple of \(2\pi\), at \(\omega = n\omega_1\), every copy is in step and it equals \(N^2\). Between those frequencies it is small: of order 1 midway between peaks, and even the side lobes next to a peak are under 5 per cent of it. The energy collects into lines at the harmonics \(n\omega_1\).

Figure 20.8. The grating function \(\sin^2(N\varphi/2)/\sin^2(\varphi/2)\), divided by its peak value \(N^2\), against \(\omega/\omega_1\), drawn for \(N = 10\) periods so that the lines are wide enough to see. Each line has its first zeros \(1/N\) either side of the harmonic and small side lobes. BioMAX’s undulator, with \(N = 111\), makes lines eleven times narrower and \(111^2/10^2 = 123\) times taller relative to the single-period wave.

  • Height and width. The peak is \(N^2\); the first zeros either side are at \(N\varphi/2 = \pm\pi\) from the peak, \(\Delta\varphi = 2\pi/N\), which at harmonic \(n\) is \(\Delta\omega/\omega = 1/(nN)\). The full width at half height is \(0.886/(nN)\). Height times width grows as \(N\): one electron’s energy in the line grows in proportion to the number of periods, but is squeezed into a band \(N\) times narrower.
  • Which harmonics. The grating function only selects frequencies; a harmonic appears only if the wave from one period contains it. For \(K \ll 1\) the electron’s wiggle is gentle, its wave is a sine, and only the fundamental appears. For \(K\) near or above 1 the electron’s direction swings across the line of sight, each period’s wave is sharpened, and harmonics appear. On axis the wave is symmetric between the two halves of each period, and the even harmonics cancel; off axis they appear.
  • Coherent within one electron, incoherent between electrons. The periods add in field because they are made by the same electron; different electrons in the bunch still add in power, as above.

At work. BioMAX’s undulator has \(N = 111\). Each line is \(111^2 = 12\,321\) times the single-period intensity at its peak. The fundamental (0.887 nm, 1.40 keV) has a relative width of \(1/111 = 0.9\) per cent; the 9th harmonic (12.6 keV) \(1/999 = 0.1\) per cent, 12.6 eV wide.

The central cone. Off axis at angle \(\theta\), the undulator equation of section 11 moves each line to longer wavelength by the factor \(1 + \gamma^2\theta^2/(1 + K^2/2)\). At a fixed frequency the light fades once that shift exceeds the line width \(1/(nN)\), that is for \(\gamma^2\theta^2/(1 + K^2/2) \gtrsim 1/(nN)\). Using \(\lambda_n = \lambda_u(1 + K^2/2)/(2n\gamma^2)\) and \(L = N\lambda_u\), this angle is

\[ \theta \approx \sqrt{\frac{1 + K^2/2}{nN\gamma^2}} = \sqrt{\frac{2\lambda_n}{L}} \]

For BioMAX’s fundamental, 30 µrad; for its 9th harmonic, 10 µrad: far narrower than a bending magnet’s \(1/\gamma = 170\) µrad.

The diffraction limit. No source of light can be both small and parallel. Let the field across a source plane be \(\mathcal{E}(x)\). Light leaving at a small angle \(\theta\) from the point \(x\) travels a path shorter by \(x\theta\) than light from \(x = 0\) (this is Fraunhofer diffraction, Landau and Lifshitz, §61), so the amplitude at angle \(\theta\) is

\[ \tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\theta) \propto \int \mathcal{E}(x)\,e^{-ik\theta x}\,dx, \qquad k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \]

the Fourier transform of the source’s field, with \(k\theta\) as the variable. Take a source whose intensity is Gaussian with rms size \(\sigma\): its field is \(\mathcal{E} \propto e^{-x^2/(4\sigma^2)}\) (the intensity \(\mathcal{E}^2\) then has rms \(\sigma\)). The transform of a Gaussian is a Gaussian: \(\tilde{\mathcal{E}} \propto e^{-k^2\sigma^2\theta^2}\), so the intensity in angle is \(e^{-2k^2\sigma^2\theta^2}\), of rms \(\sigma' = 1/(2k\sigma)\). Therefore

\[ \sigma\,\sigma' = \frac{1}{2k} = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi} \]

(A numerical transform at \(\lambda = 1\) Å (one ångström, \(10^{-10}\) m, the scale of atoms and of hard X-rays) and \(\sigma = 10\) µm gives \(\sigma' = 0.796\) µrad and the product \(7.958\times10^{-12}\) m rad, equal to \(\lambda/4\pi\).) A Gaussian beam reaches this product, and no beam goes below it. It is the smallest emittance light of wavelength \(\lambda\) can have: the diffraction-limited, or photon, emittance. For an undulator of length \(L\) the light of one electron has \(\sigma_r = \sqrt{2\lambda L}/(4\pi)\) and \(\sigma_{r'} = \sqrt{\lambda/(2L)}\) (Kim’s convention, used in section 11), whose product is \(\lambda/4\pi\).

Electron beam and photon beam together. A real beam is many electrons, each a diffraction-limited source, scattered over the electron beam’s own size \(\sigma_e\) and divergence \(\sigma_{e'}\). Independent spreads add in quadrature: \(\Sigma^2 = \sigma_e^2 + \sigma_r^2\) and \(\Sigma'^2 = \sigma_{e'}^2 + \sigma_{r'}^2\). With \(\sigma_e^2 = \varepsilon\beta\) and \(\sigma_{e'}^2 = \varepsilon/\beta\) and the electron beta function matched to the light, \(\beta = \sigma_r/\sigma_{r'}\), the product is

\[ \Sigma^2\Sigma'^2 = \varepsilon^2 + \varepsilon\left(\beta\sigma_{r'}^2 + \frac{\sigma_r^2}{\beta}\right) + \sigma_r^2\sigma_{r'}^2 = \left(\varepsilon + \frac{\lambda}{4\pi}\right)^2 \]

so \(\Sigma\Sigma' = \varepsilon + \lambda/4\pi\): the electron emittance and the photon emittance simply add. While \(\varepsilon \gg \lambda/4\pi\) the light’s phase-space area is set by the electrons, and halving \(\varepsilon\) halves it. Once \(\varepsilon \ll \lambda/4\pi\), further reduction gains little.

Transverse coherence. Light that fills only the diffraction-limited area is transversely coherent: light from two points across the beam keeps a fixed phase relation and can interfere, as in Young’s two-slit experiment (F5, section 15). When the beam’s area is larger, it holds many independent patches, each of the diffraction-limited size, and only one of them can be used coherently. The coherent fraction of the flux is the ratio of the areas, in both planes (Chao and Tigner, §3.1.2 and §3.1.3):

\[ F_{coh} = \frac{(\lambda/4\pi)^2}{\Sigma_x\Sigma_{x'}\Sigma_y\Sigma_{y'}} \approx \frac{\lambda/4\pi}{\varepsilon_x + \lambda/4\pi}\cdot\frac{\lambda/4\pi}{\varepsilon_y + \lambda/4\pi} \]

the second form for a matched beam.

At work. MAX IV’s horizontal emittance, 328 pm rad, equals \(\lambda/4\pi\) at \(\lambda = 4.1\) nm (0.30 keV): below about 0.3 keV it is diffraction-limited horizontally. With a vertical emittance taken as 8 pm rad (a round figure; it is set by coupling and is adjustable), the coherent fraction is 49 per cent at 0.3 keV, 21 per cent at 1 keV, 7 per cent at 3 keV and 1.6 per cent at 10 keV. A second-generation ring of 4 nm rad with 1 per cent coupling would give 1.7 per cent at 1 keV and 0.05 per cent at 10 keV. Chao and Tigner, §2.2.4, define the brightness as \(B = F/[(2\pi)^2\Sigma_x\Sigma_y\Sigma_{x'}\Sigma_{y'}]\), with \(F\) the flux; multiplying by the coherent fraction above, the coherent flux is \(F\,F_{coh} = (\lambda/2)^2B\). Raising brightness is the same thing as raising coherent flux.

Exercise C1. A bunch of \(10^{10}\) electrons radiates at a wavelength much shorter than the bunch. By what factor does the power exceed that of one electron? What would it be if all the electrons radiated in step?

Answer

Incoherently, \(N = 10^{10}\). In step, \(N^2 = 10^{20}\), another factor of \(10^{10}\). The difference is the whole case for microbunching in a free-electron laser.

Exercise C2. How wide, in eV, is the 9th harmonic of BioMAX’s undulator at 12.6 keV, and how wide is its central cone? Why does a beamline still use a crystal monochromator?

Answer

\(\Delta E/E \approx 1/(nN) = 1/999\), so about 12.6 eV from the peak to each first zero (25 eV between the two zeros, 11.2 eV full width at half height). The cone is \(\sqrt{2\lambda_9/L} = \sqrt{2\times0.0986\ \mathrm{nm}/2.0\ \mathrm{m}} = 10\) µrad. Off-axis light and the electrons’ own energy spread and divergence widen the line further, and experiments often need \(10^{-4}\) or better, so a monochromator selects a narrower band.

Exercise C3. A linac bunch of 100 pC has \(\sigma_z = 10\) µm. Find the coherent enhancement \(N|g|^2\) at 300 µm and at 20 µm.

Answer

\(N = 6.24\times10^8\). At 300 µm, \(|g|^2 = e^{-(2\pi\times10/300)^2} = 0.957\), enhancement \(6.0\times10^8\). At 20 µm, \(|g|^2 = e^{-(\pi)^2} = 5.2\times10^{-5}\), enhancement \(3.2\times10^4\): still large, because \(N\) is large, but falling fast. Below about 10 µm it vanishes.

11. Insertion devices: wigglers and undulators

A dipole sweeps its searchlight across the observer once a turn. An insertion device, placed in a straight section, makes the electron wiggle many times on the way past, with a field that alternates in sign along the axis:

\[ B_y(s) = B_0\cos\left(\frac{2\pi s}{\lambda_u}\right) \]

with \(\lambda_u\) the period (m) and \(B_0\) the peak field (T). The net bend is zero, so the device is transparent to the rest of the ring.

The deflection parameter \(K\). The horizontal angle is the integral of the curvature: \(x' = \int (e B_y/p)\,ds = \frac{eB_0\lambda_u}{2\pi p}\sin(2\pi s/\lambda_u)\). With \(p = \gamma mc\) the maximum angle is \(K/\gamma\), where

\[ K = \frac{eB_0\lambda_u}{2\pi m_e c}, \qquad K = 0.934\,B_0\,[\mathrm{T}]\,\lambda_u\,[\mathrm{cm}] \]

\(K\) is dimensionless and independent of the beam energy. It compares the wiggle angle with the radiation cone. If \(K \gg 1\), the electron’s direction swings by many times \(1/\gamma\); the observer sees a separate flash from each pole, and the flashes add incoherently. This is a wiggler: a row of short dipoles giving \(2N\) times the flux of one (a device of \(N\) periods has \(2N\) poles, and each pole sends its own flash towards the observer), with a critical energy set by the peak field, \(\varepsilon_c = 0.665\,E^2B_0\). A short wiggler of high field, a wavelength shifter, was how the 2 GeV SRS at Daresbury reached harder X-rays. If \(K \lesssim 1\), the observer sees a continuous wave train with \(N\) periods, the fields from successive periods interfere, and the spectrum concentrates into narrow lines. This is an undulator.

Figure 20.9. An undulator, not to scale. (a) Side view: rows of magnet blocks above and below the beam, a gap \(g\) apart, make a vertical field \(B_y\) (blue) that reverses every half period, \(B_y = B_0\cos(2\pi s/\lambda_u)\). (b) Top view: the force \(-e\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\) is horizontal, so the electron wiggles in the horizontal plane. It turns hardest where the field peaks and crosses the axis, at its largest angle \(K/\gamma\), where the field is zero. Over a whole period the bends cancel, and the electron leaves on its original line. At BioMAX the period is 18 mm and the sideways wiggle only \(K\lambda_u/(2\pi\gamma) = 1.1\) µm either side of the axis.

The undulator equation. The interference condition is that light and electron, starting together at one period, should be one wavelength \(\lambda\) (or \(n\) wavelengths) apart at the next. The electron’s speed is \(\beta c\), but part of it is sideways. Its longitudinal speed is \(\beta_s = \beta\sqrt{1 - x'^2} \approx \beta(1 - x'^2/2)\), and averaging \(x'^2 = (K/\gamma)^2\sin^2\) over a period gives \(K^2/(2\gamma^2)\), so with \(\beta \approx 1 - 1/(2\gamma^2)\),

\[ \bar\beta_s \approx 1 - \frac{1}{2\gamma^2} - \frac{K^2}{4\gamma^2} = 1 - \frac{1 + K^2/2}{2\gamma^2} \]

The electron takes \(\lambda_u/(\bar\beta_s c)\) to cross a period. Light emitted at angle \(\theta\) to the axis covers, in that time, a distance \(\lambda_u/\bar\beta_s\) along its own direction. The electron has moved \(\lambda_u\) along the axis, of which \(\lambda_u\cos\theta\) lies along the light’s direction. Measured along that direction, the light is ahead of the electron by \(\lambda_u/\bar\beta_s - \lambda_u\cos\theta\), and this must be \(n\lambda\). With \(1/\bar\beta_s \approx 1 + (1 + K^2/2)/(2\gamma^2)\) and \(\cos\theta \approx 1 - \theta^2/2\):

\[ \lambda_n = \frac{\lambda_u}{2n\gamma^2}\left(1 + \frac{K^2}{2} + \gamma^2\theta^2\right), \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots \]

The physics is a double Doppler shift: in the electron’s frame the undulator period is Lorentz-contracted to \(\lambda_u/\gamma\), the electron oscillates at that wavelength, and the light it emits forward is Doppler-shifted by \(2\gamma\). The factor \(1 + K^2/2\) is the slowing of the electron by its wiggle. On axis only odd harmonics appear; the even ones appear off axis. A line from \(N\) periods has relative width about \(1/(nN)\). The tuning knob is the gap between the magnet jaws: opening it lowers \(B_0\), so \(K\), and moves the line to shorter wavelength.

Worked numbers: BioMAX at MAX IV. The in-vacuum undulators for the BioMAX and NanoMAX beamlines have \(\lambda_u = 18\) mm, 111 periods (2.0 m), a minimum gap of 4.2 mm and an achieved effective \(K\) of 2.19 for BioMAX (Tavares and colleagues’ commissioning report, Journal of Synchrotron Radiation, 2018, table 5). Then \(B_0 = 2.19/(0.934\times1.8) = 1.30\) T, and at 3 GeV the fundamental is

\[ \lambda_1 = \frac{0.018}{2\times5871^2}\left(1 + \frac{2.19^2}{2}\right) = 0.887\ \mathrm{nm}, \qquad 1.40\ \mathrm{keV} \]

The beamline works from 5 to 25 keV, so it uses harmonics from about the third to beyond the fifteenth, tuning the gap so that a harmonic lands on the wanted energy. Opening the gap to \(K = 0.5\) raises the fundamental to 4.2 keV. The relative line width of the fundamental is about \(1/111\), 0.9 per cent.

Brightness. Experimenters care about photons per second into a small spot and a small angle, in a narrow band. The brightness (often called brilliance) is

\[ \mathcal{B} = \frac{\dot N_{ph}}{4\pi^2\,\Sigma_x\Sigma_{x'}\Sigma_y\Sigma_{y'}} \]

in photons s\(^{-1}\) mm\(^{-2}\) mrad\(^{-2}\) per 0.1 per cent bandwidth, where each \(\Sigma\) adds in quadrature the electron beam’s size or divergence (\(\sqrt{\varepsilon\beta_x}\), \(\sqrt{\varepsilon/\beta_x}\), with \(\beta_x\) the betatron function at the source) and the light’s own: \(\Sigma_x^2 = \sigma_x^2 + \sigma_r^2\). A single electron’s undulator light is itself a beam, with \(\sigma_r = \sqrt{2\lambda L}/(4\pi)\) and \(\sigma_{r'} = \sqrt{\lambda/(2L)}\) for an undulator of length \(L\) (K.-J. Kim’s convention; other texts differ by factors of order one). The product \(\sigma_r\sigma_{r'} = \lambda/(4\pi)\) is the emittance of a diffraction-limited photon beam. Once the electron emittance is below \(\lambda/4\pi\), shrinking it further no longer raises the brightness much; the light is then largely transversely coherent. At 1 Å (12.4 keV), \(\lambda/4\pi = 8\) pm rad; at BioMAX’s fundamental (0.887 nm), 71 pm rad. A ring of a few hundred pm rad is diffraction-limited only in the soft X-rays. The brightness grows as \(\varepsilon^{-2}\) while the electron emittance dominates both planes, which is why the generations of light sources are told by emittance.

12. Touschek lifetime and spin polarisation, in outline

Touschek scattering. Inside a bunch the electrons have small transverse momenta relative to each other. Occasionally two of them collide in a large-angle Coulomb scattering that turns transverse momentum into longitudinal. In the laboratory the longitudinal momentum change is magnified by \(\gamma\). If the resulting \(\delta\) exceeds the momentum acceptance \(\delta_{acc}\) (set by the RF bucket or by the lattice where the dispersion is large), both electrons are lost. The effect was found at the small storage ring AdA in Orsay and explained by Bruno Touschek (Bernardini and colleagues, Physical Review Letters 10, 407, 1963). The loss rate, quoted here in outline only (the full expression, whose constants differ between texts, is in the standard texts on electron storage rings), goes as

\[ \frac{1}{\tau_T} \propto \frac{N\,r_e^2\,c}{\gamma^2\,\sigma_x\sigma_y\sigma_z\,\delta_{acc}^3}\,D(\zeta) \]

with \(N\) the particles per bunch and \(D\) a slowly varying function of the ratio of the momentum acceptance to the spread of transverse momenta in the bunch. The density of the bunch is what kills it, and the denser the light source, the worse. Low-emittance rings fight it with large momentum acceptance and with harmonic cavities that stretch the bunch: MAX IV’s three passive third-harmonic (Landau) cavities lengthen the bunch from its natural 10.1 mm to about 54 mm, which brings the Touschek lifetime beyond 25 hours (Tavares and colleagues, 2014).

Spin polarisation by radiation. A tiny fraction of the emitted photons flip the electron’s spin, and the flip probability is slightly larger in one direction. An electron beam in a ring polarises itself, with spins antiparallel to the guide field (positrons parallel), up to a limit of \(8/(5\sqrt{3}) = 92.4\) per cent. This is the Sokolov-Ternov effect (A. A. Sokolov and I. M. Ternov, Soviet Physics Doklady 8, 1203, 1964; the Russian original appeared in 1963). For an isomagnetic ring the build-up time is

\[ \tau_p = \frac{8}{5\sqrt{3}}\,\frac{m_e\,\rho^3}{r_e\,\hbar\,\gamma^5}, \qquad \tau_p\,[\mathrm{s}] = 98.7\,\frac{\rho^3\,[\mathrm{m}]}{E^5\,[\mathrm{GeV}]} \]

multiplied by \(R/\rho\) when the ring has straight sections. For LEP at 45.6 GeV, with \(\rho = 3026\) m and \(R = 4243\) m, this is 5.4 hours. The effect was first seen at the storage rings VEPP-2 at Novosibirsk and ACO at Orsay, between about 1970 and 1972 (sources differ on the years). Depolarising resonances limit the polarisation in practice, and a polarised beam that is deliberately depolarised at a known spin-tune resonance measures the beam energy very precisely. (The spin tune is the number of turns the spin makes about the field per turn round the ring, \(a\gamma\) with \(a = 0.00116\) the electron’s magnetic anomaly (chapter 18), so it is a direct measure of \(\gamma\): at LEP’s 45.6 GeV it is 103.5. A small oscillating field at the right frequency flips the spins and destroys the polarisation, and the frequency at which that happens gives the energy.)

13. The wall for electron rings, and protons

LEP. CERN’s Large Electron-Positron collider had a circumference of 26,658.9 m (26,658.853 m in S. Y. Lee’s tables) and an effective bending radius of about 3026 m. Textbooks give a larger radius for the dipoles themselves: Lee lists 3096.2 m, and Padamsee, Knobloch and Hays quote 1860 MeV a turn at 90 GeV, which implies about 3.1 km. At 104 GeV every electron lost a thirtieth of its energy each turn and the beam forgot its injection in about 60 turns. Each milliampere of beam took 3.4 MW from the RF. The RF had to supply more than \(U_0\): a bunch sits in a bucket (the region of stable synchrotron motion, chapter 19, section 6) whose height must exceed the energy spread by a margin, or electrons diffuse out of it by quantum excitation (the quantum lifetime). The LEP RF group’s figures (Brown and colleagues, 2001) give the total voltage needed at 104 GeV for a ten-minute quantum lifetime as 3681 MV. The design electron must gain back \(U_0\) at each turn, \(e\hat V\sin\phi_s = U_0\), with \(\hat V\) the peak voltage and \(\phi_s\) the synchronous phase, the RF phase at which it crosses the cavities (chapter 12, section 2). So \(\sin\phi_s = 3.42/3.681 = 0.929\) and, above transition in the convention of this course, \(\phi_s = 180° - 68.3° = 111.7°\), with an overvoltage (the ratio \(e\hat V/U_0\)) of only 1.076. The system that gave it held 288 superconducting four-cell cavities and 56 copper ones, run at average gradients near 7.2 MV/m against a design of 6 MV/m (Padamsee’s book has the average rising to 7.5 MV/m over the operation of LEP2, the name for LEP’s high-energy years, 1996 to 2000). The RF group’s operating figures quote 104.2 GeV; the highest beam energy reached was 104.5 GeV (Shiltsev and Zimmermann, 2021). The highest voltage in physics with useful luminosity was 3675 MV. Going from 94.5 GeV to 104 GeV, a 10 per cent rise, raised the loss by 47 per cent. This is the wall: section 3’s \(E^4\).

Protons radiate too. The LHC’s 7 TeV protons in its dipoles (\(\rho = 2804\) m, from 1232 dipoles of magnetic length 14.3 m) have \(\gamma = 7461\), about the same as a 3.8 GeV electron. They lose \(U_0 = 6.7\) keV a turn, with \(\varepsilon_c = 44\) eV. For the nominal beam (2808 bunches of \(1.15\times10^{11}\) protons, revolution frequency 11.245 kHz, current 0.582 A), each beam radiates 3.9 kW, about 0.22 W per metre of dipole. That is trivial against the RF, and the damping time is about a day (\(\tau_E = 26\) hours), too slow to help. But it falls on a vacuum chamber at 1.9 K inside superconducting magnets. Removing a watt at 1.9 K costs, at the Carnot limit (F6, section 16: a refrigerator lifting heat \(Q\) from temperature \(T\) to room temperature needs at least the work \(Q(300 - T)/T\)), \((300 - 1.9)/1.9 = 157\) W at room temperature, and real refrigerators several times that. So the LHC has a beam screen, a perforated tube inside the cold bore, cooled to between 5 and 20 K, which intercepts the light (and the image-current heating) where removing it costs between 14 and 59 times its power at the Carnot limit, and shields the 1.9 K bore, which then acts as a cryopump through the screen’s slots. The 44 eV photons also knock out photoelectrons that seed the electron cloud of chapter 22.

How it was researched and built

From discovery to use, 1947 to 1970. After the General Electric observation the light was a nuisance or a curiosity to the accelerator builders. Its first systematic use for spectroscopy came at Cornell. D. H. Tomboulian and P. L. Hartman put a grazing-incidence vacuum spectrograph, without windows, on the 300 MeV Cornell synchrotron and recorded the spectrum down to about 6 nm, confirming Schwinger’s predictions and showing the absorption edges of beryllium and aluminium in the light (Physical Review 102, 1423, 1956). Others followed on synchrotrons at the National Bureau of Standards, where R. P. Madden and K. Codling first applied the light to atomic physics (1963), Frascati, DESY and elsewhere. These are called first-generation sources: machines built for particle physics, used parasitically. The ring called Tantalus, a 240 MeV storage ring of 9.38 m circumference at the University of Wisconsin, which grew out of the work of the Midwestern Universities Research Association, was finished in 1968 and became the first storage ring operated only for its light. A storage ring changed the work: the current stays for hours instead of a pulse at each acceleration cycle, and the light is steady.

At Stanford a small group won 1.2 million US dollars from the National Science Foundation in July 1973 to put a beamline on the SPEAR collider. The Stanford Synchrotron Radiation Project began operation in May 1974, parasitic on SPEAR’s particle physics, with one bending-magnet port feeding five stations; it became SSRL in 1977.

Dedicated rings, the second generation. The first ring built from the start as an X-ray source was the Synchrotron Radiation Source at Daresbury in England: 2 GeV, 96 m round, fed by a 12 MeV linac and a 600 MeV booster. Construction began in 1975; beam first circulated on 30 June 1980; it was formally opened on 7 November 1980; scheduled user operation began in spring 1981; and it reached its design current and energy in March 1982. A wavelength shifter extended it to harder X-rays. A high-brightness lattice, installed from October 1986 to March 1987, added sixteen quadrupoles to split its eight two-dipole cells into sixteen, and brought its emittance to about 100 nm rad (104 nm rad at 2 GeV). It closed on 4 August 2008, its users moving to Diamond.

The lattice that defined the generation came from Brookhaven. Renate Chasman and G. Kenneth Green, with E. M. Rowe, published a design for a dedicated facility in 1975 (IEEE Transactions on Nuclear Science 22, 1765) built of double-bend achromats: two dipoles per cell with a quadrupole between them to bring the dispersion to zero at the cell ends, leaving straight sections free of dispersion. The National Synchrotron Light Source at Brookhaven used it in two rings, a 700 MeV vacuum-ultraviolet ring that began operating in late 1982 and a 2.5 GeV X-ray ring commissioned in 1984 and at its design energy in August 1985. During the detailed design of the X-ray ring (Krinsky and colleagues, PAC 1979, describe it) the lattice went from six-fold to eight-fold symmetry, which reduced the number and cost of magnets and raised the number of insertions available for wigglers from three to five. Chasman and Green both died before the light was turned on.

Insertion devices. Hans Motz and colleagues at Stanford sent electrons from a linear accelerator through a periodic magnet and observed visible and millimetre-wave radiation (Journal of Applied Physics 24, 826, 1953): the first undulator. What made undulators practical in rings was Klaus Halbach’s design of arrays of rare-earth permanent-magnet blocks whose magnetisation rotates from block to block, giving strong, accurate, short-period fields without coils (Nuclear Instruments and Methods 187, 109, 1981). The undulator’s narrow line and high brightness needed a small emittance, and that defined the next generation.

Third generation, the 1990s. Rings designed around long straight sections for undulators, with emittances of a few nm rad:

Fourth generation: multi-bend achromats. The scaling \(\varepsilon \propto \theta^3\) says: use many small bends. The obstacle was technical. Many short dipoles and strong quadrupoles in a ring of reasonable size need small magnet bores, and small bores need a small, well-pumped vacuum chamber. MAX IV in Lund solved both. Its 3 GeV ring was first proposed by Mikael Eriksson in 2002 (Tavares and colleagues, 2014), described in a conceptual design report in 2006 and a detailed design report in 2010, and funded in April 2009. Each of its 20 seven-bend achromats is about 22 m long, with five unit cells (3° dipoles) and two matching cells (1.5° dipoles), all dipoles carrying a vertically focusing gradient. The magnets are built as compact blocks, and the vacuum chamber is a narrow copper tube coated on the inside with non-evaporable getter (NEG), which pumps along its whole length (Tavares and colleagues, 2018). The main RF is at 99.931 MHz. Commissioning of the ring began in August 2015, the facility was inaugurated on 21 June 2016, and the first user data were taken in December 2016. Its bare-lattice emittance is 328 pm rad.

The ESRF followed with the Extremely Brilliant Source, a hybrid multi-bend achromat (seven bends per cell) in the old tunnel. Design and procurement began in 2015; the old ring stopped in December 2018 after 30 years; the first beam was stored in December 2019; and users returned on 25 August 2020. The horizontal emittance fell from 4 nm rad to 133 pm rad. The design values were \(U_0 = 2.61\) MeV a turn from the dipoles and damping times of 8.5, 13 and 8.6 ms. The APS upgrade, a hybrid seven-bend achromat with extra reverse bends in the same tunnel, stored its first beam in April 2024 and delivered first light to a beamline on 17 June 2024, with a design natural emittance of 42 pm rad.

How a ring is designed today. The designer starts from the energy (set by the photon energies wanted), the number of straight sections and the circumference, which with the MBA formula fix the emittance in reach. The linear optics are built and matched in codes such as MAD-X, elegant, OPA or the Accelerator Toolbox; the radiation integrals \(I_2\) to \(I_5\) fall out of the optics, and with them \(U_0\), \(J_x\), the damping times, \(\varepsilon_{x0}\) and \(\sigma_\delta\). The sextupoles are then chosen to correct the large natural chromaticity (MAX IV’s is \(-50\) in both planes before correction) while keeping a dynamic aperture and momentum acceptance large enough for injection and Touschek lifetime; this nonlinear optimisation, by tracking, is now most of the work. The insertion devices’ output is computed with codes such as SPECTRA or SRW. The RF voltage is chosen for the loss with all devices closed plus a margin for the bucket; harmonic cavities are added to lengthen the bunch.

At the bench

The synchrotron-light monitor. The simplest view of a stored electron beam is its own visible light. A mirror in the vacuum chamber picks off the light from a dipole, and a lens images the source point onto a camera (figure 20.11). The image is an ellipse: wide horizontally, thin vertically, and it moves when the orbit moves. Its resolution is limited by diffraction, because the light comes in a cone of only about \(1/\gamma\) vertically; Diamond’s instrumentation group puts the limit for visible light at about 80 µm (chapter 08 gives the formula and LEP’s numbers). That is enough to watch injection, coupling changes and instabilities, but not to measure a modern vertical beam size of a few micrometres.

The X-ray pinhole camera. To see a few micrometres, use the X-rays and a pinhole. At the ESRF, Elleaume, Fortgang, Penel and Tarazona (1995) built one: a pinhole in air beside an aluminium window, a fluorescent screen and a CCD camera, capable of measuring emittances down to about 5 pm rad, and used routinely. Diamond’s two pinhole cameras (Thomas and Rehm, EPAC 2008) look at two bending magnets through a 1 mm aluminium window and about 9 m of air, which filter the spectrum to a peak near 26 keV. The pinholes are apertures of about 25 µm; the magnification is 2.4 and 2.7; the screen is a thin phosphor (P43) viewed by a CCD through a macro lens. A typical image is 50 µm by 25 µm (rms) at 1 per cent coupling. The point-spread function of pinhole and screen together, about 10 to 15 µm, is subtracted in quadrature. Two cameras at points of different dispersion separate emittance from energy spread through \(\sigma_x^2 = \varepsilon\beta_x + (D_x\sigma_\delta)^2\); the dispersion at each source point is measured by changing the RF frequency by 100 Hz and watching the image move. With the optics corrected Diamond measured vertical sizes as small as 6 µm, a vertical emittance of about 2 pm rad.

What the operator reads: horizontal and vertical size in micrometres, tilt, and the derived emittances, updated a few times a second. The signatures: the vertical size grows when a skew quadrupole or a sextupole is mis-set (coupling); both sizes grow and jitter when the beam goes unstable at high current; the horizontal size breathes when the insertion device gaps move, because their radiation changes \(U_0\) and so the damping.

A kicked beam. Give the stored beam a single kick with a fast kicker and watch a beam-position monitor turn by turn. The centroid oscillates at the betatron tune, and the envelope of the oscillation decays. The radiation sets a decay of tens of milliseconds at a few GeV, but what the monitor shows is usually faster: the electrons have slightly different tunes (chromaticity and amplitude dependence), they dephase, and the centroid signal dies before the electrons have damped (decoherence). At SRRC in Taiwan (1.3 GeV), Hsu and colleagues (1995) kicked a single bunch: the design radiation damping times in that configuration were 10.5, 13.75 and 8.13 ms, and the vertical decay extrapolated to zero current was 11.7 ms, close to the radiation value, while the horizontal decay was 4.3 ms, faster because of Landau damping (the fading of a coherent oscillation when its particles have a spread of tunes) and head-tail effects (wakefields that couple the head of a bunch to its tail, chapter 22) that depend on chromaticity. At the ESRF, Kernel and colleagues (1999) took the radiation damping as a constant 7 ms and separated it from the faster chromatic effects. The procedure: one bunch, small kick, chromaticity set near zero, fit the envelope, repeat at several currents and extrapolate.

The RF voltage needed. In the control room the RF voltage is a set point, and the synchronous phase follows from the loss. At MAX IV the six main cavities give up to 1.8 MV against a loss of 856 keV to 1000 keV with all devices closed (the two figures of section 3), so \(\sin\phi_s = 0.48\) to 0.56; with bare dipoles only 0.20. If the voltage is too low for the loss, the bucket is too shallow, electrons diffuse over its top by quantum excitation, and the lifetime collapses within minutes. At LEP this margin was the whole operation in 1999 and 2000. The machine was normally run with a reserve of two RF half-units, so that a trip would not lose the beam. At the very top the reserve was given up: in one fill of 2000 the energy was stepped from 102.2 GeV to 103.2 GeV and then to 104 GeV, held there for 16 minutes close to the quantum-lifetime limit, and the beam was lost after an RF trip when the energy was pushed higher still. The cryoplants were upgraded from 6.7 to 12.3 kW each to let the superconducting cavities run above their design gradient. The signature of an RF trip: a dip in total RF voltage, a sudden drop in lifetime, and beam lost on the momentum aperture where the dispersion is largest.

Hot absorbers and vacuum chamber heating. All the radiated power, hundreds of kilowatts in a modern ring (182 kW from MAX IV’s dipoles at 500 mA, before any insertion device), lands somewhere. Most of it goes to beamlines or to photon absorbers placed to catch the fan where it would otherwise hit the chamber (figure 20.12). In the APS upgrade each sector has five discrete absorbers, mostly of electron-beam-welded GlidCop AL-15 (copper strengthened with alumina), taking from about 170 W to 3400 W each, with peak power densities up to about 610 W/mm\(^2\) at normal incidence, and are water-cooled (Mulvany and colleagues, MEDSI 2020). The operator sees absorber and chamber temperatures from thermocouples and the cooling water’s temperature rise. The signatures of trouble: a thermocouple rising when the orbit moves (a mis-steered beam putting its fan on an unprotected surface), interlocks that dump the beam on orbit excursion at the insertion devices, and pressure. Photons striking the walls desorb gas, so a new ring’s pressure is high and falls as the integrated beam dose, in ampere-hours, accumulates: vacuum conditioning (chapter 03).

Beamline shutters and the hutch. Each beamline leaves the ring through a front end (figure 20.12) with photon shutters, which absorb the light’s power, and heavy-metal safety shutters, which stop gas bremsstrahlung: gamma rays from electrons scattering on residual gas, coming straight down the beamline. Further down, lead shielding stops what the first mirror scatters. The experiment sits in a lead-lined hutch interlocked to the shutters. The ESRF procedure for a crystallography hutch: press the start-search button outside, check the hutch is empty and press the search button inside, close the door, press the final search button outside; a siren sounds, and after about 30 seconds the hutch is interlocked. To get in, close the safety shutter and hold the open button for about 3 seconds. At the Canadian Light Source the panel lights turn green when the search succeeds, and only then can the front-end and hutch safety shutters be opened.

The electrons radiate along every tangent of their arc through a dipole, so the light leaves as a horizontal fan (blue, bounded by the tangents at the dipole’s entry and exit). Water-cooled absorbers catch the fan where it would hit the chamber, and a narrow slice goes down a beamline: through the front end’s photon shutter, which takes the power, and its heavy-metal safety shutter, which when closed also stops the gas bremsstrahlung coming straight down the line (dashed); then through the ring’s shielding wall to the first mirror, inside lead, and on to the sample in a lead-lined hutch whose door is interlocked to the shutters.

Where it stops

For an electron ring the end is the fourth power. LEP at 104 GeV lost 3.4 GeV a turn, needed 3.7 GV of superconducting RF, and radiated about 23 MW at its peak (Shiltsev and Zimmermann, 2021). At fixed radius each doubling of energy multiplies the loss by sixteen; to hold the loss per turn fixed the radius must grow as \(E^4\), and to hold the power fixed for a given beam current the radius must grow as \(E^4\) too. A ring for electrons at several hundred GeV becomes impractical. The way past is to stop bending: accelerate in a straight line, where (section 2) the radiation is negligible, and collide once. That is the linear collider of chapter 25, with its own price: no reuse of the beam, and therefore tiny beams at the collision point.

For light sources the limit is the diffraction limit. A ring’s emittance can be pushed down by more and smaller bends, but each step costs dynamic aperture and lifetime, and at \(\lambda/4\pi\) (8 pm rad at 1 Å) the light is already coherent and smaller emittance brings little. More fundamentally, the electrons in a ring radiate independently: the power goes as \(N\), the number of electrons, because their phases are random. If the electrons could be bunched on the scale of the wavelength itself, they would radiate in phase and the power would grow as \(N^2\). In a long undulator, a dense, short electron bunch from a linear accelerator does this to itself: the light it emits modulates the electrons’ energy, which bunches them at the light’s wavelength, which makes the light stronger. That is the free-electron laser, chapter 26.

Before any of that, every ring, for electrons or protons, has to be filled and emptied, and no single ring takes its beam from rest to full energy. The joins between machines are the subject of chapter 21.

Exercises

1. What is the vertical opening half-angle \(1/\gamma\) of the light from MAX IV (3 GeV), and how high is the fan of light 10 m from the source?

Answer

\(\gamma = 3\times10^9/510{,}999 = 5871\), so \(1/\gamma = 0.170\) mrad. At 10 m the full height is about \(2\times0.170\ \mathrm{mrad}\times10\ \mathrm{m} = 3.4\) mm. (This is the rough cone size; the actual vertical spread depends on photon energy, wider at long wavelengths.)

2. LEP ran at 45.6 GeV per beam for the Z boson. With \(\rho = 3026\) m, find \(U_0\), the fraction of the energy lost per turn, and the critical photon energy.

Answer

\(U_0 = 88.46\times45.6^4/3026\) keV \(= 126\) MeV, which is 0.28 per cent of 45.6 GeV. \(\varepsilon_c = 2.218\times45.6^3/3026\) keV \(= 69.5\) keV. Compare with 104 GeV: 3.42 GeV and 825 keV. The ratio of losses is \((104/45.6)^4 = 27\).

3. Show that for a bending magnet \(\varepsilon_c\,[\mathrm{keV}] = 0.665\,E^2\,[\mathrm{GeV}]\,B\,[\mathrm{T}]\) follows from \(\varepsilon_c = 3\hbar c\gamma^3/(2\rho)\), and find \(\lambda_c\) for MAX IV’s dipoles (\(\rho = 19.7\) m).

Answer

With \(\rho = p/(eB) \approx E/(ecB)\), \(\varepsilon_c = \frac{3}{2}\hbar c\gamma^3\,ecB/E = \frac{3}{2}\hbar e c^2 B\,\gamma^2/(m_e c^2)\), since \(\gamma = E/(m_e c^2)\). Putting in the constants for \(E = 1\) GeV and \(B = 1\) T gives 0.665 keV, and the dependence is \(E^2 B\). For MAX IV, \(\lambda_c = 0.559\times19.7/3^3\) nm \(= 0.41\) nm, a photon energy of 3.04 keV.

4. MAX IV: \(E = 3\) GeV, \(C = 528\) m, \(U_0 = 363.8\) keV, \(J_x = 1.847\). Find the three damping times in milliseconds and in turns.

Answer

\(T_0 = 528/c = 1.761\) µs. \(2ET_0/U_0 = 2\times3\times10^9\times1.761\times10^{-6}/363.8\times10^3\) s \(= 29.0\) ms. \(J_y = 1\) gives \(\tau_y = 29.0\) ms (16,500 turns); \(\tau_x = 29.0/1.847 = 15.7\) ms (8,900 turns); \(J_E = 4 - 1.847 - 1 = 1.153\) gives \(\tau_E = 25.2\) ms (14,300 turns).

5. The ESRF-EBS design gave \(U_0 = 2.61\) MeV, \(C = 843.978\) m, \(E = 6\) GeV and damping times of 8.5, 13 and 8.6 ms (horizontal, vertical, longitudinal). Check them against Robinson’s theorem.

Answer

\(2ET_0/U_0 = 2\times6\times10^9\times(843.978/c)/2.61\times10^6 = 12.9\) ms, which is \(\tau_y\) with \(J_y = 1\): agreed with 13 ms. Then \(J_x = 12.9/8.5 = 1.52\) and \(J_E = 12.9/8.6 = 1.51\), and \(J_x + J_y + J_E = 4.03\), equal to 4 within the rounding of the quoted times. As in MAX IV, gradient dipoles have moved damping from the longitudinal plane to the horizontal.

6. For the LHC at 7 TeV (\(\rho = 2803.95\) m, nominal 2808 bunches of \(1.15\times10^{11}\) protons, revolution frequency 11.245 kHz), find \(U_0\), \(\varepsilon_c\), the power per beam and the power per metre of dipole. Why is the beam screen at 5 to 20 K rather than at 1.9 K?

Answer

\(\gamma = 7000/0.938272 = 7461\). \(U_0 = e^2\gamma^4/(3\varepsilon_0\rho) = 6.66\) keV. \(\varepsilon_c = 3\hbar c\gamma^3/(2\rho) = 43.8\) eV. The current is \(2808\times1.15\times10^{11}\times11{,}245\times e = 0.582\) A, so each beam radiates \(6.66\ \mathrm{kV}\times0.582\ \mathrm{A} = 3.9\) kW, and spread over \(2\pi\rho = 17.6\) km of dipole, 0.22 W/m. Removing heat at temperature \(T\) costs at least \((300 - T)/T\) watts of work per watt: 157 at 1.9 K, but 59 at 5 K and 14 at 20 K. Intercepting the light at 5 to 20 K cuts the refrigeration bill several-fold and keeps the 1.9 K cold bore free to act as a cryopump.

7. BioMAX’s undulator has \(\lambda_u = 18\) mm and \(K = 2.19\) at 3 GeV. Find the peak field, the on-axis fundamental photon energy, and which odd harmonic lies nearest 12.4 keV at that \(K\).

Answer

\(B_0 = K/(0.934\,\lambda_u[\mathrm{cm}]) = 2.19/(0.934\times1.8) = 1.30\) T. \(\lambda_1 = (0.018/(2\times5871^2))(1 + 2.19^2/2) = 0.887\) nm, so \(\varepsilon_1 = 1.40\) keV. \(12.4/1.40 = 8.9\), so the ninth harmonic, at 12.6 keV, lies just above 12.4 keV at this \(K\). It cannot be tuned down onto 12.4 keV: that needs \(K = 2.21\), and 2.19 is the strongest the device reached (the value beside its minimum gap). Opening the gap lowers \(K\) and raises every harmonic, so 12.4 keV is reached instead with the seventh harmonic, by opening the gap to \(K = 1.83\) (from \(7\times1.40\times3.40/(1 + K^2/2) = 12.4\)).

8. Using \(\varepsilon_{x0} = F\,C_q\gamma^2\theta^3/J_x\), estimate the minimum emittance of the old ESRF (6 GeV, 32 double-bend cells) and of the EBS (the same 32 cells with seven dipoles each), both with \(J_x = 1\). Compare with 4 nm rad and 133 pm rad.

Answer

\(\gamma = 11{,}742\), \(C_q\gamma^2 = 5.28\times10^{-5}\) m. DBA: \(\theta = 2\pi/64\), \(F = 1/(4\sqrt{15}) = 0.0645\), giving 3.2 nm rad, against 4 nm rad achieved. 7BA: \(\theta = 2\pi/224\), \(F = \frac{1}{12\sqrt{15}}\cdot\frac{8}{6} = 0.0287\), giving 33 pm rad, against 133 pm rad. The ideal minimum falls by a factor of 96 (43 from \(\theta^3\), 2.25 from \(F\)); the real ring gained a factor of 30, from 4 nm rad to 133 pm rad, because the new lattice sits further above its minimum than the old one did.

9. Derive the vertical damping time \(\tau_y = 2ET_0/U_0\) from the statement that each turn reduces \(y'\) by the fraction \(U_0/E\) while leaving \(y\) unchanged.

Answer

The invariant is \(A^2 = \gamma_y y^2 + 2\alpha_y yy' + \beta_y y'^2\). With \(\Delta y' = -\epsilon y'\), \(\epsilon = U_0/E \ll 1\), to first order \(\Delta A^2 = -2\epsilon(\alpha_y yy' + \beta_y y'^2)\). Write \(y = A\sqrt{\beta_y}\cos\psi\), \(y' = -(A/\sqrt{\beta_y})(\sin\psi + \alpha_y\cos\psi)\). Then \(\alpha_y yy' + \beta_y y'^2 = A^2(\sin^2\psi + \alpha_y\sin\psi\cos\psi)\), whose average over \(\psi\) is \(A^2/2\). So on average \(\Delta A^2 = -\epsilon A^2\) per turn, \(dA^2/dt = -(U_0/(ET_0))A^2\), and \(A \propto e^{-t/\tau_y}\) with \(\tau_y = 2ET_0/U_0\).

10. Using \(\tau_p\,[\mathrm{s}] = 98.7\,\rho^3/E^5\), multiplied by \(R/\rho\), estimate the Sokolov-Ternov polarisation time of LEP at 45.6 GeV (\(\rho = 3026\) m, \(C = 26{,}658.9\) m), and say why it falls so steeply with energy.

Answer

\(\tau_p = 98.7\times3026^3/45.6^5 = 13{,}900\) s, 3.85 hours, for an isomagnetic ring; with \(R/\rho = 4243/3026 = 1.40\), 5.4 hours. The rate goes as \(\gamma^5/\rho^3\): the number of photons emitted per second goes as \(\gamma/\rho\) (section 6), and the probability that a photon flips the spin goes as the square of the photon’s energy compared with the electron’s, \((\varepsilon_c/E)^2 \propto (\gamma^2/\rho)^2\).
