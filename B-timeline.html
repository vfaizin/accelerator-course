Appendix B: a timeline

This appendix puts the history of the course in one place, in date order: first the mathematics and physics of Part 0, Foundations, from Cardano’s square roots of negative numbers in 1545, then the machines, from the discharge tubes of 1857 to the colliders running in 2026. It holds no new material. Every entry comes from a chapter, which gives the sources, the physics and the people at length; the last column of each table links to it, with F1 to F7 for the Foundations chapters. Read it as a map, then go to the chapter.

It ends with the numbers behind the Livingston plot of chapter 1: for each decade, the highest energy reached by each family of machines, and for colliders the energy a fixed-target machine would need to match them.

How to read the tables

  • Energies are kinetic energies \(W\) of one particle, in eV with prefixes, unless the entry says otherwise. For an ion the energy is that of the whole ion (190 MeV deuterons, not 95 MeV per nucleon), except where the entry says “per nucleon”.
  • Electrostatic machines are given by their terminal voltage. A singly charged ion from a single-ended machine gains \(W = eV\); a proton from a tandem gains \(2eV\) (chapter 6).
  • Colliders are given by their energy per beam and by the centre-of-mass energy \(\sqrt{s}\), the energy available to make new particles (chapter 23).
  • Dates are the first time the thing was done, published or switched on, as the entry says. Where accounts differ, both are given, and the section Where the accounts differ collects the cases.
  • A dash in the “Where” column means the chapter does not name the place.
  • Part 0 entries carry F1 to F7 in the last column. Those before 1857 have a table of their own, the first below; the later ones stand in date order among the machines.

What is plotted for a collider

A fixed-target machine and a collider are put on one axis by asking what beam energy a fixed-target machine would need to give the same centre-of-mass energy. Chapter 1, section 5, derives the invariant \(s\), the square of the total four-momentum, in units where it carries GeV\(^2\). For a beam particle of total energy \(E\) and mass \(m\) on an identical particle at rest,

\[ s = (E + mc^2)^2 - (pc)^2 = 2m^2c^4 + 2mc^2E, \]

using \(E^2 = (pc)^2 + m^2c^4\). Solving for \(E\),

\[ E_{eq} = \frac{s - 2m^2c^4}{2mc^2}. \]

For a symmetric collider with beams of energy \(E_b\) each, head on, \(\sqrt{s} = 2E_b\), so when \(E_b \gg mc^2\)

\[ E_{eq} \approx \frac{(2E_b)^2}{2mc^2} = \frac{2E_b^2}{mc^2}. \]

The equivalent energy grows as the square of the beam energy and inversely as the target’s mass. For the LHC of 2022, \(\sqrt{s} = 13.6\) TeV and \(m_pc^2 = 0.938\) GeV give \(E_{eq} = 9.86\times10^{16}\) eV, about 99 PeV: a fixed-target ring for that would need a beam 14,500 times the LHC’s. The approximation is excellent for every collider in the table; it would fail only near \(E_b \sim mc^2\).

The choice of target. For proton-proton and proton-antiproton colliders the target is a proton at rest. For electron-positron colliders this appendix uses an electron at rest, as a lepton fixed-target machine would. Palmer and Gallardo, extending Livingston’s plot in 1997, drew hadron and lepton machines on separate plots. The choice matters enormously: LEP’s \(\sqrt{s} = 209\) GeV corresponds to \(4.3\times10^{16}\) eV on an electron at rest but only \(2.3\times10^{13}\) eV for an electron beam on a proton at rest, because \(m_p/m_e = 1836\). Compare a collider with its own kind of fixed target. For HERA, an asymmetric electron-proton collider, the entry gives an electron beam on a proton at rest, from \(s = m_e^2c^4 + m_p^2c^4 + 2E_e E_p + 2p_e p_p c^2\) for head-on beams.

How the curve rose, and why it flattened

The rise. Taking the record-holding hadron machine in each year from the 1.22 MeV cyclotron of 1932 to the 1.8 TeV Tevatron collisions of 1986 (thirteen points: the cyclotrons, the 184-inch synchrocyclotron, the Cosmotron, Bevatron, Dubna, PS, AGS, U-70, ISR, SPS collider and Tevatron, with collider equivalents from the table), a straight-line fit to the logarithm of the energy against the year gives a factor of 43 per decade: tenfold every 6.1 years, for 54 years. Palmer and Gallardo’s endpoints, about \(10^5\) eV for a rectifier generator in 1930 and \(10^{15}\) eV for the Tevatron in 1988, give \(10^{10}\) in 58 years, a factor of 53 per decade; their text states “more than a factor of about 33 per decade”. The numbers differ with the points chosen, but the line is the same: an energy ten times higher every six or seven years.

The line was never one technology. Each family rose fast and then stopped at a limit set by physics, and the next family took over before the old one stalled:

  • Direct voltage stopped at some tens of MV, where insulation breaks down (chapter 4, chapter 6).
  • The cyclotron stopped at some tens of MeV for protons, when relativity put the particle out of step (chapter 10). The synchrocyclotron removed that and stopped near 1 GeV, because its magnet must fill the last orbit and its iron grows as the cube of the momentum (chapter 12).
  • The betatron stopped near 300 MeV, from the size of its core and from synchrotron radiation (chapter 11).
  • The weak-focusing synchrotron stopped near 10 GeV, where its aperture and magnet became unaffordable (chapter 15). Strong focusing shrank the aperture and took iron rings to hundreds of GeV in tunnels kilometres round (chapter 16).
  • Superconducting magnets doubled the field and took the ring to about 1 TeV per beam (chapter 17).
  • Colliders changed the measure. Because \(E_{eq}\) grows as the square of the beam energy, the ISR’s 31 GeV beams counted as 2 TeV on a fixed target, and the Tevatron’s 0.9 TeV beams as 1.7 PeV (chapter 23). Cooling made the antiproton beams for two of them (chapter 24).

The flattening. From the Tevatron’s \(1.73\times10^{15}\) eV in 1986 to the LHC’s \(9.86\times10^{16}\) eV in 2022 the equivalent energy rose 57 times in 36 years, a factor of 3.1 per decade against 43 before. In collision energy itself, \(\sqrt{s}\) went from 1.8 to 13.6 TeV, a factor of 1.75 per decade. Continued at 43 per decade, the fitted line reaches \(4.7\times10^{20}\) eV in 2022, about 4800 times the LHC. The reasons are in the chapters:

  • The field. For a hadron ring \(p = qB\rho\). The LHC’s niobium-titanium dipoles, cooled to 1.9 K, are designed for 8.33 T, four to five times the 1.6 to 2 T at which iron saturates (chapter 17). More energy means a larger ring, and the cost of tunnel, magnets and cryogenics grows with its size. The next step in field, Nb\(_3\)Sn, reaches an accelerator for the first time in the HL-LHC quadrupoles.
  • Money and politics. ISABELLE was cancelled in 1983 and the SSC, about 87 km round for 20 TeV per beam, in 1993 after about 2 billion US dollars had been spent (chapter 29). The SSC’s 40 TeV collisions would have given \(8.5\times10^{17}\) eV equivalent, which the fitted line reaches only in 2005; Palmer and Gallardo remarked that, built on schedule, it would have been a decade ahead of the line. A frontier collider became a project of decades and many nations.
  • Radiation, for electrons. An electron’s loss per turn grows as \(E^4/\rho\); LEP at 104 GeV lost about 3.4 GeV a turn, and a ring’s cost grows as \(E^2\) at best (chapter 20, chapter 25). Linear colliders avoid the loss but must make nanometre beams (chapter 25). No lepton collider has passed LEP’s 209 GeV.
  • Luminosity first. The cross-sections of the reactions physicists want fall with energy, so after 1990 much of the effort went into luminosity rather than energy. The ISR’s record of \(1.4\times10^{32}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\) in 1982 had become \(2.1\times10^{34}\) at the LHC in 2018 and \(5.3\times10^{34}\) at SuperKEKB in 2026 (chapter 22, chapter 23). A plot of luminosity against year has kept rising while the Livingston plot levelled.
  • The next family. Plasma fields of tens of GV/m and muons, which radiate far less than electrons, are the candidates for a new curve; neither has yet made a beam for a collider (chapter 27).

The envelope rose for half a century because each family met its wall only after the next family had been invented. Since the 1980s the next family has not been ready in time.
