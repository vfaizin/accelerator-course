F6 Heat, gases and probability

1654 to 1877: Pascal and Fermat’s dice, the bell curve of de Moivre, Laplace and Gauss, Poisson’s rare events; Joule’s heat as energy, Clausius’s entropy, and the molecules of Maxwell and Boltzmann. The mathematics of chance and the physics of hot and cold, which turn out to be one subject. The course needs both for the gas in a vacuum chamber, the electrons boiled off a cathode, the spread of particles in a beam, the scatter of every measurement, and the price of cold.

What this chapter assumes. High-school mathematics and physics, F1 Calculus (derivatives, integrals, the exponential, integration by parts, exponential decay) and F2 Vectors and vector calculus (integrals over areas in polar coordinates, section 13). From F3 Newtonian mechanics it uses only momentum \(mv\) and Newton’s second law in the form force = rate of change of momentum, which high-school physics already gives.

What comes next. Chapter 1’s pre-requisites on counting statistics (Poisson) and errors and their propagation, chapter 2’s on the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution and mean free path and collision rates, and all of chapter 3 take each section here further. F7 Atoms, nuclei and quanta explains why heat capacities fall at low temperature.

A note on symbols. Sections 1 to 8 are about chance, sections 9 to 16 about gases, and a few letters change meaning between them, as they do between the books on each. In sections 1 to 8, \(p\) is a probability, \(n\) a number of trials and \(P(\ldots)\) “the probability of”. From section 9 on, \(p\) is a pressure in pascals and \(n\) a number density in m\(^{-3}\). \(\sigma\) is a standard deviation everywhere except in section 14, where it is also a collision cross-section, as in chapter 3. \(Q\) is a quantity of heat in joules. The course keeps \(W\) for kinetic energy, so work is written \(W_{by}\) (done by a gas) or \(W_{el}\) (electrical work fed to a refrigerator). \(T\) is always a temperature in kelvin.

In one page

The problem. A litre of air holds \(2.5 \times 10^{22}\) molecules, each moving at hundreds of metres per second and changing direction billions of times a second. Newton’s laws apply to every one of them, but nobody can follow them all, and nobody needs to: a pressure gauge reads one steady number. In the same way, a radioactive source sends out alphas at random moments, a cathode emits electrons at random, and a beam holds \(10^{10}\) particles each a little off the ideal path. A measurement repeated gives a slightly different answer each time. Physics needs a way to say exact things about quantities that are random in detail.

The idea. Give up predicting one molecule, one decay or one reading, and predict averages and spreads instead. Probability supplies the rules: how chances combine, what the mean and the standard deviation of a random quantity are, and three shapes of distribution that keep appearing, the Gaussian (the bell curve), the Poisson (counts of rare events) and the exponential (waiting times and free paths). The central limit theorem says why the Gaussian is everywhere: anything that is the sum of many small independent random pushes ends up Gaussian. Applied to a gas, the same rules turn molecular chaos into the ideal gas law \(p = nk_BT\), show that temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the molecules, and give the Boltzmann factor \(e^{-E/k_BT}\), which says how rarely a molecule or an electron has an energy \(E\) far above \(k_BT\). Heat is energy passed on by those random motions; entropy counts how spread out it is, and the second law says it never spontaneously gathers again. That law sets the price of cold.

Equations to remember.

  • Independent events: \(P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A)\,P(B)\). Exclusive events: \(P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)\).
  • Binomial: the probability of \(k\) successes in \(n\) independent trials, each with probability \(p\), is \(P(k) = \dbinom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\), with mean \(np\) and variance \(np(1-p)\).
  • Mean \(\mu = \langle x\rangle\), variance \(\sigma^2 = \langle(x - \mu)^2\rangle = \langle x^2\rangle - \mu^2\), standard deviation \(\sigma\) (same units as \(x\)).
  • Gaussian: \(f(x) = \dfrac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-(x-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2}\); 68.3, 95.4 and 99.73 per cent within \(1\sigma\), \(2\sigma\), \(3\sigma\); full width at half maximum \(= 2.355\,\sigma\). From \(\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}dx = \sqrt\pi\).
  • Poisson: \(P(N) = \dfrac{\mu^N e^{-\mu}}{N!}\), mean \(\mu\), standard deviation \(\sqrt\mu\). A count of \(N\) is uncertain by about \(\sqrt N\).
  • Errors: independent errors add in quadrature, \(\sigma_{x+y} = \sqrt{\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2}\); the mean of \(N\) readings has error \(\sigma/\sqrt N\).
  • Ideal gas: \(pV = Nk_BT = \nu RT\), or \(p = nk_BT\); \(p\) in Pa, \(V\) in m\(^3\), \(N\) molecules, \(\nu\) moles, \(n = N/V\) in m\(^{-3}\), \(T\) in K, \(k_B = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}\) J/K, \(R = N_Ak_B = 8.314\) J/(mol K).
  • Kinetic theory: \(p = \tfrac13 nm\langle v^2\rangle\), so \(\langle\tfrac12 mv^2\rangle = \tfrac32 k_BT\); \(v_{rms} = \sqrt{3k_BT/m}\); mean speed \(\bar v = \sqrt{8k_BT/\pi m}\); each quadratic term in the energy holds \(\tfrac12 k_BT\) on average.
  • Boltzmann factor: the probability of a state of energy \(E\) goes as \(e^{-E/k_BT}\).
  • Wall collision rate: \(\Phi = \tfrac14 n\bar v\) per m\(^2\) per second. Mean free path: \(\lambda = 1/(\sqrt2\,n\sigma)\), with \(\sigma = \pi d^2\) for molecules of diameter \(d\).
  • First law: \(\Delta U = Q - W_{by}\). Second law: entropy \(S\) never falls in an isolated system; heat \(Q\) entering at temperature \(T\) brings entropy \(Q/T\). Least work to pump heat \(Q\) from \(T\) to room temperature \(T_a\): \(W_{el} = Q\,(T_a - T)/T\).

Numbers worth knowing.

  • \(k_BT = 0.02526\) eV at 20 °C (293.15 K), 0.2154 eV at a 2500 K tungsten cathode, \(3.88 \times 10^{-4}\) eV at 4.5 K; 1 eV corresponds to 11 604.5 K.
  • At 20 °C a gas holds \(2.47 \times 10^{25}\) molecules per m\(^3\) at \(10^5\) Pa and \(2.47 \times 10^{14}\) per m\(^3\) at \(10^{-6}\) Pa. A mole of it fills 24.06 litres at atmospheric pressure.
  • Nitrogen at 20 °C: mean speed 471 m/s, rms speed 511 m/s. Hydrogen: 1755 m/s mean.
  • Nitrogen at \(10^{-6}\) Pa strikes each square metre of wall \(2.9 \times 10^{16}\) times a second, enough to cover a clean surface in about 6 minutes. The mean free path of air at that pressure is 6.7 km.
  • A Gaussian beam has 31.7 per cent of its particles more than \(1\sigma\) from the centre in one plane, and 0.27 per cent beyond \(3\sigma\).
  • A count of 100 is uncertain by 10 per cent; for 1 per cent, count \(10^4\).
  • At the 4.53 eV work function of tungsten, the Boltzmann factor is \(7.4 \times 10^{-10}\) at 2500 K and \(10^{-78}\) at room temperature.
  • Removing 1 W at 4.5 K needs at least 65.7 W of work at 300 K; real plants use 235 to 280 W. One watt into liquid helium boils off about 1.4 litres an hour.

The problem it solved

Dice, and the bell curve

In 1654 the Chevalier de Méré put gambling questions to Blaise Pascal, among them how to divide the stakes of a game broken off before the end. Pascal’s letters with Pierre de Fermat on these questions began the mathematics of chance: count the equally likely cases, and the probability is the fraction that win. Jacob Bernoulli’s Ars Conjectandi, published in 1713 after his death, proved the first law of large numbers: over many trials, the fraction of successes settles on the probability. Abraham de Moivre showed in 1733 that the binomial distribution of many coin tosses approaches a smooth bell-shaped curve. Pierre-Simon Laplace generalised this around 1810 to sums of almost any small random quantities, the first form of the central limit theorem, and set out the whole subject in his Théorie analytique des probabilités (1812). Meanwhile Carl Friedrich Gauss, in his Theoria motus of 1809 on the orbits of planets, took the same curve as the law of errors of measurement and from it justified the method of least squares. Physicists still call the curve “Gaussian”; statisticians call it “normal”.

Rare events

Siméon Denis Poisson, in a book of 1837 on the probability of judgements in courts, worked out the limit of the binomial distribution when there are very many chances, each very small: the Poisson distribution. It waited seventy years for its most famous physical test. In 1910 Ernest Rutherford and Hans Geiger counted the scintillations of alpha particles from a source on a screen in 2608 intervals of 7.5 seconds, 10 097 alphas in all, and the mathematician Harry Bateman showed that the numbers of intervals with 0, 1, 2, … alphas followed Poisson’s law. Radioactive decay was random, event by event.

Heat is motion

Around 1800 most chemists thought of heat as a fluid, caloric, that flowed from hot bodies to cold. Count Rumford in 1798 noticed that boring a cannon made heat without limit, which a conserved fluid could not do. Sadi Carnot in 1824, still thinking in terms of caloric, asked how much work a heat engine could give, and found that the answer depends only on the temperatures between which it works. Between 1840 and 1850 James Prescott Joule measured the heat made by falling weights turning a paddle wheel in water, by electric currents and by compressing gas, and found always the same exchange rate: heat is energy. Julius Robert Mayer (1842) and Hermann von Helmholtz (1847) stated the conservation of energy in general. Rudolf Clausius (1850) and William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin (1851), reconciled Carnot with Joule and stated the second law; Thomson had already proposed in 1848 a temperature scale with an absolute zero. Clausius named entropy in 1865.

Molecules

Robert Boyle found in 1662 that squeezing air raises its pressure in proportion. Daniel Bernoulli in his Hydrodynamica of 1738 explained this as the impacts of many particles on the walls, an idea ignored for a century. Clausius revived it in 1857, and in 1858, answering the objection that fast molecules should make smells cross a room at once, introduced the mean free path. James Clerk Maxwell found the distribution of molecular speeds in 1860. Ludwig Boltzmann generalised it from 1868 to the factor \(e^{-E/k_BT}\), and in 1877 connected entropy with the number of ways a state can be made, which turned the second law into a statement of probability. The constant \(k_B\) is named after him. Since 2019 the kelvin itself is defined by fixing \(k_B\) at exactly \(1.380649 \times 10^{-23}\) J/K.

1. Chance: outcomes and probability

The picture. Throw a die. Nothing in high-school physics forbids predicting the result from the throw, but the result depends on details far too fine to control. What can be said is that in a long run of throws each face comes up about one time in six. Probability is the language for that kind of statement.

Definitions. A trial (a throw, a decay, a molecule hitting a wall) has a set of possible outcomes. An event is a set of outcomes: “the die shows a six”, or “shows an even number”. The probability \(P(A)\) of an event \(A\) is the fraction of a very long run of trials in which \(A\) happens. It lies between 0 (never) and 1 (always). When the outcomes are equally likely, as for a fair die, it is the number of outcomes in \(A\) divided by the number of outcomes: \(P(\text{six}) = 1/6\), \(P(\text{even}) = 3/6 = 1/2\).

Three rules.

  • Not. Either \(A\) happens or it does not, so \(P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)\).
  • Or, for exclusive events. If \(A\) and \(B\) cannot both happen (a six and a five on the same die), then \(P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)\).
  • And, for independent events. \(A\) and \(B\) are independent if knowing that one happened does not change the chance of the other: two different dice, or two different atoms decaying. Then \(P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A)\,P(B)\). In a long run, \(A\) happens in a fraction \(P(A)\) of trials, and of those, \(B\) happens in a fraction \(P(B)\).

Example: de Méré’s bet. What is the chance of at least one six in four throws of a die? “At least one” is easiest through its opposite. The chance of no six in one throw is \(5/6\); the four throws are independent, so the chance of no six in four is \((5/6)^4 = 0.4823\), and the chance of at least one six is \(1 - 0.4823 = 0.5177\), a little better than even. The same reasoning for at least one double six in 24 throws of two dice gives \(1 - (35/36)^{24} = 0.4914\), a little worse. De Méré had noticed, from losing money, that the second bet was not as good as the first.

Example: a detector in layers. A particle detector has three layers, each of which fires for a passing particle with probability 0.95, independently. A track is recognised only if all three fire: probability \(0.95^3 = 0.857\). One particle in seven is missed, though each layer misses only one in twenty. Chances multiply, and losses compound.

Where independence fails. The “and” rule needs independence. Two readings of the same gauge taken a second apart are not independent if the gauge drifts; the molecules in a liquid are not independent because they push on one another. Assuming independence when it does not hold is one of the commonest mistakes in physics (section 8 returns to it).

2. Counting ways: the binomial distribution

The question. Twenty electrons cross a short gap of gas, and each, independently, has a probability \(p = 0.05\) of hitting a molecule. What is the chance that exactly \(k\) of them hit?

Counting. Take \(k = 2\). One way is for electrons 1 and 2 to hit and the other 18 not: probability \(p^2(1-p)^{18}\), by the “and” rule. Any other choice of which two hit has the same probability. These choices are exclusive, so the “or” rule adds them: the answer is \(p^2(1-p)^{18}\) times the number of ways of choosing 2 electrons from 20.

The number of ways. Choosing \(k\) things from \(n\) in order can be done in \(n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)\) ways (\(n\) choices for the first, \(n - 1\) for the second, and so on). Each set of \(k\) is counted \(k!\) times, once for each order. So the number of distinct sets is

\[ \binom{n}{k} = \frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{k!} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}, \]

read “\(n\) choose \(k\)”. For example \(\binom{20}{2} = 190\) and \(\binom{10}{5} = 252\). These are the coefficients of the binomial theorem of high school, \((a + b)^n = \sum_k \binom{n}{k}a^kb^{n-k}\), hence the name.

The binomial distribution. The probability of exactly \(k\) successes in \(n\) independent trials, each with probability \(p\), is

\[ P(k) = \binom{n}{k}p^k(1 - p)^{n-k}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \ldots, n . \]

By the binomial theorem with \(a = p\) and \(b = 1 - p\), the probabilities add to \((p + 1 - p)^n = 1\), as they must: something happens.

The electrons. With \(n = 20\) and \(p = 0.05\):

\(k\) 0 1 2 3 4
\(P(k)\) 0.3585 0.3774 0.1887 0.0596 0.0133

On average one electron in twenty hits (section 3 proves the mean is \(np = 1\)), but in more than a third of crossings none does, and in a quarter (0.264) two or more do. An average says little about any one trial.

Ten coins. With \(n = 10\) and \(p = \tfrac12\), the chance of exactly five heads is \(252/2^{10} = 0.246\). The most likely result happens less than a quarter of the time. As \(n\) grows, the chance of exactly half falls, but the fraction of heads gathers ever more tightly round one half: Bernoulli’s law of large numbers, made precise in section 3.

3. Random variables: mean, variance and standard deviation

Random variables. A random variable is a number that comes out of a trial: the face of a die, the number of electrons that hit, the reading of a gauge. Its distribution gives the probability \(P(x)\) of each value \(x\). Two numbers summarise a distribution: where it is centred and how wide it is.

The mean. The mean, expectation or average is

\[ \mu = \langle x\rangle = \sum_x x\,P(x) . \]

The angle brackets mean “average over the distribution”, the convention used in the course’s pre-requisites. In a long run of \(N\) trials, the value \(x\) turns up about \(NP(x)\) times, so the ordinary average of the results tends to \(\langle x\rangle\). For a die, \(\langle x\rangle = (1 + 2 + \cdots + 6)/6 = 3.5\), which is never thrown: a mean need not be a possible value.

The variance and standard deviation. How far do values typically fall from the mean? The average of \(x - \mu\) is zero (the positive and negative deviations cancel), so average its square instead. The variance is

\[ \sigma^2 = \big\langle(x - \mu)^2\big\rangle = \langle x^2\rangle - \mu^2, \]

the second form following from expanding the square: \(\langle x^2 - 2\mu x + \mu^2\rangle = \langle x^2\rangle - 2\mu^2 + \mu^2\). Its square root \(\sigma\), the standard deviation, has the units of \(x\) and is the typical size of a deviation. It is also called the rms (root mean square) deviation. For a die, \(\langle x^2\rangle = 91/6\), so \(\sigma^2 = 91/6 - 3.5^2 = 35/12 = 2.917\) and \(\sigma = 1.708\).

Rules for sums. For any two random variables, the mean of a sum is the sum of the means: \(\langle x + y\rangle = \langle x\rangle + \langle y\rangle\). For independent ones, the variances also add:

\[ \sigma_{x+y}^2 = \big\langle(\delta x + \delta y)^2\big\rangle = \langle\delta x^2\rangle + 2\langle\delta x\,\delta y\rangle + \langle\delta y^2\rangle = \sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2, \]

where \(\delta x = x - \mu_x\) and \(\delta y = y - \mu_y\) are the deviations. The middle term vanishes because, for independent variables, a positive \(\delta x\) is as likely to come with a negative \(\delta y\) as with a positive one, so \(\langle\delta x\,\delta y\rangle = \langle\delta x\rangle\langle\delta y\rangle = 0\). Standard deviations do not add; their squares do. Also, multiplying a variable by a constant \(a\) multiplies its mean by \(a\) and its variance by \(a^2\).

The binomial’s mean and variance. A binomial count is the sum of \(n\) independent trials, each scoring 1 with probability \(p\) and 0 otherwise. One trial has mean \(p\) and \(\langle x^2\rangle = p\), so variance \(p - p^2 = p(1-p)\). Adding \(n\) of them,

\[ \langle k\rangle = np, \qquad \sigma_k^2 = np(1 - p) . \]

For the twenty electrons, \(\langle k\rangle = 1\) and \(\sigma_k = \sqrt{0.95} = 0.975\).

The law of large numbers. The fraction of successes \(k/n\) has mean \(p\) and standard deviation \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\). It shrinks as \(1/\sqrt n\): a hundred times more trials pin the fraction down ten times more tightly. This \(1/\sqrt n\) is everywhere in measurement, and section 8 meets it again.

4. Continuous distributions: probability density

The picture. The position of an electron leaving a cathode, the speed of a molecule, the distance a molecule flies before a collision: these take any value in a range, not a list of separate values. The chance of any one exact value, say a speed of exactly 471.000… m/s, is zero. What makes sense is the chance of a value in a small range.

Probability density. A continuous random variable \(x\) has a probability density \(f(x)\), defined so that the probability of a value between \(x\) and \(x + dx\) is \(f(x)\,dx\). Over a finite range,

\[ P(a < x < b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx, \]

the area under the curve. The density has units of 1/(units of \(x\)): a density of speeds is in s/m. It must be normalised, \(\int f\,dx = 1\) over all values. Means and variances become integrals, the sums of section 3 with \(P(x)\) replaced by \(f(x)\,dx\):

\[ \mu = \int x f(x)\,dx, \qquad \sigma^2 = \int (x - \mu)^2 f(x)\,dx . \]

A histogram of measured values, the number of values in each bin divided by the total and by the bin width, approaches \(f(x)\) as the number of values grows and the bins narrow.

The uniform distribution. Equal density over a range of width \(w\) and zero outside: \(f = 1/w\). Centred on zero, the mean is 0 and

\[ \sigma^2 = \int_{-w/2}^{w/2} x^2\,\frac{dx}{w} = \frac{1}{w}\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{-w/2}^{w/2} = \frac{w^2}{12}, \qquad \sigma = \frac{w}{\sqrt{12}} . \]

Example. A silicon strip detector with strips 50 μm apart records only which strip a particle crossed. The particle could have been anywhere across the strip, uniformly, so the error of the position is \(50/\sqrt{12} = 14.4\) μm, not 50 μm and not 25 μm. Every digital reading has this error: a meter that shows whole millivolts has an rms rounding error of \(1/\sqrt{12} = 0.29\) mV.

The exponential distribution. The waiting time to a radioactive decay, and the distance a molecule flies before it hits another, have the density

\[ f(x) = \frac{1}{\lambda}\,e^{-x/\lambda}, \qquad x \ge 0 . \]

Why? A molecule flying through a gas has the same small chance \(dx/\lambda\) of a collision in each step \(dx\), whatever it has done before (the gas has no memory). Of \(N\) molecules setting out, \(dN = -N\,dx/\lambda\) collide in \(dx\), which is F1’s decay equation, so the number still flying after \(x\) is \(N_0e^{-x/\lambda}\). The fraction that stop in \(dx\) at \(x\) is \(e^{-x/\lambda}\,dx/\lambda\), which is \(f(x)\,dx\). Its mean is \(\int_0^\infty x e^{-x/\lambda}\,dx/\lambda = \lambda\) (integration by parts, F1 section 7), so \(\lambda\) is the mean free path; its standard deviation is also \(\lambda\). The probability of flying further than \(L\) is \(e^{-L/\lambda}\).

Example. In air at 1 Pa and 20 °C the mean free path of a molecule is 6.65 mm (section 14). The fraction of molecules that fly more than 1 cm without a collision is \(e^{-10/6.65} = 0.22\). Chapter 2 uses the same law to find what fraction of a tube’s electrons cross it unscattered.

5. The Gaussian distribution

The shape. The bell curve is

\[ f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), \]

centred on \(\mu\), falling off on both sides, and with \(\sigma\) setting the width. It describes the horizontal positions of the particles in most beams, the velocities of molecules along one direction (chapter 2), and the scatter of most measurements. Section 7 says why it turns up so often. First, the constant in front.

The Gaussian integral. Normalising \(f\) needs \(I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}\,dx\). F1 noted that \(e^{-x^2}\) has no antiderivative made of ordinary functions. The trick, usually credited to Poisson, is to square it. Write the second copy with a different letter:

\[ I^2 = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}dx\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-y^2}dy = \iint e^{-(x^2 + y^2)}\,dx\,dy , \]

an integral over the whole \((x, y)\) plane. The integrand depends only on \(r^2 = x^2 + y^2\), the squared distance from the origin, so change to polar coordinates (F2, section 13), with \(dA = r\,dr\,d\phi\):

\[ I^2 = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}\,r\,dr\,d\phi = 2\pi\left[-\tfrac12 e^{-r^2}\right]_0^\infty = \pi . \]

The extra factor \(r\) from the area of a thin ring is exactly what makes the integral easy (substitute \(u = r^2\)). So

\[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}\,dx = \sqrt\pi . \]

Normalising, and checking \(\sigma\). In \(f\), substitute \(x = \mu + \sqrt2\,\sigma t\), so \(dx = \sqrt2\,\sigma\,dt\): \(\int f\,dx = \dfrac{\sqrt2\,\sigma}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{-t^2}dt = \dfrac{\sqrt\pi}{\sqrt\pi} = 1\). The variance is

\[ \int (x - \mu)^2 f\,dx = \frac{2\sigma^2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t^2e^{-t^2}\,dt = \frac{2\sigma^2}{\sqrt\pi}\cdot\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2} = \sigma^2, \]

using integration by parts with \(u = t\) and \(dv = te^{-t^2}dt\): \(\int t^2e^{-t^2}dt = \big[-\tfrac12 te^{-t^2}\big] + \tfrac12\int e^{-t^2}dt = \tfrac12\sqrt\pi\). So the \(\sigma\) in the formula is the standard deviation, as the notation promised.

The 68/95/99.7 rule. The fraction within \(k\) standard deviations of the mean has no simple formula; it is tabulated as the error function (chapter 2’s special functions). Three values are worth knowing by heart:

Range Inside Outside
\(\mu \pm 1\sigma\) 68.27 % 31.73 %
\(\mu \pm 2\sigma\) 95.45 % 4.55 %
\(\mu \pm 3\sigma\) 99.73 % 0.27 %

So a value 3σ away from what was expected happens by chance about 3 times in a thousand. Physicists usually ask for 5σ before claiming a discovery, a chance of about 3 in 10 million on one side.

Full width at half maximum. The peak height is \(1/(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})\). A screen or an oscilloscope shows a peak, and its width is easiest to read halfway up. The curve falls to half its peak where \(e^{-(x-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2} = \tfrac12\), at \(x - \mu = \pm\sigma\sqrt{2\ln 2}\), so

\[ \text{FWHM} = 2\sqrt{2\ln 2}\;\sigma = 2.355\,\sigma . \]

Example: a beam on a screen. A beam profile monitor shows a horizontal profile with a full width at half maximum of 4.7 mm. If the beam is Gaussian, \(\sigma_x = 4.7/2.355 = 2.0\) mm, and 0.27 per cent of the particles lie more than 6 mm from the centre. Accelerator physicists quote beam sizes as \(\sigma\); experimenters reading a screen often quote FWHM. Mixing them up is an error of a factor 2.355.

A round beam. For a beam that is Gaussian in both \(x\) and \(y\) with the same \(\sigma\), the density in the plane is the product of two Gaussians, \(\propto e^{-r^2/2\sigma^2}\). The fraction outside a radius \(r\) is then the integral of the squaring trick above, without the infinity: \(\int_r^\infty e^{-r'^2/2\sigma^2}r'\,dr'/\sigma^2 = e^{-r^2/2\sigma^2}\). Outside \(1\sigma\) lies 60.7 per cent, outside \(2\sigma\) 13.5 per cent, outside \(3\sigma\) 1.1 per cent: four times the one-plane figure, because a particle can be far out in \(x\), in \(y\), or both. A round aperture of radius \(3\sigma\) scrapes off 1.1 per cent of such a beam. Chapter 7’s beam temperature and chapter 22 work with these beams.

6. Counting random events: the Poisson distribution

The question. An alpha source sends on average \(\mu = 3.87\) alphas to a screen in each interval of 7.5 seconds. What is the chance of exactly \(N\) in a given interval? The source holds an enormous number \(n\) of atoms, each with a tiny chance \(p\) of sending an alpha to the screen in the interval, with \(np = \mu\). The count is binomial with \(n\) huge and \(p\) tiny.

The limit. Write the binomial with \(p = \mu/n\):

\[ P(N) = \frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n - N + 1)}{N!}\,\frac{\mu^N}{n^N}\left(1 - \frac{\mu}{n}\right)^{n - N} . \]

Now let \(n \to \infty\) with \(\mu\) and \(N\) fixed. The fraction \(n(n-1)\cdots(n-N+1)/n^N\) is a product of \(N\) factors each tending to 1; \((1 - \mu/n)^{-N} \to 1\); and \((1 - \mu/n)^n \to e^{-\mu}\) (section 0). What is left is the Poisson distribution:

\[ P(N) = \frac{\mu^N e^{-\mu}}{N!}, \qquad N = 0, 1, 2, \ldots \]

It depends only on the mean \(\mu\). Its probabilities add to \(e^{-\mu}\sum\mu^N/N! = e^{-\mu}e^{\mu} = 1\) (the series of \(e^\mu\), F1 section 8). Its mean is \(\mu\), and its variance is the limit of \(np(1 - p)\) as \(p \to 0\), which is also \(\mu\):

\[ \langle N\rangle = \mu, \qquad \sigma_N = \sqrt\mu . \]

How good is the limit? For \(n = 1000\) and \(p = 0.003\) (mean 3), the binomial gives \(P(0) = 0.0496\) and \(P(3) = 0.2244\); Poisson gives 0.0498 and 0.2240.

Rutherford and Geiger’s alphas. With \(\mu = 10\,097/2608 = 3.872\), the expected numbers of intervals with \(N\) alphas, \(2608\,P(N)\), against those counted in 1910:

\(N\) 0 1 2 3 4 5 6 7
Poisson 54 210 407 525 508 394 254 141
Counted 57 203 383 525 532 408 273 139

The differences are about the size of the square root of each number, as they should be if the counts in each column are themselves random.

The square-root rule. In practice the mean is unknown and one count \(N\) is all there is. The best estimate of \(\mu\) is \(N\), and its uncertainty is about \(\sqrt N\). The relative uncertainty is \(1/\sqrt N\): a count of 100 is known to 10 per cent, a count of \(10^4\) to 1 per cent. To halve the error, count four times as long. This is why the observers of chapter 1 sat for hours at their microscopes (counting scintillations).

Nothing happens. The chance of zero events is \(e^{-\mu}\). If a background counter averages 2.3 counts per second, a second with no counts at all happens 10 per cent of the time. Turned round: if a careful search finds nothing, the mean is probably below about 2.3, since a larger mean would give zero counts less than 10 per cent of the time.

Where Poisson fails. The events must be independent and the rate steady. A detector that is dead for a microsecond after each count, a source that is decaying away during the count, or a beam that arrives in bunches, each break the rule; chapter 1’s pre-requisite on counting statistics goes on to counting times, background and dead time.

7. Why the bell curve is everywhere: the central limit theorem

The statement. Add up many independent random quantities, none of which dominates. Whatever their individual distributions, the distribution of the sum approaches a Gaussian, with mean the sum of the means and variance the sum of the variances (section 3). This is the central limit theorem. A proof needs more mathematics than this chapter has (it is given in the probability texts listed in the sources); a demonstration shows that it is true and how fast it works.

A demonstration with dice. One die is flat: each face has probability \(1/6\), and the shape is nothing like a bell. Its mean is 3.5 and its \(\sigma\) is 1.708. Throw ten dice and add them. The sum has mean 35 and \(\sigma = \sqrt{10 \times 35/12} = 5.40\). Counting all \(6^{10}\) equally likely outcomes exactly:

Exact, ten dice Gaussian with \(\mu = 35\), \(\sigma = 5.40\)
Probability of a sum of exactly 35 0.0727 0.0739 (the peak density \(\times\) 1)
Within \(\pm 1\sigma\) (30 to 40) 0.687 0.683
Within \(\pm 2\sigma\) (25 to 45) 0.950 0.954

Ten flat distributions have already made a bell to within a per cent. (Twelve numbers drawn uniformly between 0 and 1 and added make a sum with \(\mu = 6\) and \(\sigma = 1\); 67.9 per cent of the sums fall within \(\pm 1\) and 95.5 per cent within \(\pm 2\). Programs used to make Gaussian random numbers this way.)

Poisson becomes Gaussian. A Poisson count with a large mean is the sum of many small independent counts (the counts in each tenth of the interval, say), so it too becomes Gaussian, with \(\sigma = \sqrt\mu\). For \(\mu = 100\) the probability of a count between 90 and 110 is 0.707, close to the Gaussian 0.683 for \(\pm 1\sigma\) (the difference comes mostly from counting both end values, 90 and 110). So for large counts, the 68/95/99.7 rule applies to \(\sqrt N\) errors.

Why it matters. A particle in a beam receives many small independent kicks: from the noise of the source, from scattering on gas, from the random emission of photons in an electron ring (chapter 20). Its position is the sum of all of them, so beams settle into Gaussian profiles. A measurement error is often the sum of many small disturbances (electrical noise, vibration, temperature drift), so measurement errors tend to be Gaussian too. The theorem fails when one contribution dominates (then the sum looks like that contribution), when the contributions are not independent, or when a distribution has tails so long that its variance is infinite.

8. Measurements and their errors

Random and systematic. Every measurement differs from the true value. A random error changes from one repetition to the next, as likely high as low: electrical noise on a current reading, the Poisson scatter of a count. Repeating and averaging reduces it. A systematic error is the same every time: a gauge calibrated for nitrogen reading a chamber full of hydrogen, a ruler that has stretched, a current transformer whose gain is 2 per cent off. Repeating does not reduce it, and the scatter of the readings does not reveal it. It is found only by checking the instrument against a different method or a known standard. Quote the two separately when they matter.

The best value and its scatter. Ten readings of a beam current, in mA: 10.3, 9.8, 10.1, 10.4, 9.7, 10.0, 10.2, 9.9, 10.5, 10.1. The best estimate of the true current is their mean, \(\bar x = 10.10\) mA. The scatter of a single reading is estimated by the sample standard deviation

\[ s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N - 1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i - \bar x)^2} = 0.258\ \text{mA} . \]

Dividing by \(N - 1\) rather than \(N\) corrects for using \(\bar x\), which was itself fitted to these readings and so sits a little closer to them than the true value does. For large \(N\) the difference does not matter.

The error of a mean. The mean is the sum of \(N\) independent readings divided by \(N\). The variance of the sum is \(N\sigma^2\) (variances add), and dividing by \(N\) divides the variance by \(N^2\), so

\[ \sigma_{\bar x} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt N} . \]

For the ten readings, \(\sigma_{\bar x} = 0.258/\sqrt{10} = 0.082\) mA, and the result is \(10.10 \pm 0.08\) mA. Averaging a hundred readings would bring it to about 0.026 mA, but only for the random part: a 2 per cent calibration error (0.2 mA) stays at 0.2 mA however long one averages. That is why careful measurements spend as much effort on calibration as on statistics.

Adding errors in quadrature. When a result is the sum or difference of independent measured quantities, their variances add (section 3):

\[ \sigma_{x \pm y} = \sqrt{\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2} . \]

Errors add like the sides of a right-angled triangle, “in quadrature”. Two errors of 3 and 4 make 5, not 7. The larger error dominates quickly: an error of 1 and one of 3 make 3.16. Example. A count of 400 with a measured background of 100 gives a signal of \(300 \pm \sqrt{400 + 100} = 300 \pm 22\): subtracting the background adds its error. For products and ratios, relative errors add in quadrature in the same way, and for a power \(x^n\) the relative error is multiplied by \(n\) (F1, section 5). Chapter 1’s pre-requisite on errors and their propagation derives the general rule with partial derivatives, and chapter 8’s on least-squares fitting fits curves to data with errors.

Widths add in quadrature too. A screen with a resolution of \(\sigma_{res} = 0.5\) mm shows a beam of true size \(\sigma_b\) as a spot of size \(\sqrt{\sigma_b^2 + \sigma_{res}^2}\), since the beam’s spread and the screen’s blur are independent. A measured 1.2 mm means a true \(\sqrt{1.2^2 - 0.5^2} = 1.09\) mm. When the resolution approaches the measured size, the subtraction becomes very sensitive to it, and the measurement says little.

9. Temperature, heat and internal energy

Temperature and the zeroth law. Put a hot body and a cold one in contact, and the hot one cools and the cold one warms until nothing more changes: they are in thermal equilibrium. Experience says that if A is in equilibrium with B, and B with C, then A is in equilibrium with C. This is the zeroth law (named after the first and second, because it was seen later to come before them). It makes a thermometer possible: B is the thermometer, and two bodies that give it the same reading will not exchange heat. Temperature is the quantity that is equal in bodies in equilibrium.

The kelvin scale. Gases at low pressure all behave alike (section 10): at fixed volume, their pressure falls in a straight line as they are cooled, and the lines all head to zero pressure at the same temperature, \(-273.15\) °C. That is absolute zero, and the kelvin scale counts from it, in steps the size of a degree Celsius: \(T\,[\text{K}] = t\,[°\text{C}] + 273.15\). Room temperature, 20 °C, is 293.15 K; liquid nitrogen boils at 77 K, liquid helium at 4.2 K (at atmospheric pressure); a tungsten cathode runs near 2500 K. In physics formulas \(T\) is always in kelvin.

Heat is energy in transit. Heat, \(Q\), is energy passed from one body to another because of a difference of temperature, measured in joules. A body does not “contain heat”; it contains internal energy \(U\): the kinetic energy of its molecules’ random motion plus the energy stored in the forces between them. Heat and work are two ways of changing \(U\).

The first law. Energy is conserved:

\[ \Delta U = Q - W_{by}, \]

where \(Q\) is the heat put into a body and \(W_{by}\) is the work it does on its surroundings. A gas at pressure \(p\) that expands by a small volume \(dV\) pushes the walls out against a force \(pA\) through a distance \(dV/A\), doing work \(W_{by} = p\,dV\). Joule’s experiments were the first law measured: work done by falling weights on water raised its temperature exactly as much as an equal energy of heat would.

Example: an anode with no cooling. A beam of 10 mA at 2 kV ends on a copper anode of 100 g. The beam brings energy at \(10^{-2} \times 2000 = 20\) W, all of which becomes internal energy of the copper. It takes 385 J to warm a kilogram of copper by one kelvin (its specific heat, section 15), so the anode warms at \(20/(0.1 \times 385) = 0.52\) K per second, 31 K a minute. Every beam dump, collimator and cavity wall in the course has this problem, and chapter 13 counts it for the walls of an RF cavity.

10. The ideal gas

Three laws in one. Experiment on any gas at low enough pressure gives three facts. At fixed temperature, \(pV\) is constant (Boyle, 1662). At fixed pressure, \(V\) grows in proportion to the absolute temperature (Charles and Gay-Lussac). Equal volumes at the same pressure and temperature hold equal numbers of molecules (Avogadro, 1811). Together:

\[ pV = Nk_BT, \]

with \(N\) the number of molecules and \(k_B = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}\) J/K, Boltzmann’s constant. Dividing by \(V\), with \(n = N/V\) the number density,

\[ p = nk_BT, \qquad n = \frac{p}{k_BT} . \]

The pressure depends on how many molecules there are per cubic metre and on the temperature, not on what they are: hydrogen and argon at the same \(n\) and \(T\) press equally. Section 11 shows why.

Moles. Chemists count in moles: one mole is \(N_A = 6.02214076 \times 10^{23}\) particles (Avogadro’s number, exact since 2019). A mole of molecules of molecular mass \(M\) (in atomic mass units) weighs \(M\) grams: nitrogen, N\(_2\), has \(M = 28.01\), so 28.01 g. With \(\nu\) moles, \(N = \nu N_A\) and

\[ pV = \nu RT, \qquad R = N_Ak_B = 8.314\ \text{J/(mol K)} . \]

A mole of any gas at 20 °C and atmospheric pressure (101 325 Pa) fills \(RT/p = 24.06\) litres.

Numbers for a vacuum chamber. At 20 °C:

Pressure \(n\) (m\(^{-3}\)) Molecules in a 100-litre chamber
\(10^5\) Pa (about 1 atmosphere) \(2.47 \times 10^{25}\) \(2.47 \times 10^{24}\) (4.1 mol)
1 Pa \(2.47 \times 10^{20}\) \(2.47 \times 10^{19}\)
\(10^{-6}\) Pa \(2.47 \times 10^{14}\) \(2.47 \times 10^{13}\)

Pumping a chamber down to \(10^{-6}\) Pa removes all but one molecule in \(10^{11}\), and still leaves 25 million million behind, about 16 μm apart. The beam meets molecules, not pressure, so the LHC states its vacuum as a density, at most \(10^{15}\) molecules per m\(^3\); in its beam pipe at 1.9 K that is a pressure of only \(nk_BT = 2.6 \times 10^{-8}\) Pa. The same density at room temperature would read \(4.0 \times 10^{-6}\) Pa. Chapter 3 works in these units throughout.

Where it fails. The ideal gas law assumes molecules that take up no room and do not attract one another. At high density they do both, and the law fails; near the boiling point it fails badly (helium at 4.5 K and atmospheric pressure is about to condense). In a vacuum chamber, where molecules are far apart, it is exact for practical purposes.

11. Kinetic theory: pressure from molecular impacts

The picture. A gas is a crowd of molecules flying in straight lines between collisions. When one bounces off a wall, its momentum perpendicular to the wall reverses, and the wall must supply the force that reverses it. Billions of billions of such impacts each second add up to a steady push: pressure. Bernoulli had this idea in 1738; here is the calculation.

One molecule in a box. Take a cubical box of side \(L\) and one molecule of mass \(m\) with velocity components \(v_x\), \(v_y\), \(v_z\). It bounces elastically off the wall at \(x = L\): \(v_x\) becomes \(-v_x\), a change of momentum \(2mv_x\). It returns to that wall after crossing the box and back, a distance \(2L\), which takes \(2L/v_x\). (Collisions with the other walls change \(v_y\) and \(v_z\) but not \(v_x\); collisions with other molecules only swap momentum around and do not change the average.) The average force on the wall is the momentum delivered per unit time:

\[ F = \frac{2mv_x}{2L/v_x} = \frac{mv_x^2}{L} . \]

Many molecules. With \(N\) molecules, add their forces and divide by the wall’s area \(L^2\):

\[ p = \frac{1}{L^2}\sum_i\frac{mv_{x,i}^2}{L} = \frac{Nm\langle v_x^2\rangle}{V} = nm\langle v_x^2\rangle, \]

with \(V = L^3\). No direction is special in a gas at rest, so \(\langle v_x^2\rangle = \langle v_y^2\rangle = \langle v_z^2\rangle\), and since \(v^2 = v_x^2 + v_y^2 + v_z^2\), each is \(\tfrac13\langle v^2\rangle\):

\[ p = \tfrac13\,nm\langle v^2\rangle = \tfrac23\,n\,\big\langle\tfrac12 mv^2\big\rangle . \]

The pressure is two-thirds of the kinetic energy per unit volume. The shape of the box does not matter (chapter 3’s Maxwell-Boltzmann pre-requisite does it for a piece of wall of any shape, and for walls that hold molecules briefly and let them go in random directions).

What temperature is. Compare with the ideal gas law, \(p = nk_BT\):

\[ \big\langle\tfrac12 mv^2\big\rangle = \tfrac32\,k_BT . \]

This is the meaning of temperature in a gas: it measures the average kinetic energy of the molecules’ random motion, \(\tfrac32 k_B\) joules per kelvin per molecule. At absolute zero this motion would stop (in classical physics; F7 adds a quantum correction). At 20 °C each molecule has on average \(\tfrac32 k_BT = 6.07 \times 10^{-21}\) J \(= 0.0379\) eV, whatever its mass. That is why the pressure does not depend on the kind of gas.

The rms speed. Solving for the speed,

\[ v_{rms} = \sqrt{\langle v^2\rangle} = \sqrt{\frac{3k_BT}{m}} . \]

Light molecules move fast. At 20 °C:

Gas Mass (u) \(v_{rms}\) (m/s) Mean speed \(\bar v\) (m/s)
H\(_2\) 2.016 1904 1755
He 4.003 1352 1245
H\(_2\)O 18.02 637 587
N\(_2\) 28.01 511 471
Ar 39.95 428 394

(The mass in kg is the mass in atomic mass units times \(1.66054 \times 10^{-27}\) kg. The mean speed is introduced in section 13.) Helium at 4.5 K, the temperature of a superconducting magnet’s coolant, has \(v_{rms} = 167\) m/s. The electrons that boil off a tungsten cathode at 2500 K, nearly two thousand times lighter than a hydrogen atom, leave with \(v_{rms} = 3.4 \times 10^5\) m/s, which a 2 kV gun then multiplies by about 80.

Equipartition. The energy \(\tfrac32 k_BT\) is \(\tfrac12 k_BT\) for each of the three directions of motion, since \(\langle\tfrac12 mv_x^2\rangle = \tfrac12 k_BT\) and the same for \(y\) and \(z\). This is a case of a general rule of classical physics, the equipartition theorem: in equilibrium at temperature \(T\), each term in a molecule’s energy that is a square of a position or a velocity holds, on average, \(\tfrac12 k_BT\). A diatomic molecule like N\(_2\) can also rotate about two axes, each a squared angular velocity, so at room temperature it holds \(\tfrac52 k_BT\). An atom in a solid vibrates in three directions, with both kinetic and spring energy in each, six squares, so it holds \(3k_BT\). Section 15 checks these against measured heat capacities. A beam has a temperature in the same sense: chapter 7 defines the transverse temperature of a beam by \(\langle p_x^2\rangle/2m = \tfrac12 k_BT\).

12. The energy scale: \(k_BT\) in electronvolts

Atomic and accelerator physics measure energy in electronvolts (1 eV \(= 1.602177 \times 10^{-19}\) J, the energy one electron gains crossing one volt). Then \(k_B = 8.617 \times 10^{-5}\) eV/K, and the thermal energy \(k_BT\) at a few temperatures of the course is:

Where \(T\) (K) \(k_BT\) (eV)
Liquid helium, superconducting magnets 4.5 \(3.88 \times 10^{-4}\)
Liquid nitrogen 77 0.00664
Room, 20 °C 293.15 0.02526
Tungsten cathode 2500 0.2154

and 1 eV of \(k_BT\) is 11 604.5 K. The rule of thumb is \(k_BT \approx 1/40\) eV at room temperature. Comparing an energy with \(k_BT\) is the first thing to do with it. An energy far below \(k_BT\) is shaken about freely by heat; an energy far above it is reached only rarely, how rarely being the subject of the next section. The work function of tungsten, the energy an electron needs to leave the metal, is 4.53 eV: 179 times \(k_BT\) at room temperature, 21 times at 2500 K. That ratio decides whether a cathode emits.

13. The Boltzmann factor and the speeds of molecules

The atmosphere. Air is denser at sea level than on a mountain. Why exactly? Take a column of gas at one temperature \(T\), of molecules of mass \(m\), in gravity \(g\). A thin slab of thickness \(dh\) and area \(A\) holds \(nA\,dh\) molecules; the pressure below it must exceed the pressure above by their weight per unit area, so \(dp = -nmg\,dh\). With \(p = nk_BT\) at fixed \(T\), \(dp = k_BT\,dn\), and

\[ \frac{dn}{n} = -\frac{mg}{k_BT}\,dh \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n(h) = n(0)\,e^{-mgh/k_BT} . \]

The density falls by a factor \(e\) for every scale height \(H = k_BT/mg\). For air (average mass 28.97 u) at 20 °C, \(H = 8.58\) km; at the top of Everest, 8848 m, an isothermal atmosphere would have 36 per cent of sea-level density (the real air there is colder, so the figure is approximate). Helium, seven times lighter, would have a scale height of 62 km.

The general rule. Look at what appeared in the exponent: \(mgh\), the potential energy of one molecule at height \(h\), divided by \(k_BT\). The chance of finding a molecule at height \(h\) goes as \(e^{-E/k_BT}\) with \(E\) its energy there. Boltzmann showed this holds for any kind of energy and any system in equilibrium at temperature \(T\): the probability of a small part of the system being in a particular state of energy \(E\) is proportional to

\[ e^{-E/k_BT}, \]

the Boltzmann factor. It is the most-used result of this chapter. A derivation from counting states is given in chapter 2’s pre-requisite on the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution and in the textbooks of the sources; here the atmosphere is the evidence. The factor says: energies up to a few \(k_BT\) are common; each further \(k_BT\) costs a factor \(e\); and energies of 20 or more \(k_BT\) are extremely rare, but because the factor changes so steeply with \(T\), a small rise in temperature makes them much less rare.

Example: a hot cathode. For an electron to leave tungsten it needs at least the work function, 4.53 eV, of energy in the right direction. The Boltzmann factor for that energy is \(e^{-4.53/0.2154} = e^{-21.03} = 7.4 \times 10^{-10}\) at 2500 K and \(e^{-179} \approx 10^{-78}\) at room temperature. The metal holds so many electrons, hitting the surface so often, that one in \(10^9\) is a large current; one in \(10^{78}\) is none. Between 2000 K and 2500 K the factor grows 190 times. This factor is the heart of Richardson’s law in chapter 2, and F1 (section 5) found the steep sensitivity that follows from it.

The Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution. Apply the factor to the kinetic energy of a molecule. For the velocity component \(v_x\), the energy is \(\tfrac12 mv_x^2\), so the density of \(v_x\) is proportional to \(e^{-mv_x^2/2k_BT}\): a Gaussian (section 5) with mean zero and \(\sigma^2 = k_BT/m\), which agrees with \(\langle\tfrac12 mv_x^2\rangle = \tfrac12 k_BT\) from section 11. The three components are independent. The density of speeds \(v\), found by collecting all the velocities with the same speed (they fill a thin spherical shell of area \(4\pi v^2\) in a space whose axes are \(v_x\), \(v_y\), \(v_z\)), is

\[ F(v) = 4\pi v^2\left(\frac{m}{2\pi k_BT}\right)^{3/2}e^{-mv^2/2k_BT} . \]

It rises from zero (few velocities are that small), peaks, and falls off in a long tail. Three speeds describe it: the most probable \(\sqrt{2k_BT/m}\), the mean \(\bar v = \sqrt{8k_BT/\pi m}\), and the rms \(\sqrt{3k_BT/m}\), in the ratio \(1 : 1.128 : 1.225\). For nitrogen at 20 °C they are 417, 471 and 511 m/s. Chapter 3’s Maxwell-Boltzmann pre-requisite derives \(\bar v\) by integrating \(vF(v)\), and the speeds of molecules lists them for the gases of a vacuum system.

Escape over a barrier. A molecule stuck on a surface with binding energy \(E_d\) vibrates in its place about \(10^{13}\) times a second. Each vibration has a chance of about \(e^{-E_d/k_BT}\) of carrying enough energy to leave. So the mean time it stays, the sojourn time, is about

\[ \tau = \tau_0\,e^{E_d/k_BT}, \qquad \tau_0 \approx 10^{-13}\ \text{s} . \]

For a molecule weakly held with \(E_d = 0.1\) eV, \(\tau = 5 \times 10^{-12}\) s at 20 °C: it bounces off almost at once. At 20 K, \(E_d/k_BT = 58\) and \(\tau = 1.6 \times 10^{12}\) s, about 50 000 years: it stays. A cold enough surface is a pump (section 16). The same law sets how fast water leaves the walls of a chamber, and why baking a chamber at a few hundred degrees Celsius for a day or so drives off water that would otherwise leave over weeks (chapter 3, outgassing).

14. Molecules on the move: wall collisions and the mean free path

How often molecules hit a wall. The rate at which molecules strike each square metre of wall sets how fast a surface is covered with gas, how much gas a hole lets through, and how fast a pump can remove gas. Consider first only molecules moving with speed \(v\) at angle \(\theta\) to the wall’s normal. Those that reach an area \(A\) in time \(dt\) are inside a slanted cylinder of base \(A\) and height \(v\cos\theta\,dt\). So the rate per unit area is the number density times the average of \(v\cos\theta\) over all molecules, counting only those heading towards the wall (\(\cos\theta > 0\)) and counting the others as zero.

The speeds and directions are independent, so the average is \(\bar v\) times the average of “\(\cos\theta\) if positive, else 0” over random directions. A random direction is a random point on a sphere of radius 1. Archimedes showed that a slice of a sphere between two parallel planes has an area proportional to the distance between the planes (the slice between heights \(c\) and \(c + dc\) has area \(2\pi\,dc\), whatever \(c\)). So \(\cos\theta\), the height of the point, is uniformly distributed between \(-1\) and 1, with density \(\tfrac12\). The average is

\[ \int_0^1 c\cdot\tfrac12\,dc = \tfrac14, \]

and the wall collision rate is

\[ \Phi = \tfrac14\,n\bar v \qquad \text{(per m}^2\text{ per second)} . \]

The \(\tfrac14\) is half (half the molecules are heading away) times a half (those heading in have on average half their speed along the normal). Chapter 3 derives the same result as an integral in spherical coordinates (how often molecules hit a wall).

Example: the monolayer time. Nitrogen at \(10^{-6}\) Pa and 20 °C has \(n = 2.47 \times 10^{14}\) m\(^{-3}\) and \(\bar v = 471\) m/s, so \(\Phi = 2.9 \times 10^{16}\) m\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\). A clean surface holds about \(10^{19}\) molecules per m\(^2\) in one layer. If every molecule that arrives sticks, a fresh surface is covered in \(10^{19}/2.9 \times 10^{16} = 344\) s, about 6 minutes. At atmospheric pressure it takes a few nanoseconds. This is why surface science and accelerator vacuum both need ultra-high vacuum, and the monolayer time of chapter 3 builds on it.

Example: a hole. Every molecule that reaches a hole of area \(A\) in a wall goes through it, so the hole passes the gas in a volume \(\tfrac14\bar vA\) every second, whatever the pressure (when the mean free path is long). For nitrogen at 20 °C that is 11.8 litres per second for each cm\(^2\) of hole. This is the conductance of an orifice in chapter 3, and an upper limit to the speed of any pump with an inlet of that area.

The mean free path. How far does a molecule go between collisions? Model molecules as hard spheres of diameter \(d\). Two collide when their centres pass within \(d\), so a moving molecule sweeps out a cylinder of cross-section \(\sigma = \pi d^2\) (here \(\sigma\) is an area, the collision cross-section). Flying a distance \(x\), it sweeps a volume \(\sigma x\), which holds on average \(n\sigma x\) other molecules. If the others stood still, it would hit one on average every \(1/(n\sigma)\) of distance. They do not stand still, and moving targets are met more often: the average relative speed of two molecules is \(\sqrt2\,\bar v\), not \(\bar v\) (for two independent Gaussian velocities the variances add, section 3), so

\[ \lambda = \frac{1}{\sqrt2\,n\sigma} = \frac{k_BT}{\sqrt2\,\pi d^2\,p} . \]

For air, \(d \approx 3.7 \times 10^{-10}\) m and \(\sigma = 4.3 \times 10^{-19}\) m\(^2\). At 20 °C:

Pressure \(\lambda\) for air
101 325 Pa 66 nm
1 Pa 6.7 mm
\(10^{-3}\) Pa 6.7 m
\(10^{-6}\) Pa 6.7 km

At atmospheric pressure a molecule collides \(\bar v/\lambda = 7 \times 10^9\) times a second. At \(10^{-6}\) Pa it crosses a chamber wall to wall thousands of times between collisions with other molecules: the gas no longer behaves as a fluid, and pipes and pumps work differently (chapter 3’s gas flow regimes). The free paths are distributed exponentially about \(\lambda\) (section 4). A fast electron or a beam particle is far smaller than a molecule and much faster than it, so its mean free path is longer, by a factor of at least \(4\sqrt2\): chapter 2’s mean free path and collision rates works it out, and chapter 3 gives the details for molecules.

Random walks. A molecule that changes direction at random every \(\lambda\) does not get far: after \(N\) steps its distance from the start is the sum of \(N\) independent random steps, whose variance is \(N\) times that of one step (section 3). So its distance grows as \(\sqrt N\), not \(N\): this is diffusion, and it is why a smell takes minutes to cross a room though molecules fly at 500 m/s. Chapter 3’s diffusion and Fokker-Planck pre-requisite takes it further, and chapter 20 meets the same \(\sqrt N\) growth in the random photon kicks that set an electron beam’s size.

15. Heat capacity

Definition. Put heat \(Q\) into a body and its temperature rises by \(\Delta T\). The heat capacity is \(C = Q/\Delta T\), in J/K. Per kilogram it is the specific heat \(c\), in J/(kg K); per mole the molar heat capacity, in J/(mol K). Water’s specific heat is 4186 J/(kg K), copper’s 385 J/(kg K).

A gas at constant volume. The internal energy of a monatomic ideal gas (helium, argon) is the kinetic energy of its atoms, \(U = \tfrac32 Nk_BT\) (section 11). Heated in a closed box, it does no work, so all the heat goes into \(U\) and

\[ C_V = \frac{dU}{dT} = \tfrac32 Nk_B, \qquad \text{per mole } \tfrac32 R = 12.47\ \text{J/(mol K)} . \]

For a diatomic gas, with \(\tfrac52 k_BT\) per molecule, it is \(\tfrac52 R = 20.79\) J/(mol K). Measurements on helium and argon, and on nitrogen and oxygen at room temperature, agree: a triumph of the equipartition rule.

At constant pressure. Heated in a cylinder with a free piston, a gas expands and pushes the piston, doing work \(p\,\Delta V = Nk_B\,\Delta T\). The heat must supply this as well as \(\Delta U\), so \(C_p = C_V + Nk_B\), or per mole \(C_p = C_V + R\). For a diatomic gas \(C_p = \tfrac72 R = 29.10\) J/(mol K).

Solids. An atom in a solid holds \(3k_BT\) (section 11), so a mole holds \(3RT\) and has heat capacity \(3R = 24.94\) J/(mol K), the rule found by Dulong and Petit in 1819. For copper (63.55 g/mol) this predicts \(24.94/0.06355 = 392\) J/(kg K), against the measured 385.

Where it fails: the cold. At low temperature, the heat capacities of solids fall far below \(3R\), and towards zero near absolute zero; the rotations of diatomic gases also stop taking up energy when cold. Classical equipartition cannot explain this. Energy comes in quanta, and when \(k_BT\) is smaller than the smallest quantum, the motion cannot be excited at all (F7). The consequence for accelerators is practical: at 4.5 K a superconducting magnet’s coil has a tiny heat capacity, so a small deposit of energy (a millijoule from a slipping wire or a few lost beam particles) warms it a lot, and can tip it out of the superconducting state (chapter 17).

Latent heat. Boiling a liquid takes heat without raising its temperature: the energy goes into pulling the molecules apart. This latent heat per kilogram is about 20.7 kJ/kg for liquid helium at 4.2 K, and the liquid’s density is about 125 kg/m\(^3\). One watt of heat leaking into a bath of liquid helium therefore boils off \(3600/20\,700 = 0.174\) kg an hour, about 1.4 litres. The same watt boils only 22 ml of liquid nitrogen (199 kJ/kg, 807 kg/m\(^3\)). Every watt that reaches the cold part of a superconducting accelerator is paid for, and the next section says how much.

16. Entropy and the second law

In words. Heat flows by itself from hot to cold, never from cold to hot. A dropped ball warms the floor, but a warm floor never throws a ball up. Gas let into an empty chamber fills it, and never gathers itself back into one corner. Each of these processes runs one way only, though Newton’s laws run equally well backwards. The second law of thermodynamics names what decides the direction. Clausius’s form: heat cannot pass from a colder body to a hotter one without some other change, such as work being spent. Kelvin’s form: no engine working in a cycle can turn heat from a single body entirely into work.

Why: counting ways. Think of the gas filling the chamber. Each molecule is equally likely to be in either half, independently, so the chance that all \(N\) are in the left half at once is \((\tfrac12)^N\) (section 1). For 10 molecules that is 1 in 1024, and it happens. For 100 molecules it is \(7.9 \times 10^{-31}\). For the \(2.5 \times 10^{22}\) molecules in a litre of air, it is \(10^{-7.5 \times 10^{21}}\). The spread-out arrangements are not favoured by any force; there are overwhelmingly more of them. Boltzmann defined the entropy of a state as \(S = k_B\ln\Omega\), with \(\Omega\) the number of microscopic arrangements that look the same from outside. The second law is then a law of large numbers: an isolated system moves towards the states with most arrangements, and its entropy does not fall.

Entropy and heat. Clausius found the measurable form first. When a small amount of heat \(Q\) enters a body at temperature \(T\) (slowly, so that \(T\) is well defined), the body’s entropy rises by

\[ \Delta S = \frac{Q}{T}, \]

in J/K. The \(1/T\) makes sense from counting: adding energy to a cold body, whose molecules have little, opens up many more new arrangements than adding the same energy to a hot one. Now let heat \(Q\) flow from a hot body at \(T_h\) to a cold one at \(T_c\). The hot one loses entropy \(Q/T_h\), the cold one gains \(Q/T_c\), and since \(T_c < T_h\) the total rises:

\[ \Delta S = \frac{Q}{T_c} - \frac{Q}{T_h} > 0 . \]

Heat flowing from cold to hot would make the total fall, so it does not happen by itself. Example. One watt leaking from the 300 K outside of a cryostat into its 4.5 K interior makes \(1/4.5 - 1/300 = 0.219\) J/K of entropy every second.

The price of cold. A refrigerator does what heat will not do by itself: it takes heat \(Q\) out of a cold space at \(T\) and dumps it into the room at \(T_a\). Taking \(Q\) out at \(T\) removes entropy \(Q/T\) from the cold space. The second law says this entropy cannot vanish, so at least as much must be delivered to the room. The refrigerator delivers the heat \(Q\) plus the electrical work \(W_{el}\) it uses (by the first law, that energy too ends as heat), bringing entropy \((Q + W_{el})/T_a\) to the room. So

\[ \frac{Q + W_{el}}{T_a} \ge \frac{Q}{T} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad W_{el} \ge Q\,\frac{T_a - T}{T} . \]

The equality holds only for a perfect, reversible machine, Carnot’s ideal. With \(T_a = 300\) K:

Cold end \(T\) (K) Least work per watt removed
Liquid nitrogen shield 80 2.75 W
Helium, Tevatron, HERA 4.5 65.7 W
Helium, LHC magnets 1.9 157 W

The colder the cold end, the steeper the price, growing as \(1/T\). Real plants reach between a quarter and under a third of this ideal: about 235 to 280 W of electricity per watt at 4.5 K (chapter 17, cryogenics). A string of superconducting magnets with a heat leak of 50 W at 4.5 K needs at least 3.3 kW, and in practice 12 to 14 kW. This is why every cryostat in the course is wrapped in vacuum, polished shields and a nitrogen-cooled jacket, and why chapter 13 finds superconducting cavities worthwhile only when the RF losses they save outweigh the refrigeration.

Why a cold surface pumps gas. A surface at 20 K in a vacuum chamber catches nitrogen, oxygen and water: each molecule that lands sticks for thousands of years (section 13), and the chamber’s pressure falls. Gas gathered from a whole chamber onto a small panel looks like a fall in entropy. It is, for the gas; but the panel is kept cold by a refrigerator that dumps heat, and with it more entropy, into the room. The total rises, and the second law is safe. Seen through the Boltzmann factor: the vapour pressure of a frozen gas, the pressure at which as many molecules leave the solid as land on it, goes as \(e^{-E/k_BT}\) with \(E\) the binding energy, so cooling the panel by a factor of a few lowers it by many powers of ten. That is a cryopump (chapter 3, how pumps work). In the LHC’s arcs the beam pipe, at 1.9 K, is a cryopump kilometres long.

Exercises

  1. A tracking detector has four layers, each firing for a passing particle with probability 0.90, independently. A track is recognised if at least three layers fire. What fraction of particles is recognised? Compare with a detector that needs all three of three layers.
Answer

All four fire with probability \(0.9^4 = 0.6561\). Exactly three fire with probability \(\binom43\,0.9^3 \times 0.1 = 4 \times 0.729 \times 0.1 = 0.2916\). These are exclusive, so the total is 0.948. A three-of-three detector recognises \(0.9^3 = 0.729\). Allowing one layer to miss raises the efficiency from 73 to 95 per cent, which is why real trackers have spare layers.

  1. A beam profile on a screen has a full width at half maximum of 4.7 mm. Assuming it is Gaussian, what is \(\sigma\)? What fraction of the particles lies more than 5 mm from the centre in the horizontal plane? If the beam is round, with the same \(\sigma\) in both planes, what fraction lies outside a circle of radius 5 mm?
Answer

\(\sigma = 4.7/2.355 = 2.0\) mm. 5 mm is \(2.5\sigma\); the fraction beyond \(\pm 2.5\sigma\) in one plane is 1.24 per cent (from the error function, or a table). For the round beam, the fraction outside radius \(r\) is \(e^{-r^2/2\sigma^2} = e^{-25/8} = e^{-3.125} = 0.044\), 4.4 per cent: more than three times the one-plane figure.

  1. A counter with no source present records on average 0.5 counts per second of background. (a) What is the chance of no counts in a 10 s window? (b) With a source present the counter records about 50 counts per second. How long must it count to know the rate to 1 per cent (ignoring the background)?
Answer
  1. The mean in 10 s is \(\mu = 5\), and \(P(0) = e^{-5} = 0.0067\): less than 1 per cent. (b) A relative error \(1/\sqrt N = 0.01\) needs \(N = 10^4\) counts, which at 50 per second takes 200 s.
  1. Ten readings of a beam current, in mA, are 10.3, 9.8, 10.1, 10.4, 9.7, 10.0, 10.2, 9.9, 10.5, 10.1. Find the mean, the sample standard deviation and the error of the mean. How many readings would bring the error of the mean down to 0.02 mA? Would that make the result accurate to 0.02 mA?
Answer

Mean 10.10 mA. The deviations are 0.2, \(-0.3\), 0, 0.3, \(-0.4\), \(-0.1\), 0.1, \(-0.2\), 0.4, 0; their squares add to 0.60, so \(s = \sqrt{0.60/9} = 0.258\) mA, and \(\sigma_{\bar x} = 0.258/\sqrt{10} = 0.082\) mA. For 0.02 mA, \(N = (0.258/0.02)^2 = 167\) readings. Not necessarily: averaging reduces only the random error. A calibration error of the meter is untouched, and must be checked separately.

  1. A screen with a resolution of \(\sigma_{res} = 0.5\) mm shows a beam spot of \(\sigma = 1.2\) mm. What is the true beam size? What would it be if the screen’s resolution were 1.0 mm, and why is that second result less trustworthy?
Answer

Widths add in quadrature, so \(\sigma_b = \sqrt{1.2^2 - 0.5^2} = 1.09\) mm. With \(\sigma_{res} = 1.0\) mm, \(\sigma_b = \sqrt{1.44 - 1.00} = 0.66\) mm. In the second case the answer is a small difference of two nearly equal squares: a 10 per cent error in the resolution (0.1 mm) would change \(\sigma_{res}^2\) by 0.21 mm\(^2\) and the answer from 0.66 mm to about 0.48 or 0.79 mm. A measurement is only good when the instrument’s resolution is well below the size measured.

  1. A 100-litre chamber at 20 °C is pumped to \(10^{-6}\) Pa. How many molecules are left, and how far apart are they on average? The LHC asks for at most \(10^{15}\) molecules per m\(^3\) in a beam pipe at 1.9 K: what pressure is that?
Answer

\(n = p/k_BT = 10^{-6}/(1.380649 \times 10^{-23} \times 293.15) = 2.47 \times 10^{14}\) m\(^{-3}\), so \(0.1\) m\(^3\) holds \(2.47 \times 10^{13}\) molecules. Each has on average a volume \(1/n\), a cube of side \(n^{-1/3} = 1.6 \times 10^{-5}\) m, 16 μm. At 1.9 K, \(p = nk_BT = 10^{15} \times 1.380649 \times 10^{-23} \times 1.9 = 2.6 \times 10^{-8}\) Pa.

  1. Find the rms speed of helium atoms (4.003 u) at 4.5 K and at 300 K, and \(k_BT\) in eV at 4.5 K. By what factor do the speeds differ, and why?
Answer

\(m = 4.003 \times 1.66054 \times 10^{-27} = 6.647 \times 10^{-27}\) kg. \(v_{rms} = \sqrt{3k_BT/m}\) gives 167 m/s at 4.5 K and 1367 m/s at 300 K. The ratio is \(\sqrt{300/4.5} = 8.16\): the mean kinetic energy is proportional to \(T\), so the speed goes as \(\sqrt T\). At 4.5 K, \(k_BT = 8.617 \times 10^{-5} \times 4.5 = 3.88 \times 10^{-4}\) eV.

  1. A tungsten cathode has a work function of 4.53 eV. Find the Boltzmann factor \(e^{-E/k_BT}\) for \(E = 4.53\) eV at 2000 K and at 2500 K, and their ratio. Richardson’s law multiplies the factor by \(T^2\); what is the ratio of the emitted currents?
Answer

At 2000 K, \(k_BT = 0.1723\) eV and \(4.53/0.1723 = 26.28\), so the factor is \(e^{-26.28} = 3.8 \times 10^{-12}\). At 2500 K it is \(e^{-21.03} = 7.4 \times 10^{-10}\). The ratio is 192. With \(T^2\), \((2500/2000)^2 = 1.5625\), and the currents differ by a factor of 300. A 25 per cent rise in temperature gives three hundred times the current: the exponential, not the \(T^2\), does almost all of it.

    1. How often does hydrogen (2.016 u) at \(10^{-7}\) Pa and 20 °C strike each square metre of wall? How long would it take to cover a clean surface holding \(10^{19}\) molecules per m\(^2\), if every molecule stuck? (b) What is the mean free path of air at \(10^{-6}\) Pa?
Answer
  1. \(n = 10^{-7}/(k_B \times 293.15) = 2.47 \times 10^{13}\) m\(^{-3}\) and \(\bar v = \sqrt{8k_BT/\pi m} = 1755\) m/s, so \(\Phi = \tfrac14 n\bar v = 1.08 \times 10^{16}\) m\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\). A monolayer takes \(10^{19}/1.08 \times 10^{16} = 920\) s, about 15 minutes. (Hydrogen, being light and fast, hits 3.7 times as often as nitrogen at the same pressure.) (b) \(\lambda = k_BT/(\sqrt2\,\pi d^2p)\) with \(d = 3.7 \times 10^{-10}\) m: \(4.047 \times 10^{-21}/(1.414 \times 4.30 \times 10^{-19} \times 10^{-6}) = 6.65 \times 10^3\) m, 6.7 km.
  1. A string of superconducting magnets has a heat leak of 50 W at 4.5 K. (a) What is the least electrical power, with the room at 300 K, to remove it? (b) What does a real plant using 250 W per W need? (c) If instead the heat simply boiled away liquid helium, how many litres an hour would be lost?
Answer
  1. \(W_{el} = Q(T_a - T)/T = 50 \times 295.5/4.5 = 3.28\) kW. (b) \(50 \times 250 = 12.5\) kW. (c) Each watt boils about 1.39 litres an hour, so 50 W boils about 70 litres an hour, which is why large machines recondense their helium instead of buying it.
