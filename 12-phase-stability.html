12 Phase stability, the synchrocyclotron and the isochronous cyclotron

1938 to the 1980s: Thomas’s sectors, Veksler and McMillan’s phase stability, the Berkeley 184-inch, the synchrocyclotrons of CERN, Dubna and Gatchina, the sector-focused cyclotrons of Delft, PSI, TRIUMF and Michigan State; the wall is the mass increase that pulls a particle out of step with a fixed frequency in a field that must also focus.

In one page

The wall. A cyclotron works because the revolution frequency \(\omega_0 = qB/(\gamma m)\) does not depend on the energy while \(\gamma \approx 1\). As \(\gamma\) grows, the particle comes round later each turn and drifts out of step with the fixed radio frequency. A field rising with radius would restore the timing, but such a field defocuses vertically. In 1937 Bethe and Rose concluded that this caps the cyclotron. For protons with 200 kV per turn the cap is about 11 to 19 MeV (chapter 10).

The ideas. Phase stability (Veksler 1944, McMillan 1945): a particle arriving early or late at the gap gains a different energy, and that change corrects its timing. So it oscillates about a synchronous phase, and the frequency can be swept slowly while the particles follow. The synchrocyclotron sweeps the frequency down to follow \(qB/(\gamma m)\); its beam comes in pulses. Azimuthally varying fields (Thomas 1938): hills and valleys make the orbit scalloped, and the scallop crossing the hill edges focuses vertically. The mean field can then rise as \(\gamma(r)\) with a fixed frequency and a continuous beam: the isochronous cyclotron.

The equations to remember.

\[ \begin{aligned} \eta &= \alpha_c - \frac{1}{\gamma^2}, \qquad \alpha_c = \frac{1}{1-n} \ \text{(cyclotron field of index } n\text{)} \\ Q_s^2 &= -\frac{h\,\eta\, q\hat{V}_t \cos\phi_s}{2\pi \beta^2 E}, \qquad \text{stable when } \eta\cos\phi_s < 0 \\ \bar{B}(R) &= \gamma(R)\,B_0, \qquad n = -(\gamma^2 - 1), \qquad Q_r \approx \gamma \\ Q_z^2 &\approx n + \frac{N_s^2}{N_s^2-1}\,F\,(1 + 2\tan^2\xi) \\ \frac{dR}{dn_t} &= \frac{R\,\Delta W_t}{mc^2\,\gamma(\gamma^2-1)} \quad \text{(turn separation, isochronous)} \end{aligned} \]

Numbers worth knowing. The Berkeley 184-inch ran as a synchrocyclotron in November 1946, with deuterons of about 190 MeV and alphas of about 380 MeV. CERN’s synchrocyclotron reached 600 MeV in August 1957. Rebuilt, it swept from 30 MHz to about 17 MHz 466 times a second: about 30,000 turns at about 20 keV each. Synchrocyclotrons captured ions for only about 1 per cent of the time and gave microamperes; Gatchina’s 1 GeV machine has a 7800 t magnet and gives 1 µA. The PSI ring cyclotron (1974) gives 590 MeV protons, 2.2 mA routinely, 1.3 MW, in about 186 turns, losing about \(10^{-4}\) at extraction. TRIUMF (first beam 15 December 1974) accelerates H\(^-\) to 520 MeV in a field kept low enough that the ions survive, and strips them on a foil. Isochronous cyclotrons stop at about 1 GeV.

The wall

Bethe and Rose, 1937

The betatron of chapter 11 gave electrons their energy without any radio frequency, and so had no timing to lose. Ions still needed the cyclotron, and by the late 1930s it was the most powerful accelerator there was. The question was how far it could go. In 1937 Hans Bethe and Morris Rose argued that it could not go far, and the argument has two halves.

Timing. In a uniform field the revolution period \(2\pi\gamma m/(qB)\) grows with \(\gamma\). With a fixed frequency the particle arrives a little later at the gap on each turn, and the lateness accumulates as the square of the number of turns: after \(n_t\) turns the energy, and so the excess of the period over the first one, has grown in proportion to \(n_t\), and adding up excesses that grow as \(1, 2, 3, \dots, n_t\) gives about \(n_t^2/2\) of them. The largest kinetic energy before the particle reaches the decelerating half of the cycle is of order \(\sqrt{q\hat{V}_t\,mc^2}\), with \(\hat{V}_t\) the voltage gained per turn (chapter 10 derives it). For protons and 200 kV per turn (100 kV at each of two gaps), chapter 10’s formulas give 10.9 MeV with the field tuned to resonance at the centre and 18.7 MeV with the central field set a little high: about 11 to 19 MeV, depending on how the field is set. The estimates of the time were of the same size: Rose’s fuller paper of 1938, following Bethe and Rose, gave 15 MeV for protons with 50 kV between the dees, and re-evaluated for more than 200 kV between the dees it gives 30 MeV (Livingston and Blewett, section 6-12); S. Y. Lee quotes about 12 MeV.

Focusing. A field rising with radius would keep the timing. But then the field lines bow inward, and off the median plane the radial field component pushes particles away from the plane (chapter 11). Every cyclotron had a field falling slightly outward for vertical focusing, which made the timing worse.

The remedies in sight were to raise the dee voltage, so that fewer turns are needed, or to accept the limit. Raising the voltage buys energy only as its square root, and hundreds of kilovolts at tens of megahertz meant large radio-frequency power and sparking.

Thomas’s answer, unused

In July 1938 Llewellyn H. Thomas, at Ohio State University, sent the Physical Review a paper on the paths of ions in the cyclotron. His abstract states that the Bethe–Rose conclusion holds for a field with rotational symmetry, but not in general. A field varying with azimuth by a relative amount of the order of \(v/c\) adds enough vertical focusing for the mean field to rise with radius. This is the isochronous cyclotron. But the magnetic structure was complicated, and fields of that shape could not then be calculated, measured and trimmed well enough. The war followed, and the idea was not built for more than a decade.

Two inventions of phase stability

In 1944 Vladimir Veksler, at the Lebedev Physical Institute in Moscow, published two short papers in the Doklady of the Soviet Academy of Sciences on a new method of accelerating relativistic particles. In June 1945 Edwin McMillan, a Berkeley physicist then still at Los Alamos, reached the same principle independently. His letter proposing the synchrotron reached the Physical Review on 5 September 1945. He learnt of Veksler’s work in October 1945; the two corresponded, and in 1963 they shared the Atoms for Peace Award.

The principle: particles need not arrive at exactly the right moment, if a late particle gets a different kick from an early one in the right sense. Then the error corrects itself, and the frequency or the field can be changed slowly during acceleration with the particles following. Changing the field gives the synchrotron (chapter 15). Changing the frequency in a fixed cyclotron magnet gives the synchrocyclotron, which the Russian literature calls the phasotron.

The physics

Symbols. The vertical coordinate is \(z\) (the course’s \(y\)), orbit radii are \(r\) or \(R\), and the tunes are \(Q_r\) (radial, the course’s \(Q_x\)) and \(Q_z\); cyclotron papers write \(\nu_r\), \(\nu_z\). The field index is the course’s \(n = -(r/\bar{B})\,d\bar{B}/dr\) applied to the azimuthal mean field \(\bar{B}(r)\); cyclotron papers often use \(k = -n\). \(N_s\) is the number of sectors, \(\xi\) the spiral angle of the sectors (elsewhere in the course \(\xi\) is the beam-beam parameter), \(n_t\) the turn number, \(\Delta W_t\) the energy gain per turn, and \(\hat{V}_t\) the voltage amplitude summed over the gaps crossed in one turn. An electric field is written \(\mathcal{E}\) (V/m), since \(E\) is the particle’s total energy.

1. Who comes round sooner: the phase-slip factor

Compare two particles in the same static field, one with a little more momentum. It is faster, which brings it round sooner. It also sits on a larger orbit, which is longer. Which effect wins decides everything that follows.

In a field with rotational symmetry \(B(r)\), a particle of charge \(q\) on a circle of radius \(r\) has \(p = qB(r)\,r\) (momentum in kg m/s, field in T, radius in m). Taking logarithmic differentials, that is, the small-change rule for a product, in which the relative changes of the factors add (\(\ln p = \ln q + \ln B + \ln r\), and \(d\ln x = dx/x\)), and using the field index \(n = -(r/B)\,dB/dr\), so that \(dB/B = -n\,dr/r\):

\[ \frac{dp}{p} = \frac{dB}{B} + \frac{dr}{r} = (1-n)\,\frac{dr}{r}, \qquad \alpha_c \equiv \frac{dr/r}{dp/p} = \frac{1}{1-n}. \]

\(\alpha_c\), the momentum compaction factor, says how much larger, relatively, the orbit of a particle with a little more momentum is; it has no unit. The revolution angular frequency \(\omega_0 = qB/(\gamma m)\) (rad/s; derived in chapter 10) changes by

\[ \frac{d\omega_0}{\omega_0} = \frac{dB}{B} - \frac{d\gamma}{\gamma} = -n\,\frac{dr}{r} - \beta^2\frac{dp}{p} = -\left(\frac{n}{1-n} + \beta^2\right)\frac{dp}{p}, \]

using \(d\gamma/\gamma = \beta^2\,dp/p\). That comes from \(\gamma^2 = 1 + (p/mc)^2\) (chapter 1): differentiating, \(2\gamma\,d\gamma = 2p\,dp/(mc)^2\), so \(d\gamma/\gamma = [p^2/(\gamma^2m^2c^2)]\,dp/p\), and since \(p = \gamma m\beta c\) the bracket is \(\beta^2\). The middle step also used \(dr/r = \alpha_c\,dp/p = dp/p/(1-n)\). With \(\delta = \Delta p/p\) and the change of the revolution period \(T_0 = 2\pi/\omega_0\) written \(\Delta T_0/T_0 = -\Delta\omega_0/\omega_0 = \eta\,\delta\) (a period is the inverse of a frequency, so a small relative change of one is minus that of the other),

\[ \eta = \frac{n}{1-n} + \beta^2 = \alpha_c - 1 + \beta^2 = \alpha_c - \frac{1}{\gamma^2}. \]

The second form uses \(n/(1-n) = 1/(1-n) - 1 = \alpha_c - 1\), and the third \(1 - \beta^2 = 1/\gamma^2\). \(\eta\) has no unit: a particle with 1 per cent more momentum takes \(\eta\) per cent longer to go round. This is the general phase-slip factor of chapter 19, here derived for a cyclotron. The energy at which \(\eta\) changes sign is called transition: there the two effects of the opening paragraph cancel. Below transition (\(\eta < 0\)) the more energetic particle comes round sooner. Above transition (\(\eta > 0\)) it comes round later. Transition is at \(\gamma_t = 1/\sqrt{\alpha_c} = \sqrt{1-n}\).

  • Synchrocyclotron, \(0 < n < 1\): \(\gamma_t < 1\), so \(\eta > 0\) at every energy. A synchrocyclotron always runs above transition. Near the centre, where \(n \approx 0\), \(\eta \approx \beta^2\), small but positive.
  • Uniform field, \(n = 0\): \(\eta = \beta^2\), the classical cyclotron’s slow loss of step.
  • Isochronous field: \(\eta = 0\) by construction. Section 4 shows this needs \(n = -(\gamma^2-1)\).

Pre-requisite (continued): Ordinary differential equations (linear, constant and varying coefficients)

Pre-requisite. This section gives you two steps for turning a rule applied once per turn into one equation you can solve: replacing the change per turn by a derivative, with a test of when that is allowed, and eliminating one of two coupled first-order equations to leave a single second-order one. Section 2 uses both to turn its two-line map into the pendulum equation and to read off the synchrotron tune. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §21 (small oscillations, linearising the force about an equilibrium), and S. Y. Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 3, sections I.2 and II (the synchrotron mapping equation, and when it may be treated as smooth).

Chapter 2’s section on differential equations taught the linear equation \(x'' + Kx = 0\), and chapter 10’s continuation taught how to linearise about a reference motion, how the sign of \(K\) decides between oscillation and growth, and how to treat a count of turns as a continuous variable. What is new here is a pair of equations, one for each of two variables, and the question of when a map from one turn to the next may be replaced by a differential equation at all.

A map with two variables. Section 2 follows each particle by two numbers taken once per turn: its energy error \(\Delta E\) (MeV), the difference between its total energy and the synchronous particle’s, and its phase \(\phi\) (rad) at the gap. Write the turn number as \(n\) for short. The map is

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta E_{n+1} &= \Delta E_n + q\hat{V}_t\left(\sin\phi_n - \sin\phi_s\right), \\ \phi_{n+1} &= \phi_n + c_\phi\,\Delta E_{n+1}, \qquad c_\phi = \frac{2\pi h\,\eta}{\beta^2E}. \end{aligned} \]

Here \(q\hat{V}_t\) is the peak energy gain per turn (MeV), \(h\) the harmonic number (the whole number of radio-frequency cycles in one revolution, so \(\omega_{RF} = h\omega_0\)), \(\eta\) the phase-slip factor of section 1 and \(E\) the synchronous total energy (MeV), so \(c_\phi\) has the unit rad/MeV. The first line says the energy error changes by the difference between this particle’s gain and the synchronous gain. The second says the phase changes by an amount proportional to the new energy error, because a particle with extra energy has a different revolution period.

From differences to derivatives. For any smooth \(f(n)\), Taylor’s theorem gives \(f(n+1) - f(n) = f'(n) + \tfrac12 f''(n) + \dots\). If \(f\) changes little in one turn compared with how it changes over many, the first term dominates and the difference may be replaced by \(df/dn\). For an oscillation \(f \propto \sin(2\pi Q_s n)\) the ratio of the second term to the first is about \(\pi Q_s\), so the test is \(Q_s \ll 1\): many turns per oscillation.

The test can be made exact for small oscillations. Put \(\phi_n = \phi_s + \Delta\phi_n\), use \(\sin\phi_n - \sin\phi_s \approx \cos\phi_s\,\Delta\phi_n\), and write \(A = q\hat{V}_t\cos\phi_s\) (MeV/rad). The map becomes linear: \(\Delta E_{n+1} = \Delta E_n + A\,\Delta\phi_n\) and \(\Delta\phi_{n+1} = \Delta\phi_n + c_\phi\,\Delta E_{n+1}\). Try a solution that is multiplied by the same number \(\lambda\) each turn, \(\Delta\phi_n = a\lambda^n\) and \(\Delta E_n = b\lambda^n\). Substituting,

\[ (\lambda - 1)\,b = A\,a, \qquad (\lambda - 1)\,a = c_\phi\lambda\,b . \]

Multiply the two and cancel \(ab\): \((\lambda - 1)^2 = Ac_\phi\lambda\), that is

\[ \lambda^2 - (2 + Ac_\phi)\,\lambda + 1 = 0 . \]

The two roots multiply to 1. If \(|2 + Ac_\phi| < 2\) they are complex, \(\lambda = e^{\pm 2\pi i Q}\) with \(\cos 2\pi Q = 1 + Ac_\phi/2\), and the motion is an oscillation of \(Q\) cycles per turn. The differential equation of section 2 gives instead \((2\pi Q_s)^2 = -Ac_\phi\). So the map’s exact tune obeys

\[ \cos 2\pi Q = 1 - \frac{(2\pi Q_s)^2}{2}, \]

which agrees with the smooth \(Q_s\) when \(Q_s\) is small, since \(\cos x \approx 1 - x^2/2\).

At work. In the chapter’s synchrocyclotron model at 100 MeV, \(c_\phi = 6.601\times10^{-3}\) rad/MeV and \(A = 0.040\cos 150^\circ = -0.0346\) MeV/rad. The smooth tune is \(Q_s = 0.002407\) and the map’s tune differs from it by one part in \(10^5\); iterating the map, a small oscillation takes 416 turns, against \(1/Q_s = 415.5\). The smooth equation is excellent here. It is not always: for \(Q_s = 0.1\) the map gives 0.1017, and for \(Q_s > 1/\pi = 0.318\) the right-hand side falls below \(-1\) and the linear map is unstable, although the differential equation still says it oscillates. Beyond that the full nonlinear map breaks up altogether: Lee gives (chapter 3, exercise 3.1, problem 10) a tune of 0.393 at which its whole phase space has become chaotic. Proton machines, with \(Q_s\) of order \(10^{-3}\), are far from this; electron rings, with \(Q_s\) of order 0.01 to 0.1, are closer.

Eliminating one variable. In the smooth form the map is two first-order equations,

\[ \frac{d\,\Delta E}{dn} = q\hat{V}_t\left(\sin\phi - \sin\phi_s\right), \qquad \frac{d\phi}{dn} = c_\phi\,\Delta E . \]

To get one equation for \(\phi\), solve the second for \(\Delta E = (1/c_\phi)\,d\phi/dn\) and put it into the first:

\[ \frac{d}{dn}\left(\frac{1}{c_\phi}\frac{d\phi}{dn}\right) = q\hat{V}_t\left(\sin\phi - \sin\phi_s\right). \]

This is exact. If \(c_\phi\) is constant it comes out of the derivative and gives section 2’s equation, \(d^2\phi/dn^2 = c_\phi q\hat{V}_t(\sin\phi - \sin\phi_s)\). If \(c_\phi\) changes, the product rule leaves an extra term, \(-(1/c_\phi)(dc_\phi/dn)\,d\phi/dn\) on the left after multiplying through by \(c_\phi\). It looks like a friction term, and it is the source of the slow change of amplitude that the adiabatic invariant describes. Dropping it is the approximation “with \(\eta\), \(\beta\) and \(E\) held constant over one oscillation”.

At work. In the model, \(\beta^2E/\eta\), and with it \(1/c_\phi\), rises from 882 MeV at 10 MeV to 1194 MeV at 600 MeV across about 70 oscillations. Near 100 MeV it changes by under 1 per cent in one oscillation of 415 turns, so the dropped term is small, and its slow effect is captured separately by the invariant.

Exercise 3. Eliminate \(\phi\) instead of \(\Delta E\) for the linearised equations with constant coefficients. Is the oscillation frequency the same?

Answer

Differentiate the first: \(d^2\Delta E/dn^2 = q\hat{V}_t\cos\phi_s\,d\Delta\phi/dn = A c_\phi\,\Delta E\). So \(\Delta E'' + (2\pi Q_s)^2\Delta E = 0\) with the same \((2\pi Q_s)^2 = -Ac_\phi\): both variables oscillate at the same frequency, a quarter period apart.

Pre-requisite: The pendulum, nonlinear oscillation and the separatrix

Pre-requisite. This section gives you the full, nonlinear pendulum: its energy integral, its two kinds of fixed point, the curve that divides swinging from going over the top, how the period grows with amplitude, and what a constant torque does to all of these. Section 2 uses it for the phase equation, whose stable region, the bucket, is bounded by such a curve; section 3 uses it for the limit on how fast a synchrocyclotron may sweep. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §11 (motion in one dimension, with the pendulum’s period as an elliptic integral in problem 1); Tong, Classical Mechanics, §10.1 (the pendulum’s phase space and its separatrix); Livingston and Blewett, sections 9-4 and 9-5 (the pendulum with a constant torque as the model of phase motion); and S. Y. Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 3, sections II.1 and II.2 (fixed points, separatrix and bucket).

The idea. A pendulum is a weight on a rigid rod free to turn about a pivot. Hang it straight down and nudge it: it swings, and for small swings it is the simple harmonic oscillator. Push harder and the swings take longer, because near the ends of a large swing the restoring torque grows more slowly than the angle. Push harder still and it goes over the top and keeps turning. Between these two kinds of motion lies one special motion: the pendulum that rises exactly to the upside-down position and stops there, taking forever to arrive. Its path in the phase plane is the separatrix. The phase equation of section 2 is a pendulum with a constant torque added, and its separatrix decides which particles are accelerated.

The equation and its energy integral. Let \(\theta\) (rad) be the angle from the downward vertical. For a rod of length \(l\) in gravity \(g\), with \(t\) the time,

\[ \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = -\omega_0^2\sin\theta, \qquad \omega_0^2 = \frac{g}{l}. \]

For small \(\theta\), \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\) and the motion is \(\theta = \theta_0\cos\omega_0t\). For large \(\theta\) the equation is nonlinear and that solution fails. But one integral can always be found, by the trick chapter 2 used: multiply both sides by \(d\theta/dt\). The left side becomes \(\tfrac12\,d(\dot\theta^2)/dt\) and the right side \(\omega_0^2\,d(\cos\theta)/dt\), so

\[ \tfrac12\dot\theta^2 - \omega_0^2\cos\theta = H, \qquad H \text{ constant}. \]

This is energy conservation divided by \(ml^2\): \(\tfrac12\dot\theta^2\) is the kinetic term and \(U(\theta) = -\omega_0^2\cos\theta\) the potential, lowest at \(\theta = 0\) and highest at \(\theta = \pi\). Each value of \(H\) is one curve in the phase plane \((\theta, \dot\theta)\), the plane of chapter 7’s phase-space section, and the particle moves along its curve.

Fixed points. The pendulum can rest where the torque vanishes, \(\sin\theta = 0\): at \(\theta = 0\) and \(\theta = \pi\). Linearise about each. At \(\theta = \epsilon\): \(\ddot\epsilon = -\omega_0^2\epsilon\), an oscillation; the curves near it are ellipses and the point is stable (an elliptic fixed point). At \(\theta = \pi + \epsilon\): \(\sin(\pi + \epsilon) = -\sin\epsilon \approx -\epsilon\), so \(\ddot\epsilon = +\omega_0^2\epsilon\), with solutions \(e^{\pm\omega_0t}\). A small error grows exponentially; the point is unstable (a hyperbolic fixed point), and the curves near it are hyperbolas.

The separatrix. Three kinds of curve fill the phase plane.

  • \(H < \omega_0^2\): the pendulum cannot reach the top. It swings back and forth on a closed curve around \(\theta = 0\), called libration.
  • \(H > \omega_0^2\): \(\dot\theta\) never vanishes. The pendulum goes over the top and keeps turning one way, called rotation.
  • \(H = \omega_0^2\): the energy of the upside-down pendulum at rest. Using \(1 + \cos\theta = 2\cos^2(\theta/2)\),

\[ \dot\theta = \pm 2\omega_0\cos\frac{\theta}{2}. \]

This curve, the separatrix, runs from \(\theta = -\pi\) to \(\theta = \pi\) through a greatest speed \(2\omega_0\) at the bottom. It passes through the unstable fixed point, and it bounds the region of libration: any motion starting inside stays inside for ever, because curves of constant \(H\) cannot cross.

The period grows with amplitude. From the energy integral, \(dt = d\theta/\dot\theta\), and a quarter swing from \(0\) to the amplitude \(\theta_0\) takes \(\int_0^{\theta_0} d\theta/\sqrt{2\omega_0^2(\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0)}\). Landau and Lifshitz work this into a standard form: the period is

\[ T = \frac{4}{\omega_0}\,K\!\left(\sin\frac{\theta_0}{2}\right), \]

where \(K(k) = \int_0^{\pi/2} d\xi/\sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2\xi}\) is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind (the substitution is \(\sin(\theta/2) = k\sin\xi\) with \(k = \sin(\theta_0/2)\)). \(K(0) = \pi/2\) recovers \(T_0 = 2\pi/\omega_0\). Numerically, \(T/T_0\) is 1.017 at a 30° amplitude, 1.180 at 90°, 1.762 at 150° and 3.90 at 179°. As \(\theta_0 \to \pi\), \(K\) grows without limit like a logarithm: on the separatrix the period is infinite, because the pendulum creeps towards the top ever more slowly. So in a bunch the particles near the edge of the stable region oscillate more slowly than those near its centre.

A constant torque added. Section 2’s equation, with \(n\) the turn number, is

\[ \frac{d^2\phi}{dn^2} = a\left(\sin\phi - \sin\phi_s\right), \qquad a = \frac{2\pi h\,\eta\,q\hat{V}_t}{\beta^2E}\ \ (\text{rad per turn}^2). \]

Take the synchrocyclotron’s case, above transition: \(\eta > 0\), so \(a > 0\), with \(\pi/2 < \phi_s < \pi\). The term \(a\sin\phi\) is a pendulum’s torque, and \(-a\sin\phi_s\) is a constant torque, like the weight on a cord wound round the pendulum’s hub in Livingston and Blewett’s model. The potential, from \(d^2\phi/dn^2 = -dU/d\phi\), is

\[ U(\phi) = a\left(\cos\phi + \phi\sin\phi_s\right), \]

a cosine tilted by a straight line, often called a tilted washboard (Figure 12.1, top). The energy integral is \(\tfrac12(d\phi/dn)^2 + U(\phi) = H\).

Fixed points. The torque vanishes where \(\sin\phi = \sin\phi_s\): at \(\phi_s\) and at \(\pi - \phi_s\). The curvature \(U'' = -a\cos\phi\) is positive at \(\phi_s\) (since \(\cos\phi_s < 0\)), a valley: the stable fixed point. At \(\pi - \phi_s\) it is \(a\cos\phi_s < 0\), a hill: the unstable fixed point. Linearising there, \(\phi = \pi - \phi_s + u\) gives \(u'' = -a\cos\phi_s\,u = (2\pi Q_s)^2u\), so a small error grows by \(e^{2\pi Q_s}\) each turn, at the same rate at which the stable point oscillates.

The bucket. The separatrix is the curve of constant \(H\) through the unstable point, \(H_{sx} = U(\pi - \phi_s)\). Because the washboard is tilted, the valley around \(\phi_s\) is bounded on one side by the hill at \(\pi - \phi_s\) and on the other by the point \(\phi_u\) where the tilted potential climbs back to \(H_{sx}\): \(U(\phi_u) = U(\pi - \phi_s)\), a transcendental equation solved numerically. Inside, particles oscillate; outside, they slide down the washboard turn after turn, slipping in phase without limit and gaining no net energy. The greatest \(d\phi/dn\) on the separatrix is at \(\phi_s\):

\[ \left(\frac{d\phi}{dn}\right)_{\max}^2 = 2\left[U(\pi - \phi_s) - U(\phi_s)\right] = 2a\left[-2\cos\phi_s + (\pi - 2\phi_s)\sin\phi_s\right]. \]

For \(\phi_s = \pi\), no acceleration, the bracket is 2 and the bucket stretches the full \(2\pi\): a stationary bucket, height \(2\sqrt{a}\), the pendulum’s \(2\omega_0\). As \(\sin\phi_s\) grows, the constant torque grows, the valley becomes shallower and narrower, and at \(\sin\phi_s = 1\) the two fixed points merge and the bucket vanishes. At work. Convert the height to energy with the map’s second line, \(\Delta E = (\beta^2E/2\pi h\eta)\,d\phi/dn\). 2. Phase stability

The picture. A gap carries \(\hat{V}\sin\omega_{RF}t\), with \(\hat{V}\) the peak voltage (V) and \(\omega_{RF}\) the radio-frequency angular frequency (rad/s). A particle’s phase \(\phi\) is \(\omega_{RF}t\) at the moment it crosses (taken modulo \(2\pi\), in rad or degrees), so that later arrival means larger \(\phi\), and it gains \(\Delta W = q\hat{V}\sin\phi\). The synchronous particle is the ideal particle whose energy matches the frequency exactly at every moment; it crosses at the same phase \(\phi_s\), the synchronous phase, every turn. A neighbour with extra energy arrives next turn earlier if \(\eta < 0\) and later if \(\eta > 0\). For stability its change of arrival must reduce its energy gain.

Below transition it arrives earlier, at smaller \(\phi\), so \(\sin\phi\) must rise with \(\phi\): \(0 < \phi_s < \pi/2\), the rising side of the wave. Above transition it arrives later, so \(\sin\phi\) must fall: \(\pi/2 < \phi_s < \pi\). The synchrocyclotron’s particles ride the falling side (Figure 12.2).

Figure 12.2. The energy gain per turn against the phase at which a particle crosses the gaps, for the model’s 40 kV per turn. The synchronous particle (red) crosses at 150° and gains 20 keV, the dashed line. Above transition, as in every synchrocyclotron, a particle that crosses early (125°) gains more, 33 keV; more energy makes its turn longer, so next time it comes later, towards 150°. One that crosses late (175°) gains only 3.5 keV, its turn is shorter, and it comes sooner. The errors correct themselves and the particles oscillate about \(\phi_s\). At the mirror point, 30°, the same gain would be stable only below transition, where more energy means a shorter turn.

The equations. Let the synchronous particle have total energy \(E = \gamma mc^2\), speed \(\beta c\) and period \(T_0\), with \(\omega_{RF} = h\omega_0\), \(h\) being the harmonic number, the whole number of radio-frequency cycles per revolution. A particle with energy excess \(\Delta E\) has \(\delta = \Delta E/(\beta^2E)\), since \(dE = \beta^2E\,dp/p\): differentiate \(E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2\) to get \(E\,dE = c^2p\,dp\), so \(dE/E = (pc/E)^2\,dp/p\), and \(pc/E = \gamma m\beta c^2/(\gamma mc^2) = \beta\). Its period is \(T_0(1 + \eta\delta)\), longer by \(T_0\eta\delta\), so between passages its phase moves by that extra time multiplied by \(\omega_{RF}\): \(\omega_{RF}T_0\eta\delta = h\omega_0T_0\eta\delta = 2\pi h\eta\delta\), since \(\omega_0T_0 = 2\pi\). Its energy gain differs from the synchronous gain by \(q\hat{V}_t(\sin\phi - \sin\phi_s)\). Turn by turn:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta E_{n_t+1} &= \Delta E_{n_t} + q\hat{V}_t\left(\sin\phi_{n_t} - \sin\phi_s\right), \\ \phi_{n_t+1} &= \phi_{n_t} + \frac{2\pi h\,\eta}{\beta^2 E}\,\Delta E_{n_t+1}. \end{aligned} \]

These two lines contain all of longitudinal motion in a ring. When the changes per turn are small, treat \(n_t\) as continuous. Eliminating \(\Delta E\), with \(\eta\), \(\beta\) and \(E\) held constant over one oscillation,

\[ \frac{d^2\phi}{dn_t^2} = \frac{2\pi h\,\eta\,q\hat{V}_t}{\beta^2 E}\left(\sin\phi - \sin\phi_s\right), \]

a pendulum with a constant torque added. For small deviations \(\phi = \phi_s + \Delta\phi\), \(\sin\phi - \sin\phi_s \approx \cos\phi_s\,\Delta\phi\) (the first term of the Taylor series of \(\sin\) about \(\phi_s\)), and

\[ \frac{d^2\Delta\phi}{dn_t^2} + \left(2\pi Q_s\right)^2\Delta\phi = 0, \qquad Q_s^2 = -\frac{h\,\eta\,q\hat{V}_t\cos\phi_s}{2\pi\beta^2 E}. \]

This is the equation of simple harmonic motion, with the turn number in place of time, if \((2\pi Q_s)^2\) is positive. The motion oscillates when \(\eta\cos\phi_s < 0\), the condition found in words; if \(\eta\cos\phi_s > 0\) the sign flips and \(\Delta\phi\) grows exponentially. \(Q_s\) is the synchrotron tune, oscillations per turn; the synchrotron angular frequency is \(\Omega_s = Q_s\omega_0\).

What this leaves out. The full pendulum has a separatrix. Inside it, the bucket (Figure 12.1), particles oscillate. Outside, they slip without limit and are not accelerated. In the sine convention the bucket’s unstable fixed point is at \(\pi - \phi_s\). The bucket shrinks as \(\sin\phi_s\) grows. When parameters change slowly over many oscillations, the phase-space area of an oscillation is an adiabatic invariant (Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §49), so oscillations in \(\phi\) slowly shrink as the energy rises. Buckets and large amplitudes are derived in chapter 19.

The other convention. Linear-accelerator texts write \(\Delta W = q\mathcal{E}_0T_tL\cos\phi\) (\(\mathcal{E}_0\) the average accelerating field along the axis, V/m, \(L\) the length of the cell, m, and \(T_t\) the transit-time factor, a number below 1 for the field changing while the particle crosses; chapter 14 defines them), crest at \(0\), stable phases below transition between \(-90^\circ\) and \(0\). Their phase is the course’s \(\phi - 90^\circ\): a linac \(-30^\circ\) is a sine \(60^\circ\). Cyclotron papers also often put the crest at \(0^\circ\).

3. The synchrocyclotron

Following the particle. The magnet is static, with a field falling gently outward (\(0 < n < 1\)). The frequency is swept down so that \(\omega_{RF}(t) = h\,qB(R_s)/(\gamma_s m)\) for the synchronous particle; the rise of \(\gamma_s\) and the fall of \(B\) with radius both lower it. Each sweep catches one batch at the centre and carries it out.

Sweep rate and energy gain. Along the synchronous orbit \(d\omega_0/\omega_0 = -\eta\,dE/(\beta^2E)\): section 1’s \(d\omega_0/\omega_0 = -\eta\,dp/p\) with \(dp/p = dE/(\beta^2E)\) from section 2. The energy rises by \(q\hat{V}_t\sin\phi_s\) per turn and there are \(f_0\) turns a second, so \(dE/dt = f_0\,q\hat{V}_t\sin\phi_s\). The radio frequency must follow, \(f_{RF} = hf_0\), so \(df_{RF}/f_{RF} = d\omega_0/\omega_0\); dividing by \(dt\),

\[ \frac{df_{RF}}{dt} = f_{RF}\,\frac{1}{\omega_0}\frac{d\omega_0}{dt} = -\frac{f_{RF}\,\eta}{\beta^2 E}\,f_0\,q\hat{V}_t\sin\phi_s . \]

This ties the sweep, the voltage and the synchronous phase together. Sweep faster than the voltage allows and \(\sin\phi_s \to 1\), the bucket shrinks to nothing, and the beam is lost.

Worked example: CERN’s synchrocyclotron rebuilt. The new radio-frequency system (Kannowade 1971) was specified to sweep from 30 MHz to a final frequency between 16.77 and 17.3 MHz, at 350 to 600 Hz, accelerating for 65 per cent of each period, with 10 to 30 kV at the ion source. The rotating capacitor (Beger 1971) was designed for 466 Hz, using 64 per cent of each period. Protons reached 600 MeV at \(h = 1\).

  • Centre field. \(B_0 = 2\pi f_{RF}m_p/e = 1.968\) T for 30 MHz (protons: 15.25 MHz per tesla).
  • Extraction. At 600 MeV, \(\gamma = 1.6395\), \(\beta = 0.7924\), \(p = 1219\) MeV/\(c\), \(B\rho = 4.066\) T m. For 16.77 to 17.3 MHz, \(B = 2\pi f\gamma m_p/e = 1.80\) to 1.86 T and the radius is 2.19 to 2.25 m. The frequency falls by 1.73 to 1.79, more than \(\gamma\) (1.64), because the field also falls.
  • The sweep. At 466 Hz the period is 2.146 ms, so acceleration lasts 1.373 ms.
  • A model. Take \(B(r) = B_0[1 - a(r/r_x)^2]\) with \(a = 0.071\), so that 600 MeV is reached at 17.0 MHz near \(r_x = 2.22\) m (at 2.225 m). This field shape is an assumption for the example, not CERN’s field map. With 20 kV at each of two gap crossings per turn (\(\hat{V}_t = 40\) kV) and \(\phi_s = 150^\circ\), the gain is 20 keV per turn and 600 MeV takes 30,000 turns. Summing the periods along the frequency curve gives 1.366 ms, matching the sweep. Figure 12.3 shows the frequency the sweep must follow.

The sweep is fastest at the start, about 20 MHz per millisecond. A particle makes about 70 phase oscillations during acceleration, so the slow-change assumption holds. \(Q_s\) hardly changes: near the centre \(\eta/\beta^2 \approx 1\), so \(Q_s^2 \approx q\hat{V}_t|\cos\phi_s|/(2\pi E)\).

Transverse focusing. With rotational symmetry \(Q_r = \sqrt{1-n}\) and \(Q_z = \sqrt{n}\), the tunes (oscillations per turn) of weak focusing derived in chapter 10 and chapter 11: at the model’s edge \(Q_z = 0.39\) and \(Q_r = 0.92\). Near the centre \(n \to 0\) and the gap’s electric focusing and a shaped central field carry the beam (Blosser 1969).

Capture, pulses and duty factor. A particle is captured only while the frequency is near its own at the centre, a short window at the start of each sweep. Blosser estimated this duty cycle of capture to be of order 1 in 100, even in the improved machines, so 10 µA of final current needs about 1 mA while ions are being captured. He also noted that across machines the current rose roughly linearly with the repetition rate.

The duty factor \(D_f\) is the fraction of time with beam, and there are two.

  • The macro duty factor, the fraction of each sweep spent accelerating: about 40 per cent in most machines in 1971 (Beger), 64 per cent for CERN’s new capacitor.
  • The beam duty factor. With simple extraction a batch arrives at the edge within about one synchrotron period, 27 µs in the model. At CERN’s original 54 Hz the beam is then present 0.15 per cent of the time; at 466 Hz, 1.3 per cent.

Coincidence experiments suffer from such peaks. These look for two detectors firing within a few nanoseconds of each other, from one event; two unrelated particles arriving together fake a coincidence, and the rate of such accidental coincidences goes as the square of the instantaneous counting rate, so it is the peak rate that matters, not the average. So most synchrocyclotrons stored the beam at the edge and peeled it off slowly, by an auxiliary electrode, the “Gee”, with its own slowly modulated frequency, or by a slowly varied magnetic bump. Lindbäck (1971) expected total duty cycles of about 15 per cent from the most promising electrode scheme on the improved machines, and better from magnetic bump coils.

4. The isochronous field and its vertical defocusing

Keep the frequency fixed and shape the field so that every orbit takes the same time. With fixed \(\omega_0\), \(v = \omega_0R\), so \(\gamma(R) = 1/\sqrt{1-(\omega_0R/c)^2}\), and \(p = \gamma m\omega_0R = qBR\) requires

\[ \bar{B}(R) = \frac{\gamma(R)\,m\,\omega_0}{q} = \gamma(R)\,B_0, \qquad n = -\frac{R}{\gamma}\frac{d\gamma}{dR} = -\gamma^2\beta^2 = -(\gamma^2 - 1), \]

using \(d\gamma/d\beta = \gamma^3\beta\) (differentiate \(\gamma = (1-\beta^2)^{-1/2}\): \(-\tfrac12(1-\beta^2)^{-3/2}\times(-2\beta)\)) and \(d\beta/dR = \beta/R\) (since \(\beta = \omega_0R/c\) is proportional to \(R\)). So \((R/\gamma)\,d\gamma/dR = (R/\gamma)\,\gamma^3\beta\,(\beta/R) = \gamma^2\beta^2\), and \(\gamma^2\beta^2 = \gamma^2 - \gamma^2(1-\beta^2) = \gamma^2 - 1\). Then \(\alpha_c = 1/(1-n) = 1/\gamma^2\) and \(\eta = 0\). With rotational symmetry \(Q_z^2 = n < 0\): the vertical equation \(z'' + nz = 0\) has a negative coefficient, and, as chapter 10 showed, its solutions are \(e^{\pm\sqrt{-n}\,\theta}\): the vertical motion grows exponentially. Radially \(Q_r^2 = 1 - n = \gamma^2\), so \(Q_r = \gamma\), which survives the azimuthal variation to a good approximation.

The PSI ring cyclotron. Its cavities run at 50.6 MHz with \(h = 6\) (Seidel 2010, 2013), so \(f_0 = 8.433\) MHz. At 72 MeV, \(\beta = 0.371\) and \(R = \beta c/(2\pi f_0) = 2.10\) m. At 590 MeV, \(\beta = 0.789\), \(\gamma = 1.629\), \(R = 4.47\) m. Seidel lists 2.1 and 4.5 m. The mean field at extraction is \(B\rho/R = 4.024/4.47 = 0.90\) T, though the magnets reach 2 T. The required index there is \(n = -1.65\), and the mean field must rise by 1.51 across the machine.

Pre-requisite (continued): Fourier series and transforms

Pre-requisite. This section gives you the Fourier series of a function that repeats around a circle: how to find its harmonics, why averages such as \(\langle\sin^2\rangle = 1/2\) hold, and Parseval’s rule that the mean square of a periodic function is the sum of the squares of its harmonics. Section 5 uses it to write the cyclotron field as \(\bar{B}(1 + A\cos N_s\theta)\), to average the focusing over a turn, and to define the flutter \(F\); section 7 uses it for the first-harmonic field error. Taught from Penrose, The Road to Reality, §9.1 (Fourier series), and S. Y. Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, section III.1 (field errors around a ring expanded in harmonics, the stopband integrals).

Chapter 8’s section on Fourier analysis taught the Fourier transform of a signal in time, and chapter 9 used the transforms of symmetric profiles. A field measured around a closed orbit is different: it is a function of azimuth \(\theta\) that repeats every \(2\pi\). Such a function is built from a countable set of harmonics, \(\cos k\theta\) and \(\sin k\theta\) with \(k = 0, 1, 2, \dots\), rather than a continuum of frequencies.

The series. Any reasonable function with \(f(\theta + 2\pi) = f(\theta)\) can be written

\[ f(\theta) = a_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(a_k\cos k\theta + b_k\sin k\theta\right). \]

\(a_0\) is the mean, \(a_k\) and \(b_k\) the harmonic amplitudes in the same unit as \(f\) (tesla, for a field). The \(k\)th harmonic goes through \(k\) cycles around the circle: the first harmonic is one bump per turn, a field with \(N_s\) identical sectors contains only harmonics \(N_s\), \(2N_s\), and so on.

Finding the coefficients. The key is orthogonality. Over a full turn,

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_0^{2\pi}\cos k\theta\,\cos m\theta\,d\theta &= \int_0^{2\pi}\sin k\theta\,\sin m\theta\,d\theta = \pi\,\delta_{km}, \\ \int_0^{2\pi}\cos k\theta\,\sin m\theta\,d\theta &= 0, \end{aligned} \]

for \(k, m \ge 1\), with \(\delta_{km} = 1\) if \(k = m\) and 0 otherwise. To prove the first, write \(\cos k\theta\cos m\theta = \tfrac12[\cos(k-m)\theta + \cos(k+m)\theta]\): each cosine integrates to zero over a whole number of cycles unless its argument is zero, which happens only for \(k = m\) and gives \(\tfrac12\times2\pi\). The others follow the same way. Now multiply the series by \(\cos m\theta\) and integrate: every term vanishes except the one with \(k = m\), so

\[ a_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f\,d\theta, \qquad a_m = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f\cos m\theta\,d\theta, \qquad b_m = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f\sin m\theta\,d\theta . \]

The averages used in section 5 are the case \(k = m\) divided by \(2\pi\): \(\langle\cos^2 N_s\theta\rangle = \langle\sin^2 N_s\theta\rangle = 1/2\), and \(\langle\sin N_s\theta\,\cos N_s\theta\rangle = 0\).

Parseval’s rule. Square the series and average over a turn. Every cross term averages to zero by orthogonality, and each squared term gives \(\tfrac12\) of its amplitude squared:

\[ \langle f^2\rangle = a_0^2 + \frac12\sum_{k\ge1}\left(a_k^2 + b_k^2\right). \]

So the mean square variation about the mean, \(\langle f^2\rangle - \langle f\rangle^2\), is half the sum of the squared harmonic amplitudes. Applied to the field, with amplitudes measured relative to \(\bar{B} = a_0\), this is the flutter:

\[ F = \frac{\langle B^2\rangle - \langle B\rangle^2}{\langle B\rangle^2} = \frac12\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{a_k^2 + b_k^2}{\bar{B}^2}. \]

For one harmonic of relative amplitude \(A\), \(F = A^2/2\), as section 5 states; for several, the squares add.

At work: hard-edged hills. Take \(N_s = 4\) sectors, each with a hill of field \(B_h = 1.9\) T over a fraction \(\alpha = 0.5\) of the sector and a valley of \(B_v = 0.9\) T over the rest. The mean is \(\bar{B} = \alpha B_h + (1-\alpha)B_v = 1.40\) T. The field is a square wave in \(N_s\theta\); with the hill centred on \(\theta = 0\), the integrals above give relative amplitudes of the harmonics \(jN_s\),

\[ \frac{a_{jN_s}}{\bar{B}} = \frac{2(B_h - B_v)}{\pi j\,\bar{B}}\sin(\pi j\alpha) , \]

0.455 for \(j = 1\) (the fourth harmonic in \(\theta\)), 0 for \(j = 2\), \(-0.152\) for \(j = 3\). Summing half the squares over \(j\) gives \(F = 0.1276\), the same as the direct formula \(\alpha(1-\alpha)(B_h - B_v)^2/\bar{B}^2 = 0.1276\) quoted in section 5. The first term alone carries \(8/\pi^2 = 81\) per cent of it, which is why the one-harmonic theory works tolerably for real hills.

At work: finding a field error. A field mapper measures \(B\) at \(M\) equally spaced azimuths around a circle. The integrals become sums, \(a_k \approx (2/M)\sum_j B(\theta_j)\cos k\theta_j\), which are exact for every harmonic below \(M/2\) if the field has none at or above it. (Harmonics \(k\) and \(M - k\) look identical at the sample points, which is why the samples must be dense.) A synthetic test: a 1.2 T field with a 25 per cent fourth harmonic and a first harmonic of 2 G (two parts in \(10^4\)) at 40°, sampled every 10°. The sums return the 2 G at 40° exactly, untouched by a fourth harmonic 1500 times larger, because the two are orthogonal. This is how the first-harmonic errors of section 7 are found and then cancelled with trim coils.

5. Thomas focusing: hills, valleys and the scalloped orbit

The picture. Let the field alternate between \(N_s\) strong hills and \(N_s\) weak valleys. In a hill the orbit bends sharply; in a valley it runs nearly straight. The closed orbit becomes a rounded polygon, bulging outward in the hills (Figure 12.4), so it crosses each hill edge with a radial velocity component. Off the median plane, at a hill edge, the field has an azimuthal component. Radial velocity times azimuthal field gives a vertical force. Both factors change sign between entry and exit, so the force points towards the median plane at both edges. This is the edge focusing of a bending magnet (chapter 16), repeated at every hill edge.

Figure 12.4. Four radial hills (shaded, strong field) and valleys (weak field), seen from above, with the field pointing into the page so that a positive particle going anticlockwise is bent inward. The red closed orbit is \(r = R[1 + A\cos(4\theta)/15]\) for \(A = 0.3\), with its scallop drawn three times larger than the true 2 per cent. It bends sharply in the hills and runs nearly straight in the valleys, so it lies outside the mean circle (dashed) in the hills and inside it in the valleys. Where it enters a hill it is moving outward, where it leaves inward. Off the median plane the field at a hill edge has an azimuthal part \(B_\theta\) that also changes sign between entry and exit, since the field rises at one edge and falls at the other; the product of \(v_r\) and \(B_\theta\), which gives the vertical force, therefore has the same sign at both edges, and pushes towards the median plane.

The derivation. Use cylindrical coordinates \((r, \theta, z)\) with motion towards increasing \(\theta\), and write the median-plane field as \(B_z = -B(r,\theta)\), \(B > 0\), so that the force on a positive particle points inward; \(z\) is measured from the median plane, the plane of symmetry halfway between the poles. No current flows in the gap, so by Ampère’s law the field there has no curl. Two components of \(\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = 0\) in cylindrical coordinates read \(\partial B_r/\partial z = \partial B_z/\partial r\) and \(\partial B_\theta/\partial z = (1/r)\,\partial B_z/\partial\theta\). On the median plane \(B_r = B_\theta = 0\), because the poles are mirror images of each other, so the horizontal components reverse across it. Integrating from \(z = 0\) to a small height \(z\) with \(B_z = -B\) gives, to first order in \(z\), the first two of the following; the third is the vertical component of the cross product \(q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\) in the right-handed order \((r, \theta, z)\):

\[ B_r = -z\frac{\partial B}{\partial r}, \qquad B_\theta = -\frac{z}{r}\frac{\partial B}{\partial\theta}, \qquad F_z = q(v_rB_\theta - v_\theta B_r) = q z\left(v_\theta\frac{\partial B}{\partial r} - \frac{v_r}{r}\frac{\partial B}{\partial\theta}\right). \]

With \(\theta\) as the independent variable (\(z' = dz/d\theta\)), \(v_\theta \approx v\), \(\dot\theta \approx v/r\) and \(\gamma mv = q\bar{B}r\), the equation \(\gamma m\ddot{z} = F_z\) becomes

\[ z'' = \frac{r}{\bar{B}}\frac{\partial B}{\partial r}\,z - \frac{1}{\bar{B}}\,\frac{1}{r}\frac{dr}{d\theta}\,\frac{\partial B}{\partial\theta}\,z . \]

To see this, note that the angular speed \(\dot\theta \approx v/r\) hardly changes, so \(\dot z = z'\dot\theta\) and \(\ddot z = z''\dot\theta^2 = z''v^2/r^2\). Then \(z'' = (r^2/v^2)\,\ddot z = r^2F_z/(\gamma mv^2)\), and \(r^2q/(\gamma mv^2) = r/(\bar{B}v)\) because \(\gamma mv = q\bar{B}r\). Finally \(v_r = dr/dt = (dr/d\theta)\,\dot\theta = (dr/d\theta)\,v/r\), which turns \(v_r/v\) into \((1/r)\,dr/d\theta\). Every quantity in the result is a pure number per radian squared.

The first term, for a symmetric field, is \(-nz\) (by the definition of the field index, \((r/\bar{B})\,\partial B/\partial r = -n\)): weak focusing.

For the second, take radial sectors with one harmonic, \(B = \bar{B}(1 + A\cos N_s\theta)\), with \(A\) the relative amplitude of the field’s variation (no unit). The closed orbit is \(r = R(1 + x)\), with \(R = \gamma mv/(q\bar{B})\) the radius in the mean field and \(x\) a small relative displacement. It follows from the radial equation of motion of chapter 10, \(\gamma m(\ddot r - v_\theta^2/r) = -qv_\theta B\): the radial acceleration is \(\ddot r\) less the centripetal acceleration \(v_\theta^2/r\) of motion on a circle, and the field pushes inward. To first order in \(x\), \(v_\theta \approx v\), \(\ddot r = R\,x''\dot\theta^2 \approx x''v^2/R\) and \(v^2/r \approx (v^2/R)(1 - x)\). Dividing by \(\gamma mv^2/R\) and using \(qR/(\gamma mv) = 1/\bar{B}\) gives \(x'' - 1 + x = -B/\bar{B}\). (Orbit theory often writes the same equation in \(u = 1/r\), as \(u'' + u = B/(\bar{B}R)\).) So

\[ x'' + x = -A\cos N_s\theta \quad\Rightarrow\quad x = \frac{A}{N_s^2 - 1}\cos N_s\theta . \]

The answer comes from trying \(x = C\cos N_s\theta\): \(-N_s^2C + C = -A\), so \(C = A/(N_s^2-1)\). It is the steady response of an oscillator of natural tune 1 driven at the harmonic \(N_s\) (F3), and it is largest where the field is largest, as pictured. Then, to first order, \(r^{-1}dr/d\theta = x' = -AN_s\sin(N_s\theta)/(N_s^2-1)\) and \(\bar{B}^{-1}\partial B/\partial\theta = -AN_s\sin N_s\theta\); their product, with the minus sign in front, makes the second term \(-[A^2N_s^2/(N_s^2-1)]\sin^2(N_s\theta)\,z\). It focuses everywhere except at the hill centres, where \(\sin N_s\theta = 0\). When the tune is small compared with \(N_s\) the particle feels the average, and with \(\langle\sin^2\rangle = 1/2\) (from the Fourier section),

\[ Q_z^2 = n + \frac{N_s^2}{N_s^2-1}\,F, \qquad F = \frac{\langle B^2\rangle - \langle B\rangle^2}{\langle B\rangle^2}. \]

\(F\) is the flutter, the mean square relative variation of the field around a circle. For one harmonic \(F = A^2/2\); for several, the squared amplitudes add. (Seidel writes the same quantity as \(F^2\).) For hard-edged hills of field \(B_h\) filling a fraction \(\alpha\) of the circle, and valleys of \(B_v\), \(F = \alpha(1-\alpha)(B_h - B_v)^2/\bar{B}^2\) (Kleeven and Zaremba); with field-free valleys, \(F = (1-\alpha)/\alpha\).

Thomas’s condition. For many sectors \(N_s^2/(N_s^2-1) \approx 1\), and \(Q_z^2 > 0\) with the isochronous \(n = -(\gamma^2-1)\) needs \(F > \gamma^2 - 1 = \beta^2\gamma^2\), or, with \(F = A^2/2\), \(A > \sqrt{2}\,\beta\gamma\): at low energy a relative variation of about \(\sqrt{2}\,v/c\), of the order of \(v/c\) as Thomas found. For protons \(A\) must exceed 0.21 at 10 MeV, 0.47 at 50 MeV and 0.67 at 100 MeV. The flutter needed rises with energy, and is limited by how deep the valleys can be made.

Pre-requisite (continued): Linear systems and transfer matrices

Pre-requisite. This section gives you two things: how to find the transfer matrix of one period of a ring by tracking two rays through it, when the focusing varies too smoothly to be split into simple elements, and the thin lens made by a magnet edge that the beam crosses at an angle, which focuses in one plane and defocuses in the other according to the sign of the tilt. The Floquet section next turns the one-period matrix into a tune and a test of stability, and section 6 uses the tilted edge to explain spiral-sector focusing. Taught from S. Y. Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, section II.1 (the transfer matrix built from the cosine-like and sine-like solutions) and exercise 2.2.2 (edge focusing), and Livingston and Blewett, section 15-11 (the alternating force at the hill edges of sector-focused cyclotrons).

Chapter 8’s section on transfer matrices built the matrices of a drift and a thin lens and multiplied them along a beam line. Here the line closes on itself, and its focusing comes from the shape of a field rather than from separate lenses.

The matrix of a period by tracking. Take the vertical motion of section 5, \(z'' = -K(\theta)\,z\), with \(\theta\) the azimuth as independent variable, \(z' = dz/d\theta\), and \(K(\theta)\) any function. Because the equation is linear, the state after one period, \((z, z')\) at \(\theta_0 + \Theta\), is a linear function of the state at \(\theta_0\):

\[ \begin{pmatrix} z \\ z' \end{pmatrix}_{\theta_0+\Theta} = M \begin{pmatrix} z \\ z' \end{pmatrix}_{\theta_0}, \qquad M = \begin{pmatrix} C & S \\ C' & S' \end{pmatrix}. \]

To find \(M\) there is no need to know a formula for the solution. Track two particles, by numerical integration if need be. The cosine-like ray starts with \((z, z') = (1, 0)\); where it ends is \((C, C')\), the first column. The sine-like ray starts with \((0, 1)\) and ends at \((S, S')\), the second column. Any other ray starts as \(z_0(1,0) + z_0'(0,1)\), so by linearity it ends at \(z_0(C, C') + z_0'(S, S')\), which is \(M\) times its start. Two runs give the whole matrix.

At work. Section 5’s radial-sector field with \(N_s = 4\), \(A = 0.3\) and \(n = 0\) gives, to the order kept there, \(K(\theta) = n + [A^2N_s^2/(N_s^2-1)]\sin^2N_s\theta\). Integrating the two rays across one sector, \(\Theta = 2\pi/4\), from the centre of a valley:

\[ M = \begin{pmatrix} 0.94134 & 1.53765 \\ -0.07406 & 0.94134 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Its determinant is \(0.94134^2 + 1.53765\times0.07406 = 1.00000\), and its two diagonal elements are equal because the sector is symmetric about its middle. The Floquet section below reads the vertical tune from it. The same two-ray method, run through a measured field map rather than a model, is what the closed-orbit codes of “How such a machine is designed today” do at every energy.

A tilted edge is a thin lens. A bending magnet of field \(B_0\) bends a particle of rigidity \(B\rho = p/q\) on a radius \(\rho = B\rho/B_0\). If the beam crosses the magnet’s edge square on, the edge does nothing special. If the edge is turned by an angle \(\varepsilon\) from that, it acts as a thin lens.

Horizontally. Let \(x\) be the horizontal distance from the reference orbit, outward positive, and turn the edge so that a particle further out meets the field later. At distance \(x\) the field begins a path length \(x\tan\varepsilon\) further on, so that particle is bent through less angle, by \(x\tan\varepsilon/\rho\). Since bending is inward, bending less is a kick outward, proportional to \(x\):

\[ \Delta x' = +\frac{\tan\varepsilon}{\rho}\,x, \]

with \(x' = dx/ds\) and \(s\) the path length. This is a thin defocusing lens of focal length \(\rho/\tan\varepsilon\).

Vertically. Off the median plane the fringe field at the edge is not purely vertical. Let \(w\) (m) be the distance along the edge’s normal, horizontal and square to the edge (written \(w\) so as not to confuse it with the field index \(n\)), and \(B_n\) the field component along that normal. The gap field has no curl, so \(\partial B_n/\partial z = \partial B_z/\partial w\), and at height \(z\) to first order \(B_n = z\,\partial B_z/\partial w\) (it vanishes on the median plane, as in section 5), a field pointing across the edge wherever \(B_z\) is changing. The beam crosses the edge at angle \(\varepsilon\) to its normal, so \(B_n\) has a horizontal component \(B_n\sin\varepsilon\) across the beam, which with the beam’s velocity gives a vertical force \(qvB_n\sin\varepsilon\). A path element \(ds\) covers \(dw = ds\cos\varepsilon\), so \(ds = dw/\cos\varepsilon\), \(\sin\varepsilon/\cos\varepsilon = \tan\varepsilon\), and the total vertical kick across the fringe, in which \(B_z\) rises from 0 to \(B_0\), is

\[ \Delta p_z = q\int B_n\sin\varepsilon\,ds = q\,z\tan\varepsilon\int\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial w}\,dw = q\,z\,B_0\tan\varepsilon . \]

Dividing by \(p = qB_0\rho\), the vertical slope changes by \(z\tan\varepsilon/\rho\) in size: a thin lens of the same strength. Its sign is opposite to the horizontal one. A thin slice of a field free of curl and divergence behaves like a quadrupole, whose focusing in one plane equals its defocusing in the other. So:

\[ \frac{1}{f_x} = -\frac{\tan\varepsilon}{\rho}, \qquad \frac{1}{f_z} = +\frac{\tan\varepsilon}{\rho}, \]

for an edge turned so that outer particles meet less field; turn it the other way and both signs flip. This is the edge focusing of a rectangular bending magnet, which has no vertical focusing from its body but gains it from its edges (chapter 16).

At work. In the PSI ring at 590 MeV, \(B\rho = 4.024\) T m in hills of 2 T, so \(\rho = 2.01\) m. A hill edge that the beam crosses 30° from square is a lens of focal length \(2.01/\tan30^\circ = 3.49\) m. In a spiral sector the beam crosses one edge of a hill leaning one way and the other edge leaning the other way, so the two edges have focal lengths \(+f\) and \(-f\) vertically. Between them the hill’s uniform field exerts no vertical force, so it is a drift of length \(d\) for vertical motion. The pair has the matrix (thin lens, drift, thin lens)

\[ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ \pm1/f & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & d \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ \mp1/f & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \text{lower-left element } -\frac{d}{f^2} \]

in either order: a net focusing lens of focal length \(f^2/d\). For an assumed \(d = 1.5\) m, \(f^2/d = 8.1\) m. The individual lenses are strong but cancel to first order; what remains is second order and always focusing. This is alternating-gradient focusing in its simplest form, and the reason section 6’s spiral adds to \(Q_z^2\).

Pre-requisite: Floquet theory

Pre-requisite. This section gives you the theory of linear motion under focusing that repeats with a fixed period: why the one-period matrix has determinant 1, how its trace decides at once whether the motion is stable, how the phase advance per period and so the tune follow from the trace, and Floquet’s theorem on the form of the solutions. Section 6 uses it for the tune of a cyclotron with sectors, read from a one-sector matrix found by tracking, and for the boundary between stable and unstable vertical motion. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §27 (equations with periodic coefficients, the multipliers and their product), and S. Y. Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, sections II.1 to II.3 (transfer matrix, stability condition, Courant–Snyder form, Floquet transformation).

The idea. In a cyclotron with \(N_s\) sectors, the focusing a particle feels repeats every sector. The vertical motion obeys Hill’s equation (chapter 11) \(z'' + K(\theta)z = 0\) with \(K(\theta + \Theta) = K(\theta)\) and period \(\Theta = 2\pi/N_s\). If \(M\) is the matrix of one period, as found by tracking in the section above, then after \(k\) periods the state is \(M^k\) times the start. The motion is stable if \(M^k\) stays bounded for all \(k\), and unstable if it grows. One matrix, found once, decides the fate of every particle for ever.

The determinant is 1. Let \(z_1\) and \(z_2\) be two solutions, and form the Wronskian \(W = z_1z_2' - z_1'z_2\). Its derivative is \(z_1z_2'' - z_1''z_2 = z_1(-Kz_2) - (-Kz_1)z_2 = 0\), whatever \(K\) is: \(W\) is constant. Take \(z_1\) the cosine-like ray and \(z_2\) the sine-like ray. At the start \(W = 1\times1 - 0\times0 = 1\); at the end of the period \(W = CS' - SC' = \det M\). So \(\det M = 1\). (Landau and Lifshitz reach the same fact as the product of the two multipliers being 1, their equation 27.5.) Geometrically, a matrix of determinant 1 keeps areas in the \((z, z')\) plane: this is Liouville’s theorem of chapter 7 for linear motion.

The eigenvalues and the trace. Look for rays that come back multiplied by a number \(\lambda\) after a period, \(Mv = \lambda v\), with \(v\) a column \((z, z')\) that is not zero. Such a \(v\) is an eigenvector and \(\lambda\) an eigenvalue. Written as \((M - \lambda I)v = 0\), with \(I\) the unit matrix, this has a non-zero solution only if the matrix \(M - \lambda I\) squashes some area to nothing, that is, if its determinant is zero. So the eigenvalues solve \(\det(M - \lambda I) = (C - \lambda)(S' - \lambda) - SC' = 0\); for a \(2\times2\) matrix of determinant 1 this is

\[ \lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr}M)\,\lambda + 1 = 0, \qquad \lambda_1\lambda_2 = 1, \qquad \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = \operatorname{tr}M, \]

where \(\operatorname{tr}M = C + S'\) is the trace, the sum of the diagonal elements. Two cases:

  • \(|\operatorname{tr}M| < 2\). The roots are complex, \(\lambda = e^{\pm i\mu}\) with \(\cos\mu = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}M\) (by Euler’s formula, \(e^{i\mu} + e^{-i\mu} = 2\cos\mu\), and their product is 1 as it must be). Both have modulus 1. Any ray is a combination of the two eigenvectors, each of which only turns by \(\mu\) each period, so nothing grows: the motion is stable. \(\mu\) is the phase advance per period.
  • \(|\operatorname{tr}M| > 2\). The roots are real, one larger than 1 in size and one smaller. Almost every ray contains some of the growing eigenvector, which multiplies by \(|\lambda| > 1\) each period: the motion is unstable, exponentially.

The boundary \(|\operatorname{tr}M| = 2\) is \(\mu = 0\) or \(\mu = \pi\): a whole or a half oscillation per period. These are the edges of the stop bands of Hill’s equation, met again in the next section.

The tune. With \(N_s\) periods per turn, the betatron phase advances by \(N_s\mu\) per turn, so

\[ Q = \frac{N_s\,\mu}{2\pi}, \qquad \cos\mu = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}M . \]

One caution: \(\arccos\) returns \(\mu\) between 0 and \(\pi\), but the true phase advance could be \(2\pi - \mu\) or have whole turns of \(2\pi\) added. The matrix of one period cannot tell; following the ray through the period, or a rough smooth estimate, settles it. For the vertical motion of a cyclotron, where \(\mu\) is small, there is no doubt.

Floquet’s theorem. Take a stable eigen-solution, \(z(\theta + \Theta) = e^{i\mu}z(\theta)\). Define \(P(\theta) = e^{-i\mu\theta/\Theta}z(\theta)\). Then \(P(\theta + \Theta) = e^{-i\mu}e^{-i\mu\theta/\Theta}\,e^{i\mu}z(\theta) = P(\theta)\): \(P\) is periodic with the period of the focusing. So

\[ z(\theta) = P(\theta)\,e^{i\mu\theta/\Theta}, \]

an oscillation that advances steadily by \(\mu\) per period, multiplied by an envelope that repeats every period (Landau and Lifshitz, equation 27.3). The real motion is a combination of this and its complex conjugate. In a ring this envelope becomes the betatron function of chapter 16: Lee writes the one-period matrix as

\[ M = I\cos\mu + J\sin\mu, \qquad J = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha_z & \beta_z \\ -\gamma_z & -\alpha_z\end{pmatrix}, \]

so its upper-right element is \(\beta_z\sin\mu\).

At work: a tune from a tracked matrix. The one-sector matrix of the section above, for four radial sectors with \(A = 0.3\), has \(\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}M = 0.94134\). So \(\mu = 19.72^\circ\), \(Q_z = 4\times19.72/360 = 0.2191\) and \(Q_z^2 = 0.0480\): the value of section 5’s averaged formula, \(n + [N_s^2/(N_s^2-1)]F = 0.048\). The upper-right element gives \(\beta_z = 1.5377/\sin19.72^\circ = 4.56\) in units of \(\theta\) (multiply by the radius for metres), close to the smooth estimate \(1/Q_z = 4.56\).

At work: focusing with no average. Let the focusing be a square wave: \(K = +k^2\) for a length \(L\), then \(-k^2\) for a length \(L\), repeating, so its average is zero. The focusing half has the matrix on the left and the defocusing half the one on the right:

\[ \begin{pmatrix}\cos kL & \sin kL/k \\ -k\sin kL & \cos kL\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \begin{pmatrix}\cosh kL & \sinh kL/k \\ k\sinh kL & \cosh kL\end{pmatrix}. \]

Multiplying them, the trace is

\[ \operatorname{tr}M = 2\cos kL\,\cosh kL . \]

For small \(kL\), multiply the Taylor series \(\cos x = 1 - x^2/2 + x^4/24\) and \(\cosh x = 1 + x^2/2 + x^4/24\): the \(x^2\) terms cancel and the \(x^4\) terms add to \(1/24 + 1/24 - 1/4 = -1/6\), so \(\cos x\cosh x = 1 - x^4/6 + \dots\) Then \(\cos\mu = 1 - (kL)^4/6\), and comparing with \(\cos\mu \approx 1 - \mu^2/2\) gives \(\mu \approx (kL)^2/\sqrt3\): stable, with a real phase advance, although the average focusing is zero. For \(kL = 1\), \(\mu = 33.5^\circ\); for \(kL = 1.5\), 80.4°. At \(kL = 1.875\) the trace reaches \(-2\) and beyond it the motion is unstable: alternating gradients focus, but only up to a strength (Figure 12.5). This is the arithmetic behind section 6’s “focusing on balance”.

With four radial sectors of \(A = 0.5\), tracking one sector gives \(\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}M = 1.0317\), beyond 1. The larger eigenvalue is 1.285 per sector, 2.73 per turn: a small vertical error grows 23,000-fold in ten turns. Section 6’s table finds the same field stable once the sectors spiral by 30°.

Pre-requisite (continued): Parametric resonance, Hill and Mathieu equations

Pre-requisite. This section gives you two results for Hill’s equation when the focusing has a strong oscillating part, \(z'' = -[K_0 + G\cos N\theta]\,z\): averaging over the fast oscillation, which leaves a net focusing \(G^2/2N^2\) even when the oscillating part has no average; and parametric resonance, the instability in a band of tunes around half the driving harmonic. Section 6 uses the first for the spiral’s contribution to \(Q_z^2\); section 7 uses the second for the structural resonance \(Q_r = N_s/2\) and the \(2Q_r = 3\) stop band. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §27 (parametric resonance, the band of instability and its growth rate) and §30 (motion in a rapidly oscillating field), and S. Y. Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, section III.4 (the half-integer stopband).

Chapter 11’s section wrote Hill’s equation \(x'' + K(s)x = 0\) and solved it for constant \(K\), with the tune as oscillations per turn. The Floquet section above gave the exact test for a periodic \(K\). Here are two approximate results for the commonest periodic \(K\), a constant plus one harmonic:

\[ z'' = -\left[K_0 + G\cos N\theta\right]z , \]

with \(\theta\) the azimuth, \(K_0\) the average focusing (in \(\text{rad}^{-2}\), so that \(\sqrt{K_0}\) is a tune), \(G\) the amplitude of the alternating part and \(N\) its harmonic number.

Averaging over a fast oscillation. Suppose \(N\) is large compared with \(\sqrt{K_0}\) and \(\sqrt{G}\), so the force alternates many times during one slow oscillation. Split the motion into a slow part \(Z\) and a small fast wiggle \(\zeta\): \(z = Z + \zeta\). The fast wiggle is driven by the fast force acting on the slow position: \(\zeta'' \approx -G\cos(N\theta)\,Z\). Over one fast cycle \(Z\) hardly changes, so integrate twice holding \(Z\) fixed:

\[ \zeta = \frac{G}{N^2}\cos(N\theta)\,Z . \]

Now average the force over a fast cycle. The force is \(-[K_0 + G\cos N\theta](Z + \zeta)\). The term \(-G\cos(N\theta)Z\) averages to zero. The term \(-G\cos(N\theta)\,\zeta = -(G^2/N^2)\cos^2(N\theta)\,Z\) does not: \(\cos^2\) averages to \(1/2\). Dropping the small \(-K_0\zeta\), which averages to zero, the slow motion obeys

\[ Z'' = -\left[K_0 + \frac{G^2}{2N^2}\right]Z . \]

The wiggle is in step with the force: the particle is displaced further from the axis exactly when the force is focusing, and nearer when it is defocusing, so the focusing half-cycles win. Landau and Lifshitz (§30) derive the same as an effective potential, the original potential plus the mean square of the fast force divided by twice the square of its frequency; Kapitza used it to show that a pendulum whose pivot is shaken fast enough can stand upside down.

At work. In section 6, a spiral sector gives \(G = AN_s\tan\xi\). For \(N_s = 4\), \(A = 0.3\) and \(\xi = 45^\circ\), \(G = 1.2\) and \(G^2/2N_s^2 = 0.0450\), which is \(F\tan^2\xi\) with \(F = A^2/2\). Checking with the exact one-period matrix of the Floquet section (for \(K_0 = 0\)): \(Q^2 = 0.0454\). For \(G = 2\) the average gives 0.125 and the exact value is 0.128; the approximation weakens as \(G/N^2\) grows, as it must.

Parametric resonance. Now take the alternating part small instead, and write the equation in Mathieu’s form:

\[ z'' + Q_0^2\left[1 + h\cos N\theta\right]z = 0, \qquad h \ll 1 , \]

with \(Q_0\) the unperturbed tune and \(h = G/Q_0^2\) the relative depth of modulation. Most of the time the modulation does little. But if the stiffness rises and falls twice per oscillation, \(N \approx 2Q_0\), each rise can meet the particle at the same point of its swing, and energy is pumped in every cycle, as a child on a swing pumps by standing and crouching twice per swing. Write \(N = 2Q_0 + \varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon\) small, and look for a solution near the natural one with slowly varying amplitudes,

\[ z = a(\theta)\cos\frac{N\theta}{2} + b(\theta)\sin\frac{N\theta}{2} . \]

Substitute, use \(\cos u\cos2u = \tfrac12[\cos u + \cos3u]\) and \(\sin u\cos2u = \tfrac12[-\sin u + \sin3u]\) with \(u = N\theta/2\), drop the terms in \(3u\) (they are far from resonance), drop \(a''\), \(b''\) and products of small quantities, and collect the coefficients of \(\cos u\) and \(\sin u\). In detail, with \(a\) and \(b\) changing slowly, \(z'' \approx Nb'\cos u - Na'\sin u - (N/2)^2(a\cos u + b\sin u)\), and the modulation adds \(\tfrac12Q_0^2h(a\cos u - b\sin u)\) to \(Q_0^2z\). The coefficient of \(\cos u\) in the equation is then \(Nb' - [(N/2)^2 - Q_0^2]\,a + \tfrac12Q_0^2h\,a = 0\), and that of \(\sin u\) is \(-Na' - [(N/2)^2 - Q_0^2]\,b - \tfrac12Q_0^2h\,b = 0\). Near resonance \((N/2)^2 - Q_0^2 = (N/2 - Q_0)(N/2 + Q_0) \approx (\varepsilon/2)(2Q_0) = \varepsilon Q_0\), and \(N \approx 2Q_0\); dividing both by \(Q_0\) gives, as Landau and Lifshitz’s §27 does,

\[ 2a' + \left(\varepsilon + \tfrac12hQ_0\right)b = 0, \qquad 2b' - \left(\varepsilon - \tfrac12hQ_0\right)a = 0 . \]

Try \(a, b \propto e^{s\theta}\), so that \(a' = sa\) and \(b' = sb\). The first equation gives \(b = -2sa/(\varepsilon + \tfrac12hQ_0)\); putting this in the second and cancelling \(a\), the two equations have a non-zero solution only if

\[ s^2 = \frac14\left[\left(\tfrac12hQ_0\right)^2 - \varepsilon^2\right]. \]

When \(|\varepsilon| < \tfrac12hQ_0\), \(s\) is real and one solution grows as \(e^{s\theta}\): instability, called parametric resonance. In terms of the tune, the unstable band is

\[ \left|Q_0 - \frac{N}{2}\right| < \frac{hQ_0}{4} = \frac{G}{4Q_0}, \]

with the fastest growth at the centre, \(s_{\max} = hQ_0/4\) per radian. This band is a stop band: tunes inside it are forbidden. Outside it the motion is stable but its amplitude is modulated, more strongly the nearer the band.

At work. For \(N = 3\) and \(h = 0.3\) the formula predicts instability for \(Q_0\) from 1.388 to 1.613 and growth \(s_{\max} = 0.1125\) per radian. Scanning the exact one-period trace of the Floquet section: unstable from 1.397 to 1.624. Most of the upward shift is because the half-width \(hQ_0/4\) grows with \(Q_0\): solving \(|Q_0 - 1.5| < 0.075\,Q_0\) instead of putting \(Q_0 = 1.5\) gives 1.395 to 1.622. The exact growth is 0.112 per radian at the centre, a factor of 2.0 per turn. For \(h = 0.05\) the predicted half-width 0.01875 matches the exact 0.01875. In a three-sector cyclotron the radial focusing contains the third harmonic at full strength, so this is the \(2Q_r = 3\) stop band of section 7. Since \(Q_r \approx \gamma\) there, the band is reached at \(\gamma \approx 1.5\) minus its half-width, and once inside it the beam doubles its radial amplitude every turn or so. It cannot be crossed.

Exercise 3. A particle sits at a half-integer tune and passes once per turn through a thin quadrupole-like error that kicks its slope by \(-z/f\). Explain without equations why the kicks add up, and why at a tune just off the half-integer they do not.

Answer

At a half-integer tune the particle is on the opposite side of the axis on each passage, with its slope reversed. The kick, proportional to \(z\), is reversed too, so relative to the motion it pushes the same way every turn and the amplitude grows. Off the half-integer the phase of each passage drifts, and the kicks alternate in effect and cancel over many turns (Lee, chapter 2, section III.4, draws exactly this).

6. Spiral sectors: alternating gradients in a cyclotron

The fix for weak flutter came from the Midwestern Universities Research Association (MURA). Donald Kerst proposed sectors that spiral outward, a pattern published in 1956 by Symon, Kerst, Jones, Laslett and Terwilliger (chapter 27). The hill edges then lean at the spiral angle \(\xi\) to the radial direction, so they cross the orbit obliquely, \(\xi\) away from its normal. A particle crosses one edge leaning into it and the next leaning away, and a tilted edge is a thin lens whose sign depends on the tilt. The two edges of each hill become alternately focusing and defocusing vertically: alternating-gradient focusing (chapter 16), focusing on balance.

To leading order. Let \(B = \bar{B}[1 + A\cos\psi]\) with \(\psi = N_s(\theta - \tan\xi\,\ln(r/R))\), a logarithmic spiral meeting every radius at \(\xi\) (Figure 12.6). Along a hill edge \(\psi\) is constant, so \(d\theta = \tan\xi\,dr/r\), that is \(r\,d\theta/dr = \tan\xi\): a step \(dr\) outward goes with a sideways step \(r\,d\theta\), and the ratio of the two is the tangent of the angle between the edge and the radius, the same at every radius. Now \(B\) depends on \(r\) through \(\psi\) too: \(\partial\psi/\partial r = -N_s\tan\xi/r\), so \(\partial B/\partial r = -\bar{B}A\sin\psi\,\partial\psi/\partial r\) gains \(\bar{B}AN_s\tan\xi\,\sin\psi/r\), which, through the first term \((r/\bar{B})(\partial B/\partial r)\,z\) of section 5’s equation, adds \(AN_s\tan\xi\,\sin\psi\,z\) to \(z''\): an alternating gradient of amplitude \(G = AN_s\tan\xi\). Two terms survive averaging.

Figure 12.6. Four spiral hills seen from above, each edge a logarithmic spiral \(\theta = \theta_0 + \tan\xi\ln(r/R)\) with \(\xi = 45^\circ\). Where an edge crosses the orbit (red), its tangent (blue) makes the angle \(\xi\) with the radius, which is the orbit’s normal; the angle is the same at every radius, which is what makes the spiral logarithmic. A particle meets every hill edge obliquely, entering through one edge and leaving through the other, and the tilted-edge lenses of the two edges have opposite signs.

  • The alternation. For \(z'' = -[K_0 + G\cos N_s\theta]z\) with fast oscillation, write \(z = Z + \zeta\) with \(Z\) slow. Then \(\zeta'' \approx -G\cos(N_s\theta)Z\), so \(\zeta = (G/N_s^2)\cos(N_s\theta)Z\), and the average of \(-G\cos(N_s\theta)\,\zeta\) is \(-G^2Z/(2N_s^2)\). This is Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §30, motion in a rapidly oscillating field. It adds \(A^2\tan^2\xi/2 = F\tan^2\xi\) to \(Q_z^2\).
  • The scallop meeting the spiral. On the scalloped orbit \(\ln(r/R) \approx x\), so \(\sin\psi \approx \sin N_s\theta - N_s\tan\xi\,x\cos N_s\theta\). With the scallop \(x\) above, this part averages to add \([N_s^2/(N_s^2-1)]F\tan^2\xi\).

For large \(N_s\) these sum to \(2F\tan^2\xi\). The standard result, quoted from Kleeven and Zaremba, is

\[ Q_z^2 \approx n + \frac{N_s^2}{N_s^2-1}\,F\,(1 + 2\tan^2\xi), \qquad Q_r^2 \approx 1 - n + \frac{3N_s^2}{(N_s^2-1)(N_s^2-4)}\,F\,(1 + \tan^2\xi). \]

The \(Q_r\) formula is quoted without derivation. Hagedoorn and Verster (1962) gave a more exact theory in terms of the field’s Fourier harmonics.

Pre-requisite: Hamiltonian perturbation theory, resonances, KAM and chaos

Pre-requisite. This section gives you the way to find which of the many small imperfections of a ring matter: action-angle variables for an oscillation, a disturbance sorted into harmonics of the angle and of the azimuth, the resonance condition \(m_rQ_r + m_zQ_z = p\) and its order, the integer resonance and what crossing it costs, the coupling resonances and why a difference resonance only exchanges amplitude while a sum resonance is unstable, stop bands, and what the KAM theorem and chaos say about whether motion near resonances stays bounded. Section 7 uses it for the resonances an isochronous cyclotron meets: \(Q_r = 1\) driven by a first-harmonic error, the coupling resonance \(Q_r = 2Q_z\), and the structural resonance \(Q_r = N_s/2\). Taught from Tong, Classical Mechanics, §10.5 (action-angle variables, integrable systems and invariant tori); Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §29 (resonance in nonlinear oscillations), §50 (action-angle variables) and §52 (motion with several frequencies); and S. Y. Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, sections III.1 and III.4 (integer and half-integer stopbands), VI (linear coupling) and VII (nonlinear resonances and the KAM theorem).

The idea. A particle in a ring meets the same field every turn, imperfections included. A small field error is therefore a push repeated once per turn. If the particle’s oscillation is at a different point of its cycle at each push, the pushes point every which way relative to the motion and average out: the amplitude wobbles a little and returns. If the oscillation comes back to the same point of its cycle at each push, the pushes add, turn after turn. That is a resonance, and the whole art is to find which combinations of tune and field harmonic make the pushes add. A swing pushed once per swing at the same moment goes higher and higher; pushed once every 1.37 swings, it hardly moves.

Action and angle. Take the radial motion in the smooth approximation, with the azimuth \(\theta\) as the time variable: \(x'' + Q^2x = 0\), with \(x\) the displacement and \(x' = dx/d\theta\). Its solution is \(x = a\cos\psi\), \(x' = -aQ\sin\psi\), with the phase \(\psi = Q\theta + \psi_0\). Instead of \((x, x')\), describe the state by the phase \(\psi\), called the angle, and

\[ J = \frac{Q\,a^2}{2}, \]

called the action. The orbit in the \((x, x')\) plane is an ellipse of semi-axes \(a\) and \(aQ\), whose area is \(\pi a^2Q = 2\pi J\) (chapter 7’s ellipse). The energy-like Hamiltonian \(H_0 = \tfrac12(x'^2 + Q^2x^2)\) becomes simply \(H_0 = QJ\). Tong (§10.5.1) shows that \((\psi, J)\) are a legitimate pair of canonical coordinates: a position-like and a momentum-like variable in which Hamilton’s equations keep their usual form, with \(\psi\) in the place of position, \(J\) in the place of momentum and \(\theta\) in the place of time:

\[ \frac{d\psi}{d\theta} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial J} = Q, \qquad \frac{dJ}{d\theta} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial\psi} = 0 . \]

The angle turns steadily at the tune; the action is constant. It is also the quantity that stays constant when \(Q\) changes slowly, the adiabatic invariant, so an amplitude scales as \(a \propto Q^{-1/2}\) when the tune drifts.

A small disturbance, sorted into harmonics. Add a small term \(\Delta H(x, z, \theta)\) to the Hamiltonian, the effect of a field error or of a nonlinear part of the field. It repeats every turn, so it is periodic in \(\theta\); and, written with \(x = \sqrt{2J_r/Q_r}\cos\psi_r\) and \(z = \sqrt{2J_z/Q_z}\cos\psi_z\), it is periodic in the angles too. So it can be expanded in a double Fourier series:

\[ \Delta H = \sum h_{m_r,m_z,p}(J_r, J_z)\cos\left(m_r\psi_r + m_z\psi_z - p\theta + \chi\right), \]

over whole numbers \(m_r\), \(m_z\) and \(p\). Hamilton’s equation for the radial action is

\[ \frac{dJ_r}{d\theta} = -\frac{\partial\Delta H}{\partial\psi_r} = \sum m_r\,h\,\sin\left(m_r\psi_r + m_z\psi_z - p\theta + \chi\right). \]

Along the unperturbed motion the argument of each sine is \((m_rQ_r + m_zQ_z - p)\theta\) plus a constant. If the bracket is not near zero, the sine oscillates and the change of \(J_r\) integrates to something of size \(h/(m_rQ_r + m_zQ_z - p)\) that comes and goes: harmless. If the bracket is zero, the sine is constant and \(J_r\) changes steadily: that term drives a resonance. The condition is

\[ m_rQ_r + m_zQ_z = p , \]

and \(|m_r| + |m_z|\) is the order of the resonance. Everything else in \(\Delta H\) averages away to first order. This is the core of Hamiltonian perturbation theory: transform to action-angle variables, expand the disturbance, and keep only the slowly varying terms.

Which disturbance drives which resonance. The angle harmonics \(m\) come from powers of \(x\) and \(z\): \(\cos^k\psi\) contains \(\cos k\psi\), \(\cos(k-2)\psi\), and so on down. The azimuth harmonic \(p\) comes from how the disturbance varies around the ring.

  • \(\Delta H \propto x\): a field error independent of position, a dipole error. It contains \(m_r = 1\) only, so it drives the integer resonances \(Q_r = p\). With \(p = 1\), the first harmonic of the error, it drives \(Q_r = 1\).
  • \(\Delta H \propto x^2\): a gradient error. It contains \(m_r = 2\), the half-integer resonances \(2Q_r = p\), and \(m_r = 0\), a shift of the tune.
  • \(\Delta H \propto x^3\): a field varying as \(x^2\). It drives third-order resonances \(3Q_r = p\), and \(Q_r = p\) again.
  • \(\Delta H \propto xz^2\): a radial displacement changes the vertical focusing, as it does wherever the field index varies with radius. With \(\cos\psi_r\cos^2\psi_z\) it contains \(m_r = \pm1\) with \(m_z = \pm2\): the coupling resonances \(Q_r \pm 2Q_z = p\). With \(p = 0\) this is \(Q_r = 2Q_z\), the Walkinshaw resonance of section 7.

The harmonic \(p\) divides disturbances into two kinds. Errors are random around the ring and contain every \(p\), each weakly. The designed field of a machine with \(N_s\) identical sectors contains only \(p = 0, N_s, 2N_s, \dots\), but at full strength. So resonances with \(p\) a multiple of \(N_s\) are structural: they are there in a perfect machine, cannot be corrected, and must be avoided by choice of tune. The half-integer one, \(2Q_r = N_s\), is the parametric resonance of the section above, with its stop band.

The integer resonance, worked through. A first-harmonic field error of relative size \(b\), with the field \(\bar{B}(1 - b\cos\theta)\) lowest at \(\theta = 0\), gives by section 5’s closed-orbit equation the radial equation, with \(x\) measured relative to the radius,

\[ x'' + Q^2x = b\cos\theta . \]

This is the driven oscillator. Away from resonance its steady answer is a distorted closed orbit, \(x = b\cos\theta/(Q^2 - 1)\), which grows without limit as \(Q \to 1\). At \(Q = 1\) exactly the solution starting from rest is \(x = \tfrac12b\,\theta\sin\theta\): the amplitude grows by \(\pi b\) every turn.

An isochronous cyclotron does not sit on \(Q_r = 1\); it passes through it, since \(Q_r \approx \gamma\) rises from 1 at the centre. Let \(Q = 1 + \delta\) with \(\delta\) small, and write the motion as \(x = \operatorname{Re}[A(\theta)e^{i\theta}]\) with \(A\) a slowly varying complex amplitude: its size is the amplitude of the oscillation and its angle the phase. Substituting, \(x'' = \operatorname{Re}[(A'' + 2iA' - A)e^{i\theta}]\) and \(Q^2x \approx (1 + 2\delta)\operatorname{Re}[Ae^{i\theta}]\); the \(-A\) and \(+A\) cancel, \(A''\) is dropped because \(A\) changes slowly, and \(b\cos\theta = \operatorname{Re}[be^{i\theta}]\). Matching the multipliers of \(e^{i\theta}\) gives \(2i\,dA/d\theta + 2\delta A = b\). With \(A = B\,e^{i\int\delta\,d\theta}\) (\(B\) here a complex amplitude, not a field), \(dA/d\theta = (dB/d\theta + i\delta B)\,e^{i\int\delta\,d\theta}\), the \(\delta\) terms cancel, and this becomes

\[ \frac{dB}{d\theta} = -\frac{ib}{2}\exp\left(-i\int\delta\,d\theta\right). \]

If the tune rises steadily from 1, \(\delta = \kappa\theta/2\pi\), with \(\kappa\) the rise per turn, the exponent is \(-i\kappa\theta^2/4\pi\). The integral \(\int_0^\infty e^{-iu\theta^2}d\theta\) has modulus \(\tfrac12\sqrt{\pi/u}\) (a Fresnel integral). It is the Gaussian integral \(\int_0^\infty e^{-a\theta^2}d\theta = \tfrac12\sqrt{\pi/a}\) with \(a = iu\), and \(|\sqrt{\pi/(iu)}| = \sqrt{\pi/u}\); the oscillating exponential adds up only where its phase \(u\theta^2\) is still less than about a radian, which is the stretch near the resonance. Here \(u = \kappa/4\pi\), so the integral’s modulus is \(\pi/\sqrt\kappa\), and multiplying by \(b/2\), after the crossing

\[ |A| = \frac{\pi b}{2\sqrt{\kappa}} . \]

A slow crossing does more harm, as \(1/\sqrt\kappa\): the particle spends about \(1/\sqrt\kappa\) turns within reach of the resonance, gaining \(\pi b\) per turn.

At work. In a compact cyclotron with \(Q_r \approx \gamma\), \(\kappa = \Delta W_t/m_pc^2\). For protons gaining 100 keV per turn, \(\kappa = 1.07\times10^{-4}\) and \(\sqrt\kappa = 0.0103\). A first-harmonic error of one part in \(10^4\), 1 G in 1 T, leaves \(|A| = \pi\times10^{-4}/0.0206 = 0.0152\) of the radius: at 0.2 m, a 3 mm coherent oscillation, the orbit centre pushed off by 3 mm. Integrating the equation directly gives 0.0133 by the time the tune has reached 1.32, which is the same once the adiabatic shrinking \(Q^{-1/2} = 0.87\) is applied. Hence the tight tolerances on the first harmonic near the centre, and the trim coils that cancel it.

Coupling: exchange or growth. Keep only the resonant part of an \(xz^2\) term near \(Q_r = 2Q_z\):

\[ H = Q_rJ_r + Q_zJ_z + c\sqrt{J_r}\,J_z\cos(\psi_r - 2\psi_z), \]

with \(c\) a small constant. Hamilton’s equations give

\[ \frac{dJ_r}{d\theta} = c\sqrt{J_r}J_z\sin(\psi_r - 2\psi_z), \qquad \frac{dJ_z}{d\theta} = -2c\sqrt{J_r}J_z\sin(\psi_r - 2\psi_z), \]

so \(dJ_r/d\theta = -\tfrac12 dJ_z/d\theta\) and \(J_r + \tfrac12J_z\) is constant. Both actions are positive, so neither can grow beyond what this constant allows: the resonance only exchanges oscillation between the planes. Starting with a radial oscillation and almost no vertical one, \(J_z\) can reach \(2J_r(0)\). With \(J = Qa^2/2\) and \(Q_r = 2Q_z\), the vertical amplitude can reach \(a_z^2 = 4J_r(0)/Q_z = 4a_r^2\): twice the radial amplitude. A simulation of \(x'' + Q_r^2x = \epsilon z^2\), \(z'' + Q_z^2z = 2\epsilon xz\) with \(\epsilon = 0.003\), \(Q_r = 0.6\), \(Q_z = 0.3\) confirms it: a radial amplitude of 1 hands over completely, and \(z\) reaches 2.000. Detune to \(Q_r = 0.62\) and \(z\) barely moves. In a cyclotron, where the vertical aperture is the narrow pole gap, a few millimetres of radial oscillation from off-centring can become twice that vertically.

For a sum resonance, \(Q_r + 2Q_z = p\), the same steps give \(J_r - \tfrac12J_z\) constant. Now both actions can grow together without limit: a sum resonance is unstable, a difference resonance is not (Lee, chapter 2, section VI).

Stop bands. Near a resonance driven linearly in the amplitude, the integer and half-integer ones, there is a band of tunes where the response is unacceptable: for the integer resonance, where the closed-orbit distortion \(b/(Q^2 - 1)\) exceeds a tolerance; for the half-integer, where the motion is actually unstable, of width proportional to the driving harmonic, as found for Mathieu’s equation. This band is the stop band, and Lee calls the harmonic that sets its width the stopband integral. A machine’s working tunes must stay outside every stop band, or cross the narrow ones fast.

Nonlinear detuning, islands, KAM and chaos. In a nonlinear field the tune depends on the amplitude (Landau and Lifshitz, §28 and §29). As a resonance drives the amplitude up, the tune moves off the resonance and the growth stops. The particle is then trapped in an island: written in the resonant phase, the Hamiltonian near an isolated resonance has the form \(\alpha J^2 + h\cos(m\psi - p\theta)\), which is the pendulum again, with its stable and unstable fixed points and a separatrix around the islands. Section 2’s bucket is the same structure for the longitudinal motion.

What if many weak resonances act at once? The motion of an unperturbed oscillator in each plane lies on a torus, labelled by the actions (Tong, §10.5.2). The Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser (KAM) theorem says that for a small enough, smooth disturbance most of these invariant tori survive, slightly deformed: motion that starts on them stays quasi-periodic and bounded for ever. The tori that are destroyed are those near resonances, and they break into chains of islands. When the disturbance is strong enough that neighbouring island chains overlap, the surviving tori between them are gone, and a particle can wander from one resonance to the next: chaos, with no bound on the amplitude except the aperture. Lee reports that experiments on electron models and fixed-field machines bore out the practical rule that follows: motion is stable if the tunes avoid low-order resonances, and designers avoid all with \(|m_r| + |m_z| \le 4\) where they can.

At work: a cyclotron’s tune diagram. Plot \(Q_r\) against \(Q_z\) and draw the lines \(m_rQ_r + m_zQ_z = p\). As the beam gains energy its tunes trace a path, the working path. An isochronous machine starts at \(Q_r \approx 1\), which it must cross quickly, with the first harmonic shimmed small. Spiral lifts \(Q_z\) clear of \(Q_r/2\), where compact machines would otherwise meet \(Q_r = 2Q_z\). And \(Q_r \approx \gamma\) climbs towards the structural line \(2Q_r = N_s\), which ends the machine: \(\gamma = 1.5\) (469 MeV for protons) with three sectors, \(\gamma = 2\) (938 MeV) with four (Figure 12.7).

7. Resonances, turn separation and isochronism

Resonances. Since \(Q_r \approx \gamma\), every isochronous machine crosses \(Q_r = 1\) near the centre. There a first-harmonic field error, a dipole bump once a turn, pushes the orbit centre off. Further out lie the coupling resonance \(Q_r = 2Q_z\) (Walkinshaw), which compact cyclotrons often avoid by adding spiral to lift \(Q_z\), and the structural resonance \(Q_r = N_s/2\). A field with \(N_s\) sectors contains the \(N_s\)th harmonic at full strength, so this resonance needs no errors. \(Q_r = \gamma = N_s/2\) bounds three sectors to \(\gamma = 1.5\) (469 MeV), four to \(\gamma = 2\) (938 MeV). These are upper bounds. Kleeven and Zaremba found that a compact three-sector cyclotron (an IBA study for H\(_2^+\)) enters the \(2Q_r = 3\) stop band at the \(\gamma\) of 185 MeV protons, because \(Q_r\) departs from \(\gamma\) near the resonance, too low for the 230 MeV of proton therapy.

Turn separation. Clean extraction needs a septum, a thin wall or electrode that separates the last turn from the one before it and sends only the last turn out, so the turns must be far apart. From \(p = qBR\), as in section 1, \(dp/p = (1-n)\,dR/R\). From \(p = mc\sqrt{\gamma^2-1}\), \(dp/p = \tfrac12\,d(\gamma^2-1)/(\gamma^2-1) = \gamma\,d\gamma/(\gamma^2-1)\). Equating the two, \((1-n)\,dR/R = \gamma\,d\gamma/(\gamma^2-1)\). Each turn adds \(\Delta W_t\) to the energy \(\gamma mc^2\), so \(d\gamma/dn_t = \Delta W_t/mc^2\), and

\[ \frac{dR}{dn_t} = \frac{\gamma R}{(\gamma^2-1)(1-n)}\,\frac{\Delta W_t}{mc^2}, \qquad \text{isochronous } (1-n=\gamma^2)\text{:} \quad \frac{dR}{dn_t} = \frac{R\,\Delta W_t}{mc^2\,\gamma(\gamma^2-1)} . \]

At low energy \(\gamma \approx 1\) and \(\gamma^2 - 1 = (\gamma-1)(\gamma+1) \approx 2W/mc^2\), so this is \(R\,\Delta W_t/(2W)\); at high energy it falls as \(1/\gamma^3\) (Seidel 2013 gives the derivation). Figure 12.8 shows it across the PSI ring. At PSI, about 186 turns take protons from 72 to 590 MeV (188 in the 2010 paper), so \(\Delta W_t \approx 2.78\) MeV. At \(R = 4.5\) m and \(\gamma = 1.629\), \(dR/dn_t = 4.96\) mm; at injection, 36 mm. PSI widens the separation at extraction about threefold, to about 15 mm, by injecting slightly off centre so that a betatron oscillation of the beam centre peaks at the last turn, and by lowering the field gradient over the last turns, which lowers \(Q_r^2 = 1-n\) in the general formula. In the synchrocyclotron model, with 20 keV per turn at 600 MeV, the step is only 54 µm.

How exact isochronism must be. Let the mean field be too high by a fraction \(\Delta B/B\). A particle of given energy keeps its momentum, so from \(p = qBR\) its orbit shrinks by \((1-n)\,\Delta R/R = -\Delta B/B\), that is \(\Delta R/R = -\Delta B/(\gamma^2B)\) in the isochronous field. Its speed is unchanged, so its revolution frequency \(v/R\) rises by the fraction \(\Delta B/(\gamma^2 B)\): the field error itself only while \(\gamma \approx 1\). The phase slips by \(2\pi h\,\Delta B/(\gamma^2B)\) per turn. To keep the accumulated slip over \(n_t\) turns below 20°, a uniform error must satisfy \(|\Delta B/B| < 20^\circ/(360^\circ h\, n_t\langle 1/\gamma^2\rangle)\), with \(\langle 1/\gamma^2\rangle\) averaged over the turns. For PSI (\(h = 6\), 186 turns, \(\langle 1/\gamma^2\rangle = 0.57\) with the energy rising evenly from 72 to 590 MeV) that is \(9\times10^{-5}\); for TRIUMF (\(h = 5\), about 1500 turns, \(\langle 1/\gamma^2\rangle = 0.64\) up to 520 MeV), \(1.2\times10^{-5}\). Setting \(\gamma = 1\) gives the stricter low-energy bounds, \(5\times10^{-5}\) and \(7\times10^{-6}\). No magnet is built that well, so isochronous cyclotrons carry trim coils on the poles (18 circuits in the PSI ring magnets) and phase probes to set them.

Pre-requisite (continued): Lorentz transformations, time dilation, length contraction

Pre-requisite. This section gives you how electric and magnetic fields look to a moving observer, and in particular why a charge moving through a pure magnetic field finds, in its own rest frame, an electric field \(\gamma\beta cB\). Section 8 uses it for the field that strips the loosely bound electron from an H\(^-\) ion in a cyclotron magnet. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, The Classical Theory of Fields, §24 (Lorentz transformation of the field), Tong, Electromagnetism, §5.3.2 and §5.3.3 (the electromagnetic tensor, boosted fields and the boosted line charge), and Furman, in Chao and Tigner’s Handbook, section 7.1.8 (Lorentz stripping of H\(^-\)).

Chapter 1’s section taught time dilation, proper time and the Lorentz transformation of coordinates, and chapter 8 used it for the field of a moving charge. What is new is the transformation of a field that is magnetic in the laboratory.

The argument from force. An ion of charge \(q\) moves with velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) through a magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\) in the laboratory, with no electric field. It feels the force \(q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\), perpendicular to its motion. Now look from the frame moving with the ion at that instant. There the ion is at rest, and a charge at rest feels only an electric force. The ion is still being pushed sideways, so in its frame there must be an electric field \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}'\).

How large? The sideways push changes the transverse momentum, and a change of transverse momentum is the same in both frames, because a boost along \(\mathbf{v}\) leaves momentum components across \(\mathbf{v}\) unchanged. But the time over which it happens is not the same: the ion’s own clock runs slow by \(\gamma\), so an interval \(\Delta t\) in the laboratory is \(\Delta t' = \Delta t/\gamma\) in the ion’s frame. Force is momentum change divided by time, so the transverse force in the ion’s frame is \(\gamma\) times larger:

\[ q\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}' = \gamma\,q\,\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad |\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}'| = \gamma\beta c\,B \]

for \(\mathbf{B}\) perpendicular to \(\mathbf{v}\), with \(\beta = v/c\). The magnetic field is itself seen as \(\gamma B\) in that frame, but it exerts no force on a charge at rest.

The general rule. For fields \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) in the laboratory and an observer moving with velocity \(\mathbf{v}\), split each field into parts along and across \(\mathbf{v}\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}'_\parallel &= \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_\parallel, & \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}'_\perp &= \gamma\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\right)_\perp, \\ \mathbf{B}'_\parallel &= \mathbf{B}_\parallel, & \mathbf{B}'_\perp &= \gamma\left(\mathbf{B} - \frac{\mathbf{v}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}{c^2}\right)_\perp . \end{aligned} \]

Landau and Lifshitz derive these from the transformation of the field tensor, and Tong does the same with the boosted line charge as a check. The force argument above is the special case \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = 0\). Two consequences are worth holding: the components along the motion never change, and \(\mathcal{E}^2 - c^2B^2\) and \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\mathbf{B}\) are the same in every frame, so a field that is purely magnetic in one frame (\(\mathcal{E} = 0\), \(\mathcal{E}^2 - c^2B^2 < 0\)) can never be made purely electric by changing frames.

At work: H\(^-\) in a cyclotron. The extra electron of H\(^-\) is bound by only 0.754 eV, so a modest electric field can pull it off by tunnelling. Moving through TRIUMF’s magnet at 520 MeV (\(\gamma = 1.554\), \(\beta\gamma = 1.189\)), it meets in its own frame

\[ \mathcal{E}' = \beta\gamma\,c\,B = 1.189\times2.998\times10^8\ \text{m/s}\times0.576\ \text{T} = 205\ \text{MV/m} \]

at the peak field of 0.576 T, and 164 MV/m at the average of 0.46 T. That is far more than any large high-voltage gap of chapter 4 holds, yet here it arises from a modest magnet. Furman quotes a fit to measurements of the ion’s mean life in its own frame, \(\tau = (A/\mathcal{E}')\exp(C/\mathcal{E}')\) with \(A = 2.47\times10^{-14}\) s MV/cm and \(C = 44.94\) MV/cm, and the mean path in the laboratory, \(\beta\gamma c\tau\), longer by time dilation: in the laboratory the ion lives \(\gamma\tau\) on average and travels at \(v = \beta c\), so it goes \(\beta c\times\gamma\tau\). The fit was made for 1.87 to 7.02 MV/cm, so below 187 MV/m it is an extrapolation. At 205 MV/m (2.05 MV/cm) \(\tau = 3.9\times10^{-5}\) s and the mean path is 14 km; at 164 MV/m it is 4300 km; at the 713 MV/m that 2 T would give, \(\tau = 1.9\times10^{-12}\) s and the path is 0.7 mm. A 25 per cent rise in field shortens the path three hundred-fold: the steep exponential of tunnelling. This is why TRIUMF has a large, low-field magnet: 14 km is still only about 280 turns of 50 m, so near the outer edge, where the field peaks, a fraction of a per cent of the ions can be lost per turn spent in that field (Figure 12.9).

8. Getting the beam out: deflectors and stripping

Electrostatic deflection. A thin septum electrode between the last two turns gives the outermost turn an outward kick \(\theta = q\mathcal{E}L/(pv)\), and a septum magnet finishes the job. The kick follows from chapter 1: a field \(\mathcal{E}\) across the path, acting for the time \(L/v\) the particle takes to cross the length \(L\), gives it a sideways momentum \(q\mathcal{E}L/v\), and the angle is that divided by \(p\). PSI’s channel holds 140 kV across 16 mm, \(\mathcal{E} = 8.75\) MV/m, over 920 mm. At 590 MeV \(pv/q = 952\) MV, so \(\theta = 8.5\) mrad; the paper quotes 8.2 mrad.

Stripping. Accelerate a negative ion instead. At the chosen radius a thin foil strips its electrons, the H\(^-\) becomes a proton, its charge changes sign, and in the same field it curves the other way, out of the machine. Moving the foil changes the energy, several foils give several beams, and nothing sits between the turns.

The cost is fragility. The second electron of H\(^-\) is bound by only 0.754 eV (Lykke and co-authors, 1991). In its rest frame the ion sees the magnetic field as an electric field \(\mathcal{E}' = \gamma\beta cB\), which pulls that electron off at a rate rising steeply with \(\mathcal{E}'\). For 520 MeV H\(^-\) (\(\gamma = 1.554\), \(\beta\gamma = 1.189\)) in 0.576 T, the peak at TRIUMF’s outer edge, \(\mathcal{E}' = 205\) MV/m; in 2 T it would be 713 MV/m. TRIUMF kept its field low, 0.30 T at the centre and an average of about 0.46 T outside (Seidel), so its 520 MeV orbit has a radius of 8 m (\(B\rho = 3.725\) T m).

The K-value. Multi-ion cyclotrons are rated by \(K\), with \(W/A = K(Q_i/A)^2\) for an ion of charge state \(Q_i\) and mass number \(A\) (non-relativistic; Seidel 2013), and \(K = (eBR)^2/(2m_u)\), with \(m_u\) the atomic mass unit, as in chapter 10. A K500 has \(BR = 3.22\) T m. At a radius of about 0.66 m, half the 52-inch figure listed for the Michigan State K500, the mean field would be about 4.9 T: superconducting coils are needed.

How it was researched and built

Pre-requisite (continued): Transmission lines, matching, S-parameters

Pre-requisite. This section gives you the resonance of a transmission line shorted at one end and loaded by a capacitor at the other, the circuit of a synchrocyclotron’s dee: its resonance condition, how the frequency moves as the capacitor changes, why a line needs a far larger swing of capacitance than a lumped circuit for the same swing of frequency, and the currents that flow. The next section uses it for the frequency modulators of the first synchrocyclotrons and for CERN’s rotating capacitor of 550 to 5000 pF. Taught from Livingston and Blewett, sections 11-7 and 11-8 (the variable-frequency oscillator, quarter-wave and three-quarter-wave dee circuits, and the variable capacitor), with the line theory of chapters 8 and 9.

Chapters 8 and 9 (transmission lines) taught a line’s inductance and capacitance per metre \(L'\) and \(C'\), its wave speed \(1/\sqrt{L'C'}\) (here \(c\), for a line in vacuum), its characteristic impedance \(Z_0 = \sqrt{L'/C'}\), and that a quarter-wave line shorted at one end behaves like a parallel resonator. A dee is such a line: the dee and its stem form the inner conductor, the grounded liner and tank the outer, and the stem is shorted to ground at its far end. What is new is a capacitor at the open end, and the effect of changing it.

The shorted line seen from its open end. Measure \(x\) from the short, where the voltage is zero. A standing wave satisfying that is

\[ V(x) = V_s\sin kx, \qquad I(x) = \frac{V_s}{jZ_0}\cos kx, \qquad k = \frac{\omega}{c}, \]

with the current from the line equations of chapter 8 (the current is largest at the short, where the voltage vanishes). Here \(j\) is the imaginary unit, the \(i\) of F5, as circuit texts and chapter 4’s phasors write it; the factor \(1/j\) says the current is a quarter of a cycle out of step with the voltage, and \(V_s\) (V) is the amplitude of the standing wave. At the open end, \(x = l\), the line presents the impedance

\[ Z_{in} = \frac{V(l)}{I(l)} = jZ_0\tan kl . \]

For \(kl < \pi/2\), a line shorter than a quarter wavelength, this is positive and imaginary: an inductance, \(L_{eff} = Z_0\tan(kl)/\omega\). For a short line, \(\tan kl \approx kl\) and \(L_{eff} \approx Z_0l/c = L'l\), just the inductance of the line’s length, as it should be.

Loading by a capacitor. Put a capacitance \(C\) between the open end and ground (the dee’s capacitance to the liner, plus a variable capacitor). The circuit resonates where the line’s inductive reactance equals the capacitor’s reactance:

\[ Z_0\tan\frac{\omega l}{c} = \frac{1}{\omega C}. \]

This cannot be solved in closed form, but its behaviour is plain. With no capacitance the resonance is at \(kl = \pi/2\), the quarter-wave frequency \(f_{\lambda/4} = c/4l\). Adding capacitance lowers the frequency. For large \(C\), \(kl\) is small, the line acts as a lumped inductance \(L'l\), and \(f \to 1/(2\pi\sqrt{L'lC})\), falling as \(C^{-1/2}\). In between, near the quarter-wave end, the frequency is stubborn: there \(\tan kl\) changes fast with frequency, so a large change of \(C\) moves \(f\) only a little. Livingston and Blewett add that the capacitor works best at the open end, where the voltage is highest, so that a given capacitance has the most effect.

At work: a model of CERN’s dee. The chapter’s figures are a frequency falling from 30 MHz to about 17 MHz as the rotating capacitor swings from 550 to 5000 pF. A lumped circuit with fixed inductance would need only \((30/17)^2 = 3.1\) times the capacitance; CERN’s swing was 9.1 times. Suppose, as a model and not CERN’s actual circuit, that the whole capacitance hangs at the end of one uniform line, and ask what line fits both points. Solving the resonance condition at both gives \(Z_0 = 1.9\ \Omega\) and \(l = 2.19\) m, a quarter-wave frequency of 34.2 MHz. A dee a couple of metres long and wide, a few centimetres from its liner, is just such a low-impedance line. The model then gives:

\(C\) (pF) 100 550 1000 2000 5000 10,000
\(f\) (MHz) 33.4 30.0 27.4 23.2 17.0 12.7

At 30 MHz the line is 79° long electrically (\(kl = 1.38\) rad), close to a quarter wave: multiplying the capacitance by 5.5, from 100 to 550 pF, lowers the frequency by only 10 per cent. From 550 to 5000 pF, a factor of 9.1, it falls by 43 per cent, where a lumped circuit would fall by 67 per cent. At 17 MHz the line is 45° long and behaves more like a lumped inductance.

The currents. At resonance the current into the capacitor is \(\omega CV\). For 20 kV on the dee this is \(2\pi\times30\ \text{MHz}\times550\ \text{pF}\times20\ \text{kV} = 2.1\) kA at the start of the sweep and \(2\pi\times17\ \text{MHz}\times5000\ \text{pF}\times20\ \text{kV} = 10.7\) kA at the end, and the stem carries more, \(V/(Z_0\sin kl)\). Radio-frequency currents of kiloamperes through moving blades are why CERN’s capacitor dissipated about 50 kW and needed water cooling, and why its bearings had to be kept out of the current’s path.

Exercise 1. A lumped LC circuit runs at 30 MHz with 550 pF. What capacitance takes it to 17 MHz? And for Berkeley’s proton-deuteron system, which swept 23.2 to 9.5 MHz?

Answer

\(C \propto 1/f^2\): \(550\times(30/17)^2 = 1713\) pF, a ratio of 3.1. For Berkeley, a ratio \((23.2/9.5)^2 = 6.0\) for a lumped circuit; a line near a quarter wave at the top frequency needs more. Livingston and Blewett describe how Berkeley added a stub to make a three-quarter-wave circuit that slid towards a quarter-wave resonance as the frequency fell.

Exercise 2. Show that for \(kl \ll 1\) the resonance condition reduces to the lumped formula \(\omega^2 = 1/(L'lC)\).

Answer

\(\tan kl \approx kl = \omega l/c\), so \(Z_0\omega l/c = 1/(\omega C)\) and \(\omega^2 = c/(Z_0lC)\). Since \(Z_0/c = \sqrt{L'/C'}\sqrt{L'C'} = L'\), this is \(\omega^2 = 1/(L'lC)\).

\(V(x)/V(l) = \sin kx/\sin kl\). At 30 MHz, \(kl = 1.377\) rad: \(\sin0.689/\sin1.377 = 0.636/0.981 = 0.65\). At 17 MHz, \(kl = 0.780\): \(\sin0.390/\sin0.780 = 0.380/0.703 = 0.54\). The first synchrocyclotrons, 1946 to 1954

McMillan returned to the Radiation Laboratory at Berkeley in September 1945. The principle was tested first on the old 37-inch cyclotron, converted to frequency modulation (“Frequency modulated cyclotron”, Richardson, MacKenzie, Lofgren and Wright, received 3 June 1946). It worked, and the large magnet was converted the same way. That was Lawrence’s 184-inch, named for its pole diameter of 4.67 m. Its construction had stalled during the war while the laboratory worked on the electromagnetic separation of uranium, and it was completed as a synchrocyclotron in November 1946, with Robert Thornton leading the conversion. Brobeck, Lawrence, MacKenzie, McMillan, Serber, Sewell, Simpson and Thornton reported its first performance in a paper received on 26 February 1947. It gave deuterons of about 190 MeV and alphas of about 380 MeV (190 and 380 MeV in Livingston and Blewett, section 11-1; Wikipedia gives 195 and 390 MeV). Both are about 95 MeV per nucleon with nearly the same charge-to-mass ratio, hence the same \(\gamma\) (1.10), the same rigidity (2.88 to 2.89 T m) and the same frequency programme. Later it also gave protons of about 340 to 350 MeV (Lawrence’s Nobel lecture of 1951 gives 340 MeV, Craddock and Livingston and Blewett’s Table 11-1 350 MeV).

Others followed quickly (proton energies and first-operation dates from Craddock’s 2010 review; Table 11-1 of Livingston and Blewett agrees, except that it gives Columbia 400 MeV; the Wikipedia article on the synchrocyclotron matches except where noted): Rochester (240 MeV, 1948), Columbia’s Nevis machine (380 MeV, 1950), Chicago (450 MeV, 1951), Carnegie Institute of Technology (450 MeV, 1952; Wikipedia gives 435 MeV, 1950) and Liverpool (400 MeV, 1954; in operation by April 1954). At Dubna the synchrocyclotron of the future Institute of Nuclear Problems was in use by 1950, when biophysical research on its beams began, and later ran at 680 MeV. It was rebuilt as a phasotron in the early 1980s and shut down in 2023.

The frequency modulator. The hard part is changing a resonant frequency by tens of per cent, hundreds of times a second, at kilovolts. The dee is a resonant line whose frequency is set by a variable capacitor at its end. Many large machines used tuning forks, vibrating plates, at about 50 Hz (Blosser 1969). The alternative, the rotating capacitor, has rows of rotor blades passing rows of stator blades, giving a capacitance varying at the rotation frequency times the number of blades. Rotating capacitors had run at Orsay and Dubna. But they need bearings in vacuum, under radio-frequency current, carrying a heavy fast rotor; Blosser noted that their development leaned on steam-turbine technology, and that rates near 500 Hz looked possible.

CERN’s synchrocyclotron, 1951 to 1990

At a UNESCO meeting in Paris in December 1951 a synchrocyclotron was proposed for the planned European laboratory, to give physics to do until the 28 GeV proton synchrotron was ready. Cornelis Bakker led the design group from 1952. Design began in 1953, before CERN existed, and construction at Meyrin in 1954. The machine reached 600 MeV in August 1957, and experiments began in April 1958. One early result was the long-sought decay of a pion into an electron and a neutrino. From 1967 it fed ISOLDE, the radioactive-beam facility.

By the late 1960s synchrocyclotron laboratories had to improve or close. Blosser’s 1969 review set out the goals of the programmes at CERN, Columbia, Harwell and Dubna: 10 µA or more, at least 50 per cent extraction efficiency, and a tenfold gain in beam definition. The means were a closed ion source, a dee voltage raised from the typical 10 kV to about 30 kV so that ions clear the source on the first turn, a faster modulator, and better extraction. He remarked that the Dubna magnet held twice as much iron as Brookhaven’s AGS synchrotron.

CERN chose one rotating capacitor, built by AEG-Telefunken to a CERN concept: 16 rows of teeth, a rotor-to-stator gap of about 4 mm down to 1 mm, a capacitance of 550 to 5000 pF, and the frequency curve fixed by the machined shape of the stator teeth. It was water-cooled, dissipated about 50 kW, and had its own hydrocarbon-free vacuum with a turbomolecular pump (a pump whose fast-spinning bladed rotor knocks gas molecules towards the exhaust, with no oil on the vacuum side; chapter 3), since hydrocarbons lowered the voltage the gaps could hold. Its bearings sat at equal radio-frequency potential in a bridge circuit, to keep current out of them. At 466 Hz with 16 teeth the rotor turns 29 times a second, about 1750 revolutions a minute, close to the 1800 rpm of the bearing tests. A 230 kW tetrode generator drove it (a tetrode is a vacuum-tube amplifier, the triode with a fourth electrode, a screen grid, that lets it work at high frequency). The machine stopped in June 1973, restarted as SC2 in January 1975, and closed in 1990 when ISOLDE moved to the PS Booster.

The largest: Gatchina

The SC-1000 at the Petersburg Nuclear Physics Institute, Gatchina, accelerates protons to 1000 MeV. The institute says it was put into operation in the 1970s and has been upgraded since; Craddock’s 2010 table dates its first operation to 1967. Its magnet weighs 7800 t, with 1.9 T at the centre, and its extracted beam is 1 µA, up to \(6\times10^{12}\) protons a second (1 µA is \(6.24\times10^{12}\) protons a second). A 1 GeV proton has \(B\rho = 5.66\) T m, a 3 m radius even at 1.9 T.

Sector focusing is built

Thomas’s idea waited for orbit computation and for a reason to want continuous beams. Classified prototypes came first. The first isochronous cyclotron in the open was built at Delft by F. Heyn and K. T. Khoe; Seidel dates it to 1958, other sources to 1956 to 1958. Livingston and Blewett (section 15-11) count instead Los Alamos’s 42-inch cyclotron, rebuilt with radial sectors in 1954, as the first practical one, and name Los Alamos and Delft (12 MeV deuterons) as the only two in operation in February 1959. Spiral sectors made the focusing strong enough for higher energies. As Blosser wrote in 1969, the neglected Lawrence cyclotron blossomed again from the late 1950s through sector focusing.

The separated-sector ring at PSI

In 1963, at a conference on sector-focused cyclotrons in Geneva, Hans Willax proposed a 500 MeV isochronous cyclotron built as a ring of separate magnets. No iron in the valleys means large flutter, and the gaps between magnets leave room for big cavities and for injection and extraction. The Swiss Institute for Nuclear Research (SIN), founded in 1968, built it at Villigen. The ring went into service in 1974 at 100 µA. A second injector cyclotron, Injector 2 (72 MeV), followed in 1984, and in 1988 SIN became part of the Paul Scherrer Institute (PSI).

From Seidel’s papers of 2010 and 2013: 8 sector magnets of 250 to 280 t, 2 T peak, 18 correction-coil circuits; 4 resonators at 50.6 MHz, copper since 2005 to 2008, \(Q_0\) of 4 to \(4.8\times10^4\), gap voltage typically 830 kV (design about 1.2 MV), each able to give 400 kW to the beam; a flat-top resonator at 150 MHz, the third harmonic, run decelerating at 1/9 of the main amplitude to flatten the voltage across the bunch (subtracting a little of the third harmonic, whose curvature at the crest is nine times larger, cancels the curvature of the main wave there); vacuum chambers 2.5 m wide at \(10^{-6}\) mbar; transmission 0.9998. The current is 2.2 mA routinely, up to 2.4 mA, 1.3 to 1.4 MW: the most powerful cyclotron beam.

The current grew about twentyfold, mainly by cutting the number of turns from more than 300 to about 186 with higher gap voltages. Joho showed in 1981 that losses from longitudinal space charge grow as the cube of the number of turns (chapter 22), so at constant loss the current can grow as \(n_t^{-3}\): 300 to 186 turns allows \((300/186)^3 = 4.2\). Seidel reports that the current did scale this way. Counting the radio-frequency chain alone, mains to beam is about 32 per cent efficient.

TRIUMF, H\(^-\) and stripping

TRIUMF was founded in 1968 by Simon Fraser University, the University of British Columbia and the University of Victoria, in Vancouver. The cyclotron’s power supply was first switched on on 10 December 1972, and the first high-energy beam came on 15 December 1974 (TRIUMF’s account). H\(^-\) ions are accelerated at 23 MHz in about 1500 turns lasting 326 µs. The six-sector magnet weighs about 4000 t, with coils 17.7 m across carrying 18,500 A; the field is about 0.30 T at the centre and 0.576 T at the outer edge. The tank is about 18 m across, half a metre high, at about \(10^{-8}\) Torr. Graphite foils about 11 µm thick extract protons from 70 to 520 MeV, up to four beams at once.

The numbers check each other. The H\(^-\) cyclotron frequency in 0.30 T is 4.569 MHz; the fifth harmonic is 22.8 MHz. 1500 turns at 4.569 MHz take 328 µs. The isochronous radius at 520 MeV is 7.99 m, against Seidel’s 3.8 to 7.9 m extraction radii. Seidel also gives an overall transmission of 0.70 and up to about 110 kW, about 210 µA.

Pre-requisite: Superconductivity basics

Pre-requisite. This section gives you what a superconductor is and why it makes strong magnets possible: zero resistance and the persistent current, the three limits of temperature, field and current density that together form the critical surface, why ordinary superconductors fail in small fields while the type II alloys niobium-titanium and niobium-tin carry thousands of amperes per square millimetre at several tesla, and what it costs to keep them cold. The next section uses it for superconducting cyclotrons and synchrocyclotrons, from Michigan State’s K500 to the 9 T niobium-tin magnet of a proton-therapy machine. Taught from Wilson, Superconducting Magnets, chapter 1 (the critical surface and the case for superconducting magnets) and section 12.1 (critical temperature, field and current density; type II materials and fluxoids); Tong, Electromagnetism, §6.5.2 and §6.5.3 (the Meissner effect and vortices); and Padamsee, RF Superconductivity, section 3.1 (Cooper pairs and the two-fluid picture).

The idea. The field of a magnet is set by its ampere-turns, and the ampere-turns a copper coil can carry are set by the heat it makes. Below about 2 T, iron does most of the work and the coil only has to drive flux across the gap. Above 2 T iron saturates (chapter 8), and the extra field must come from current alone. A superconductor carries current with no resistance at all, so the heat limit disappears, and current densities a hundred times those of copper become possible. The price is a temperature of a few kelvin.

Zero resistance. Kamerlingh Onnes found in 1911 that mercury loses all its resistance below 4.2 K, three years after he first liquefied helium. The resistance is not merely small: a current set going in a closed superconducting ring, a persistent current, shows no measurable decay. The reason is quantum mechanical. Below a critical temperature \(T_c\), an attraction between electrons, carried by vibrations of the crystal lattice, binds them into Cooper pairs of opposite momentum and spin. The pairs move together in one quantum state and cannot be scattered one at a time, so they flow without friction. Breaking a pair costs an energy \(2\Delta\), the superconducting gap, which the theory of Bardeen, Cooper and Schrieffer (BCS) relates to the critical temperature by

\[ 2\Delta(0) \approx 3.5\,k_BT_c , \]

with \(k_B\) Boltzmann’s constant. For niobium-titanium, \(T_c = 9.3\) K and \(2\Delta \approx 2.8\) meV: more than a thousand times smaller than the work function of a metal, which is why only very low temperatures preserve it. (At radio frequencies the pairs’ inertia lets a small loss through, which is the subject of superconducting cavities in chapter 13; for the steady current of a magnet coil the resistance is truly zero.)

The critical field, and why pure metals are useless for magnets. A superconductor expels magnetic field from its interior, the Meissner effect: screening currents flow in a surface layer some tens of nanometres deep (the London penetration depth, 36 nm for niobium) and cancel the field inside. Expelling a field \(B\) costs energy \(B^2/2\mu_0\) per unit volume, the energy of the field pushed out. The superconducting state is cheaper than the normal one by a condensation energy, so above the field at which the two are equal,

\[ \frac{B_c^2}{2\mu_0} = G_n - G_s , \]

the metal turns normal (\(G_n\) and \(G_s\) are the free energies per unit volume of the two states, J/m\(^3\): the free energy is the energy that decides which state a material settles into when its temperature is held fixed, the energy less the share tied up in disorder, \(TS\), in the sense of F6’s entropy). The condensation energy is small, and for metals like lead and tin \(B_c\) is about 0.05 to 0.2 T. Wilson’s example: \(B_c(0) = 0.19\) T, a condensation energy of only 14 kJ/m\(^3\), against 10 MJ/m\(^3\) stored in a 5 T field. Such “type I” superconductors cannot carry a magnet’s field.

Type II: letting the field in. In alloys and compounds a second behaviour takes over. Above a lower critical field \(B_{c1}\) the field enters the material, not uniformly but in thin threads called fluxoids or vortices, each carrying exactly one flux quantum,

\[ \Phi_0 = \frac{h}{2e} = 2.07\times10^{-15}\ \text{Wb}, \]

the \(2e\) being the charge of a Cooper pair. Each fluxoid has a normal core about a coherence length across (the coherence length, a few nanometres in alloys, is the shortest distance over which the density of Cooper pairs can change, roughly the size of a pair), surrounded by circulating supercurrent; between them the material stays superconducting. As the field rises the fluxoids crowd together, spaced about \(\sqrt{\Phi_0/B}\) apart (20 nm at 5 T), until at the upper critical field \(B_{c2}\) their cores overlap and superconductivity ends. Because the field is let in rather than pushed out, \(B_{c2}\) can be far above the type I value: the theory of Ginzburg, Landau, Abrikosov and Gor’kov gives \(B_{c2} = \sqrt2\,\kappa B_c\), where \(\kappa\), the ratio of penetration depth to coherence length, is large in dirty alloys. For niobium-titanium Wilson estimates \(\kappa \approx 49\) near \(T_c\), rising by a factor of 1.19 at absolute zero, and \(B_c(0) = 0.22\) T, which predict \(B_{c2}(0) = \sqrt2\times58\times0.22 \approx 18\) T; the measured value is about 15 T, held down by an effect of the electrons’ spins.

Critical current and the critical surface. A current in a type II superconductor in a field pushes on the fluxoids with a force \(\mathbf{J}\times\mathbf{B}\) per unit volume. If they move, they dissipate energy and the material has resistance. They are held in place by pinning at defects, grain boundaries and fine precipitates, so the critical current density \(J_c\) is not a fundamental constant but a product of metallurgy. The three limits are linked: raise any one of temperature, field and current density and the other two allowed values fall. The region where the material superconducts lies under a surface in the space of \((T, B, J)\), the critical surface. For practical conductors at 4.2 K, the boiling point of liquid helium at atmospheric pressure:

\(T_c\) \(B_{c2}\) at 0 K \(B_{c2}\) at 4.2 K notes
Niobium-titanium (NbTi) 9.3 K about 15 T about 11 T ductile alloy, the workhorse
Niobium-tin (Nb\(_3\)Sn) 18.5 K (ideal) about 28 T well above NbTi’s brittle compound, made in place by heat treatment

Critical current densities at a few tesla are of order \(10^9\) A/m\(^2\) (a thousand amperes per square millimetre) in the superconductor itself. A practical wire embeds thin filaments in copper, which carries the current for a moment if a patch goes normal and lets the magnet be protected; with copper and insulation, a coil’s overall current density is of order \(10^8\) A/m\(^2\). Water-cooled copper coils run at about \(10^7\) A/m\(^2\) (Wilson’s figure 1.2).

At work: why a strong field needs a superconducting coil. The field inside a long, thick coil of thickness \(t\) carrying current density \(J\) is about \(B = \mu_0Jt\). For 5 T:

  • copper at \(10^7\) A/m\(^2\): \(t = 5/(4\pi\times10^{-7}\times10^7) = 0.40\) m. For a coil of mean radius 1 m and 1 m long, the copper volume is \(2\pi\times1\times0.4\times1 = 2.5\) m\(^3\), and with copper’s resistivity \(1.7\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\) m the power is \(\rho J^2V = 4.3\) MW, all to be carried off by cooling water;
  • superconductor at \(10^8\) A/m\(^2\): \(t = 4\) cm, and no ohmic power at all.

The superconducting coil still costs power, to remove the heat that leaks in to 4.2 K. Wilson notes that each watt removed at 4.2 K needs 500 to 1000 W at room temperature; the thermodynamic minimum, from Carnot’s efficiency (F6), is \((300 - 4.2)/4.2 = 70\) W. A magnet with a 50 W heat leak thus needs 25 to 50 kW: tens of kilowatts instead of megawatts.

At work: the shrinking cyclotron. At a given rigidity the orbit radius is \(B\rho/B\). A 250 MeV proton (\(B\rho = 2.43\) T m) needs a radius of 1.28 m at 1.9 T and 0.27 m at 9 T. A magnet of solid iron across the orbit grows roughly as the cube of the radius, so the high field divides its size by about 100. This is how a 250 MeV synchrocyclotron fits on a gantry. Its 9 T coil uses niobium-tin because the field at the winding exceeds the field at the beam, and niobium-titanium’s 11 T at 4.2 K leaves too little margin. The coil must also hold the magnetic pressure \(B^2/2\mu_0\): 32 MPa at 9 T, pushing the windings outward, which is why such coils are clamped in strong steel or aluminium structures.

Exercise 1. Estimate the spacing of fluxoids in niobium-titanium at 1 T and at 9 T, and compare it with the London penetration depth of tens of nanometres.

Answer

In a triangular lattice the spacing is \(d = \sqrt{2/\sqrt3}\,\sqrt{\Phi_0/B}\): 49 nm at 1 T and 16 nm at 9 T. At high field the fluxoids are closer than the depth over which each one’s field spreads, so the field inside is nearly uniform; superconductivity survives in the gaps between the normal cores until \(B_{c2}\).

Exercise 2. A compact cyclotron needs 5.7 T. Iron gives at most about 2 T. Using \(B \approx \mu_0Jt\) for the remainder, how thick must a coil be at \(10^8\) A/m\(^2\), and at a copper-like \(10^7\) A/m\(^2\)?

Answer

The coil must provide about 3.7 T: \(t = 3.7/(4\pi\times10^{-7}\times J)\) = 2.9 cm at \(10^8\) A/m\(^2\) and 29 cm at \(10^7\) A/m\(^2\). The copper version would also dissipate megawatts, as in the text.

Exercise 3. By what factor does the radius of a 230 MeV proton orbit shrink between a 1.9 T conventional magnet and IBA’s 5.7 T, and roughly by what factor the iron?

Answer

\(B\rho = 2.32\) T m, so 1.22 m and 0.41 m, a factor of 3. The iron, scaling roughly as the radius cubed, falls by about 27. (The real gain is smaller, since the high-field magnet needs a heavier return yoke for its flux.)

Superconducting cyclotrons

A superconducting coil gives several tesla, and since \(R \propto 1/B\) at given rigidity the magnet shrinks. Henry Blosser’s group at Michigan State University built the first superconducting cyclotron, the K500, with first beam in 1982 (some sources say 1981), then the K1200; a group at Chalk River in Canada pursued the idea by 1973. Joho’s statistics, quoted by Seidel, show superconducting cyclotrons lighter on average by a factor of 15. Blosser’s group also built a compact superconducting cyclotron for neutron therapy, mounted on a gantry at Harper Hospital in Detroit, which treated more than 2000 patients from 1992 to 2012. RIKEN’s superconducting ring cyclotron (2006) has \(K = 2600\) and sector magnets peaking at 3.8 T.

High fields bring one problem. The flutter comes from the pole iron, which saturates: Kleeven and Zaremba put the modulation iron can give at about 2 T. As the mean field rises the flutter falls, and eventually cannot beat \(n = -(\gamma^2-1)\). A synchrocyclotron has no such limit, so the most compact proton-therapy machines are superconducting synchrocyclotrons. Mevion’s has about 9 T from a niobium-tin coil, gives 250 MeV and rides on the gantry around the patient. IBA’s S2C2 has 5.7 T, weighs about 45 t and gives 230 MeV; its frequency sweeps from about 88 to about 63 MHz 1000 times a second, through more than 45,000 turns in about 600 µs. (88 MHz needs 5.77 T at the centre.)

Medical cyclotrons

Most cyclotrons built today are medical. IBA, founded in 1986, had by the mid-2010s sold more than 300 cyclotrons for isotope production and about 40 for proton therapy (Kleeven and Zaremba); isotope machines run below about 100 MeV and therapy machines below about 250 MeV. A 2020 count found close to 1500 medical cyclotrons worldwide. Many isotope cyclotrons accelerate H\(^-\) and extract by stripping, choosing a target by rotating a foil into the beam. The uses are in chapter 28.

How such a machine is designed today

The magnet is modelled in three dimensions with a finite-element code, which divides the iron, coils and air into many small cells and solves the field equations cell by cell (Kleeven and Zaremba use Opera-3d). Its median-plane field goes to a closed-orbit code that finds the scalloped orbits at each energy, their tunes and their revolution times (CYCLOPS is one, after Gordon 1984; IBA writes its own), and the pole edges are shimmed until the revolution time is the same at every radius. The tune diagram is checked against \(Q_r = 1\), \(Q_r = 2Q_z\) and \(Q_r = N_s/2\). The central region is tracked through the gap’s electric field. For high intensity, tracking with space charge and neighbouring turns follows the beam to extraction; PSI used OPAL with about \(10^8\) macroparticles, each a computational particle standing for many real ones with the same charge-to-mass ratio. Shielding and activation are computed with Monte Carlo codes such as MARS and MCNPX, which follow very many particles and their secondaries one at a time, choosing each collision at random with the right probabilities.

The standard choices: compact medical machines with (often) four sectors, deep valleys for large flutter, and a little spiral to stay clear of the Walkinshaw resonance; separated-sector rings for high current; H\(^-\) and foils where the field or energy is low; superconducting synchrocyclotrons where compactness matters more than current.

At the bench

At a synchrocyclotron

The frequency sweep. On an oscilloscope triggered at the start of each modulation cycle, a pickup on the dee shows a radio-frequency envelope falling in frequency through the cycle, at CERN from 30 MHz to about 17 MHz. The sweep is fast at first and slower near the end, then flies back quickly with nothing accelerated. The curve’s shape is fixed in metal, by the cut of the stator blades or the plates of a tuning fork. The operator sets what is electrical: the dee-voltage programme, the timing of the ion-source pulse against the sweep, and, on the CERN design, the final frequency between 16.77 and 17.3 MHz.

The beam current trace. A target current on a fast oscilloscope shows short pulses, one per sweep: 54 a second on CERN’s original machine, several hundred on the rebuilt one. The meter’s average, microamperes, is charge per pulse times repetition rate. Mistime the ion source against the moment the frequency passes the central cyclotron frequency, and the pulses shrink or vanish, because capture happens only then. Set the dee voltage too low for the sweep, and the beam is lost partway out. A radial probe driven outward then shows the current falling at the radius where the bucket closed.

Duty factor. Without a stretcher the extracted beam arrives in bursts of tens of microseconds, present for a fraction of a per cent of the time. With one, the bursts become long spills. An experimenter sees the duty factor as the ratio of average to peak counting rate, and in the rate of accidental coincidences.

How it fails. The modulator is the weak point. In CERN’s bearing tests, vibration stayed low for about 80 per cent of a bearing’s life and then rose fast. Electrodes contaminated by hydrocarbons arc, and once contaminated could rarely be saved. CERN suppressed multipacting, a resonant avalanche in which electrons knocked from one surface by the radio-frequency field strike another in step with the field and knock out more, by biasing the rotor, cone and dee at about 3 kV direct, which throws the electrons out of step. Extraction is weak because the turns are packed to tens of micrometres apart. Synchrocyclotrons extract by regeneration: a deliberate field perturbation drives the \(2Q_r = 2\) resonance until the radial oscillation throws particles into a channel (Kleeven and Zaremba).

At an isochronous cyclotron

Phase probes and trim coils. Pickups at a series of radii see each bunch pass. Comparing its arrival with the radio frequency gives the beam phase against radius. A phase drifting earlier with radius means the field is too high there, later means too low. The main field and the trim-coil currents are adjusted until the curve is flat; at PSI, phase probes in both cyclotrons are used for exactly this. The tolerance is several parts in \(10^5\) at PSI and about one in \(10^5\) at TRIUMF (section 7). A phase far from the crest lowers the energy gain per turn, raises the number of turns, shrinks the turn separation and widens the energy spread.

Turn separation on a radial probe. A probe with a thin wire is driven slowly in radius through the last turns. Its signal against radius shows a row of peaks, one per turn, with valleys between; the extraction electrode sits in the deepest valley, between the last two turns. The peak spacing is the \(dR/dn_t\) of section 7, about 5 mm by the plain formula at PSI and about three times that with off-centre injection. Peaks smeared into a continuum mean overlapping turns, from too large an energy spread or a wrong phase, and higher extraction losses. PSI has about 190 wire monitors, radial probes, harps (grids of parallel wires that read a profile at once) and fluorescence monitors.

Foil extraction. In an H\(^-\) machine the operator drives a stripper foil to the radius of the wanted energy and watches the extracted current and the foil. The foil is heated by the beam and by the stripped electrons, which spiral in the field and pass through it again; foil lifetime is a standing problem (Seidel 2013). Ions stripped by the field or by gas before the foil become neutral atoms and leave in straight lines.

Activation of the extraction region. At PSI the relative losses are about \(10^{-4}\), about 130 W of a 1.3 MW beam. Residual dose rates, in millisieverts per hour (chapter 8; a radiation worker’s yearly limit is 20 mSv), are about 1 mSv/h on average in the ring and up to about 10 mSv/h at hot spots in the extraction beam line. The extraction channel is exchanged using mobile shielding. A run-permit system detects losses within about 100 µs and stops the beam, both when a loss signal rises too high and when it falls well below normal, which means a monitor has failed.

How it fails. PSI sees 20 to 50 short beam trips a day, most from high-voltage breakdowns in the electrostatic elements, whose insulators collect sputtered metal; the extraction channel alone sparks 2 to 5 times a day in good condition. After a trip the current is back to nominal in about 30 s. Radio-frequency trips are much rarer.

Where it stops

The synchrocyclotron stops near 1 GeV for size and current. Its magnet is solid across the whole orbit, so its iron grows roughly as the cube of the radius, and the radius grows with rigidity: 5.66 T m at 1 GeV, 3 m even at 1.9 T. Gatchina’s 7800 t gives 1 µA, because only one batch is accelerated per sweep. To go higher the magnet must shrink to a narrow ring around one orbit of fixed radius, with the field rising in time: the synchrotron of chapter 15, made practical by the strong focusing of chapter 16.

The isochronous cyclotron also stops at about 1 GeV, for reasons that compound (Seidel).

  • Turn separation. \(dR/dn_t \propto 1/[\gamma(\gamma^2-1)]\) falls from 0.371 at 590 MeV to 0.148 at 1 GeV and 0.036 at 2 GeV. Clean single-turn extraction, the cyclotron’s great advantage, is lost.
  • Vertical focusing. The flutter term must beat \(\gamma^2 - 1\): 1.65 at 590 MeV, 3.27 at 1 GeV. For an eight-sector ring with field-free valleys and a filling like PSI’s (\(\alpha = 0.449\), about its mean field over its 2 T peak; \(F = 1.23\)), the leading-order formula already needs a spiral of about 22° for \(Q_z = 0\) at 590 MeV, more at 1 GeV. (Rough: the smooth formula is poor at such flutter.)
  • Resonances. \(Q_r \approx \gamma\) climbs towards \(N_s/2\), through coupling resonances.
  • H\(^-\). The rest-frame field \(\gamma\beta cB\) forces low fields and huge magnets.

The way past keeps the fixed field but focuses so strongly with alternating gradients that the orbits barely move with energy: the fixed-field alternating-gradient accelerators that grew from Kerst’s spiral sectors, in chapter 27. The full longitudinal theory is in chapter 19.

Exercises

1. CERN’s synchrocyclotron starts at 30 MHz on the first harmonic. What is the field at the centre? At 600 MeV in 1.83 T, what is the revolution frequency?

Answer

\(B_0 = 2\pi fm_p/e = 1.968\) T (15.25 MHz per tesla). At 600 MeV \(\gamma = 1.6395\), so \(f = 15.25 \times 1.83/1.6395 = 17.0\) MHz. The factor of 1.76 comes from \(\gamma\) (1.64) and the 7 per cent fall of the field.

2. CERN’s rotating capacitor has 16 rows of teeth and runs at 466 Hz, accelerating for 64 per cent of each period. Find the rotor speed and the acceleration time. If the frequency fell linearly from 30 to 17 MHz, how many turns would a proton make, what gain per turn reaches 600 MeV, and what synchronous phase does that need with 20 kV at each of two gap crossings?

Answer

\(466/16 = 29.1\) revolutions a second, 1750 rpm. Period 2.146 ms, acceleration 1.373 ms. Turns \(= \bar{f}t = 23.5\ \text{MHz} \times 1.373\ \text{ms} \approx 32{,}000\) (32,100 to 32,500 for a final 16.77 to 17.3 MHz), so about 18.6 keV per turn. With \(\hat{V}_t = 40\) kV, \(\sin\phi_s = 0.465\), \(\phi_s = 152^\circ\): the falling side, as a machine above transition needs.

3. CERN’s original machine pulsed 54 times a second, the rebuilt one 466. With the same charge per pulse, by what factor does the current rise? If each extracted burst lasts one synchrotron period at 600 MeV (36.5 kHz in the model), what fraction of the time is beam present?

Answer

\(466/54 = 8.6\). The burst is \(1/36.5\ \text{kHz} = 27.4\) µs, so \(27.4\ \mu\text{s} \times 54 = 0.15\) per cent and \(27.4\ \mu\text{s} \times 466 = 1.3\) per cent.

4. In the model synchrocyclotron at 300 MeV, \(n = 0.0590\) and \(f_0 = 22.08\) MHz. With \(\hat{V}_t = 40\) kV, \(\phi_s = 150^\circ\), \(h = 1\), find \(\eta\), \(Q_s\) and \(\Omega_s/2\pi\). What happens at \(\phi_s = 30^\circ\)?

Answer

\(\gamma = 1.3197\), \(\beta^2 = 0.4258\), \(E = 1238.3\) MeV. \(\alpha_c = 1/(1-n) = 1.0627\), \(\eta = 1.0627 - 1/\gamma^2 = 0.489\). \(Q_s^2 = 0.489 \times 0.040 \times 0.866/(2\pi \times 0.4258 \times 1238.3) = 5.1\times10^{-6}\), \(Q_s = 0.00226\): one oscillation in 442 turns, \(\Omega_s/2\pi = 50\) kHz. At \(30^\circ\), \(\eta\cos\phi_s > 0\) and the phase runs away.

5. For the PSI ring (\(h = 6\), 186 turns) and TRIUMF (\(h = 5\), 1500 turns), what average field error makes the phase slip 20° over the acceleration?

Answer

The revolution frequency is off by \(\Delta B/(\gamma^2B)\) (section 7), so the total slip is \(360^\circ\,h\,n_t\langle1/\gamma^2\rangle\,\Delta B/B\). With the energy rising evenly, \(\langle1/\gamma^2\rangle = 0.570\) at PSI (72 to 590 MeV) and \(0.643\) at TRIUMF (0.3 to 520 MeV, H\(^-\)). PSI: \(20/(360\times6\times186\times0.570) = 8.7\times10^{-5}\). TRIUMF: \(20/(360\times5\times1500\times0.643) = 1.2\times10^{-5}\). Ignoring the \(1/\gamma^2\) gives the stricter \(5.0\times10^{-5}\) and \(7.4\times10^{-6}\). More turns and a higher harmonic make isochronism harder.

6. Derive the radial step per turn in an isochronous cyclotron and evaluate it at PSI: 518 MeV gained in 186 turns, extraction at 590 MeV and 4.5 m, injection at 72 MeV and 2.1 m. What would it be at 1 GeV with the same radius and gain?

Answer

\((1-n)\,dR/R = \gamma\,d\gamma/(\gamma^2-1)\) with \(1-n = \gamma^2\) and \(d\gamma/dn_t = \Delta W_t/mc^2\) gives \(dR/dn_t = R\Delta W_t/[mc^2\gamma(\gamma^2-1)]\). \(\Delta W_t = 2.785\) MeV. At 590 MeV (\(\gamma = 1.6288\)): 4.96 mm. At 72 MeV (\(\gamma = 1.0767\)): 36.3 mm. At 1 GeV (\(\gamma = 2.0658\)): \(4.96 \times 0.148/0.371 = 1.98\) mm.

7. TRIUMF’s field is 0.30 T at the centre and its cavities run at 23 MHz on the fifth harmonic. Check the frequency. At 520 MeV find the isochronous radius and the rest-frame electric field on an H\(^-\) ion in 0.576 T, and in 2 T.

Answer

\(m_{H^-}c^2 = m_pc^2 + 2m_ec^2 - (13.6 + 0.754)\ \text{eV} = 939.29\) MeV (the binding energies are negligible here); \(f = eB/(2\pi m_{H^-}) = 4.569\) MHz, \(\times5 = 22.8\) MHz. At 520 MeV \(\gamma = 1.5536\), \(\beta = 0.7653\), \(B\rho = 3.725\) T m; mean field \(0.30\gamma = 0.466\) T, so \(R = 7.99\) m. \(\mathcal{E}' = \gamma\beta cB = 205\) MV/m in 0.576 T and 713 MV/m in 2 T.

8. Show that a cyclotron field of index \(n\) has \(\eta = \alpha_c - 1/\gamma^2\) with \(\alpha_c = 1/(1-n)\), that a synchrocyclotron is always above transition, and that an isochronous field has \(\eta = 0\). Then derive the radial-sector Thomas term \(Q_z^2 = n + [N_s^2/(N_s^2-1)]F\), and use the spiral formula to estimate the spiral angle an eight-sector ring with field-free valleys and \(\alpha = 0.449\) needs at 590 MeV for \(Q_z = 0.5\).

Answer

\(p = qBr\) gives \(\alpha_c = 1/(1-n)\); \(\omega_0 = qB/(\gamma m)\) gives \(d\omega_0/\omega_0 = -[n/(1-n) + \beta^2]\,dp/p\), so \(\eta = \alpha_c - 1 + \beta^2 = \alpha_c - 1/\gamma^2\). For \(0 < n < 1\), \(\alpha_c > 1 \ge 1/\gamma^2\), so \(\eta > 0\). For \(n = -(\gamma^2-1)\), \(\alpha_c = 1/\gamma^2\) and \(\eta = 0\).

Thomas term: as in section 5, the scallop \(x = A\cos(N_s\theta)/(N_s^2-1)\) times \(\partial B/\partial\theta\) gives \(-[A^2N_s^2/(N_s^2-1)]\sin^2(N_s\theta)\,z\) in \(z''\), averaging to \([N_s^2/(N_s^2-1)]F\) with \(F = A^2/2\).

Ring: \(F = (1-\alpha)/\alpha = 1.23\). Need \(1 + 2\tan^2\xi = (0.25 + 1.653)\times63/(64\times1.227) = 1.527\), so \(\tan\xi = 0.513\) and \(\xi = 27^\circ\). Rough, since the smooth formula assumes small flutter.
