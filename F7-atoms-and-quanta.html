F7 Atoms, nuclei and quanta

1897 to 1935: Thomson’s electron, Planck’s and Einstein’s quanta, Rutherford’s nucleus, Bohr’s atom, de Broglie’s waves, Chadwick’s neutron and Anderson’s positron. What matter is made of, how big the pieces are, how much energy holds them together, and why light and matter both come in lumps. The particles every accelerator pushes, and the energies it must reach, are named here.

What this chapter assumes. High-school physics and mathematics, F1 Calculus (derivatives, exponential decay), F2 Vectors (circular motion), F3 Newtonian mechanics (kinetic energy, momentum and its conservation), F4 Electricity and magnetism (Coulomb’s law, potential, the force \(q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\)), F5 Oscillations and waves (wavelength, frequency, interference) and F6 Heat, gases and probability (\(k_BT\), the mean of a random count). Section 0 lists what is used.

What comes next. Chapter 1 takes every part of this chapter deeper, in its pre-requisite sections: Nuclear reactions and Q value, De Broglie waves and uncertainty, Tunnelling, Photons and the photoelectric effect, Four-momentum and Rutherford scattering. Relativity itself, which this chapter only quotes, is taught in Lorentz transformations.

In one page

The problem. An accelerator pushes particles, and the particles are pieces of atoms: electrons, protons, nuclei, and the antiparticles and short-lived particles that collisions make. To follow any chapter of the course one has to know what these pieces are, how big they are, what holds them together and how much energy it takes to pull them apart or to make them. The physics of 1900, Newton’s mechanics and Maxwell’s electromagnetism, could not answer: it predicted that atoms should collapse, that light of any colour should knock out electrons if it were bright enough, and it had no idea where the energy of radioactivity came from.

The idea. Matter is made of atoms about \(10^{-10}\) m across, each a cloud of electrons round a nucleus \(10^{-15}\) to \(10^{-14}\) m across that holds almost all the mass. Energy is exchanged in lumps, quanta: light of frequency \(f\) comes in photons of energy \(hf\), and an atom’s electrons can hold only certain energies. Particles of matter behave as waves of wavelength \(h/p\), so a probe sees detail only down to its wavelength, and a particle confined to a small space must move fast. Inside the nucleus a strong, short-range force binds protons and neutrons with energies of millions of electron-volts, a million times the energies of chemistry, and a measurable part of the mass: \(E = mc^2\). Those energies, the MeV of nuclei and the GeV and TeV of new particles, are what accelerators are built to reach.

Equations to remember.

  • Energy unit: 1 eV \(= 1.602\,176\,634 \times 10^{-19}\) J, the energy of charge \(e\) across 1 V. Rest energies: \(m_ec^2 = 0.511\) MeV, \(m_pc^2 = 938.272\) MeV, 1 u \(= 931.494\) MeV\(/c^2\).
  • Photon: \(E = hf = hc/\lambda\), momentum \(p = E/c = h/\lambda\), with \(hc = 1239.84\) eV nm \(= 1239.84\) MeV fm.
  • X-ray tube at voltage \(V\): shortest wavelength \(\lambda_{min} = hc/(eV)\).
  • Bohr’s hydrogen: \(E_n = -13.606\ \text{eV}/n^2\), radius \(r_n = n^2a_0\) with \(a_0 = 0.0529\) nm; a photon from level \(n_2\) to \(n_1\) has \(hf = E_{n_2} - E_{n_1}\).
  • De Broglie: \(\lambda = h/p\). Uncertainty: \(\Delta x\,\Delta p \gtrsim \hbar\), with \(\hbar = h/2\pi\) and \(\hbar c = 197.327\) MeV fm.
  • Nuclear radius: \(R \approx 1.2\,A^{1/3}\) fm, with \(A\) the number of protons and neutrons.
  • Binding energy: \(B = \big(Z\,m(^1\mathrm{H}) + N m_n - M_{atom}\big)c^2\); Q value of a reaction: \(Q = (\text{masses before} - \text{masses after})\,c^2\).
  • Decay: \(N = N_0e^{-\lambda_d t}\), half-life \(t_{1/2} = \ln 2/\lambda_d\), activity \(\mathcal{A} = \lambda_d N\) in becquerels; 1 Ci \(= 3.7 \times 10^{10}\) Bq.
  • Pair creation needs at least \(2m_ec^2 = 1.022\) MeV.

Numbers worth knowing.

  • Atoms are about 0.1 to 0.3 nm across; copper has \(8.5 \times 10^{22}\) atoms per cm\(^3\), 0.23 nm apart. Avogadro’s number is \(6.022 \times 10^{23}\) per mole.
  • \(e/m_e = 1.759 \times 10^{11}\) C/kg; the proton is 1836 times heavier than the electron.
  • Room temperature is \(k_BT = 0.026\) eV; visible light 1.6 to 3.3 eV a photon; chemistry a few eV; hydrogen’s ionisation 13.6 eV; X-rays 1 to 100 keV; nuclear binding about 8 MeV per nucleon; a proton in the LHC 6.8 TeV, \(1.09 \times 10^{-6}\) J.
  • A 2 kV electron has a wavelength of 0.027 nm; a 1 GeV proton 0.73 fm, about its own size.
  • Lithium-7 plus a proton gives two alphas and 17.35 MeV, 0.23 per cent of the mass.
  • Binding energy per nucleon peaks near iron and nickel at 8.79 MeV; helium-4 has 7.07, uranium-238 7.57.
  • One curie of polonium-210 is 0.22 mg of it, and sends out alphas carrying 12 nA of charge in all directions; a 1 μA proton beam is \(6.24 \times 10^{12}\) particles a second, all one way.

The problem it solved

Atoms, and their size

John Dalton, in his New System of Chemical Philosophy (1808), explained why elements combine in fixed proportions by weight: each element is made of identical atoms, and compounds of atoms joined in small whole numbers. Amedeo Avogadro proposed in 1811 that equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure hold equal numbers of molecules. Neither could say how many, or how big. Josef Loschmidt in 1865 made the first estimate of the size of a molecule from the viscosity of gases, and found it below a nanometre. Many physicists still doubted that atoms were real rather than a bookkeeping device until Jean Perrin’s experiments on Brownian motion, from 1908, confirmed Einstein’s theory of 1905 and gave Avogadro’s number to within a few per cent.

The electron, and quanta

In 1897 J. J. Thomson measured the ratio of charge to mass of cathode rays and found it over a thousand times that of the hydrogen ion: the rays were particles far lighter than any atom, carried out of every kind of cathode. Atoms had parts. Robert Millikan, from 1909 to 1913, measured the charge itself on oil drops. Meanwhile Max Planck, in December 1900, could fit the spectrum of the light from a hot body only by supposing that energy is exchanged with light in lumps \(hf\); Albert Einstein, in 1905, took the lumps literally and explained the photoelectric effect with them.

The nucleus and the quantum atom

Ernest Rutherford, with Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, fired alpha particles at thin metal foils; in 1909 a few bounced back, and in 1911 Rutherford explained it by a tiny, massive, positive nucleus. Such an atom should collapse by classical physics, its electrons spiralling in as they radiate. Niels Bohr, in 1913, kept it standing by decree: only certain orbits are allowed. Louis de Broglie in 1924 proposed that electrons are waves, Werner Heisenberg and Erwin Schrödinger in 1925 and 1926 built quantum mechanics, and Max Born in 1926 said what the waves are: amplitudes of probability.

Inside the nucleus

Francis Aston’s mass spectrograph showed from 1919 that atomic masses are close to whole numbers, but not exactly, and that four hydrogen atoms weigh more than one of helium. James Chadwick found the neutron in February 1932. In the same year Carl Anderson found the positron in cosmic rays, and John Cockcroft and Ernest Walton split lithium with protons from a machine, the first nuclear reaction driven by an accelerator (chapter 5). Their two alphas carried away exactly the energy that the missing mass predicted.

The physics

0. What this chapter assumes, and a quick check

  • Mechanics (F3). Kinetic energy \(W = \tfrac12 mv^2\) (the course writes \(W\) for kinetic energy and keeps \(E\) for total energy); momentum \(p = mv\), so \(W = p^2/(2m)\); momentum is conserved when no outside force acts.
  • Electricity (F4). Like charges repel with force \(q_1q_2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)\) and have potential energy \(q_1q_2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)\); a charge \(q\) crossing a potential difference \(V\) gains energy \(qV\); a charge moving at \(v\) across a magnetic field \(B\) feels a force \(qvB\) at right angles to its motion.
  • Circles (F2). A body moving on a circle of radius \(r\) at speed \(v\) needs a force \(mv^2/r\) towards the centre.
  • Waves (F5). Speed \(=\) frequency \(\times\) wavelength, \(c = f\lambda\) for light; waves from two sources add up in step where their paths differ by a whole number of wavelengths.
  • Decay (F1). \(dN/dt = -\lambda N\) gives \(N = N_0e^{-\lambda t}\).
  • Powers of ten. nano (n) \(10^{-9}\), pico (p) \(10^{-12}\), femto (f) \(10^{-15}\); kilo, mega, giga, tera are \(10^3\), \(10^6\), \(10^9\), \(10^{12}\).

Check yourself. (a) A body of 2 kg moves at 3 m/s; find its kinetic energy and momentum. (b) What energy does a charge of \(2e\) gain across 5 kV, in joules? (c) Light of wavelength 500 nm: what is its frequency? (d) How many femtometres in a nanometre? Answers: (a) 9 J and 6 kg m/s; (b) \(2 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \times 5000 = 1.60 \times 10^{-15}\) J; (c) \(3.00 \times 10^8/5 \times 10^{-7} = 6.0 \times 10^{14}\) Hz; (d) \(10^6\).

1. Atoms: how many, how big

The mole and Avogadro’s number. Chemists weigh, but reactions count atoms. The bridge is the mole: an amount holding exactly \(N_A = 6.022\,140\,76 \times 10^{23}\) particles, Avogadro’s number (fixed exactly by the SI since 2019). It is chosen so that a mole of a substance weighs, in grams, about its molecular mass in atomic mass units. The atomic mass unit is one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom:

\[ 1\ \mathrm{u} = 1.660\,539 \times 10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}, \qquad N_A \times 1\ \mathrm{u} = 1.000\,000\ \mathrm{g} \]

(the second equality is exact to about one part in \(10^9\)). So 12 g of carbon-12 is one mole, \(6.022 \times 10^{23}\) atoms, and 63.546 g of copper (the average over its isotopes) is a mole of copper atoms.

How big is an atom? In a solid the atoms touch, so the volume per atom gives their size. Copper has a density of 8.96 g/cm\(^3\). A cubic centimetre holds \(8.96/63.546\) mol, that is

\[ n = \frac{8.96}{63.546} \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 8.49 \times 10^{22}\ \text{atoms per cm}^3 . \]

Each atom has a cube of side \(n^{-1/3}\), which is \((8.49 \times 10^{28}\ \mathrm{m}^{-3})^{-1/3} = 0.228\) nm. Water gives 0.31 nm per molecule the same way. Atoms are a few tenths of a nanometre across, \(10^{-10}\) m being a convenient round size (it was once called the ångström, Å).

A gas is mostly empty. At 0 °C and one atmosphere, a gas holds \(p/(k_BT) = 101\,325/(1.381 \times 10^{-23} \times 273.15) = 2.69 \times 10^{25}\) molecules per m\(^3\) (F6), so the molecules are on average 3.3 nm apart, ten times their size. Chapter 3 pumps this down by factors of \(10^{12}\) and more, so that a beam can travel kilometres without meeting a molecule.

Why this matters to an accelerator. A beam is counted in particles, a target in atoms per unit area, and a vacuum in molecules per unit volume. Every rate in the course is a product of such numbers (chapter 1, section 6).

2. The electron

Thomson’s measurement. A cathode ray is bent by electric and magnetic fields, so it carries charge; the question was what kind of thing it is. A particle of charge \(e\) and mass \(m\) moving at speed \(v\) across a magnetic field \(B\) feels a force \(evB\) at right angles to its motion and moves on a circle (F2) of radius \(r\) where

\[ \frac{mv^2}{r} = evB \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = \frac{mv}{eB} . \]

The radius gives \(m/e\) only if \(v\) is known. Thomson’s idea (1897) was to add an electric field \(\mathcal{E}\) at right angles to both \(B\) and the beam, pushing the other way, and adjust it until the beam went straight. Then the two forces balance, \(e\mathcal{E} = evB\), and

\[ v = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{B}, \qquad \frac{e}{m} = \frac{v}{rB} = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{rB^2} . \]

The modern value is \(e/m_e = 1.758\,820 \times 10^{11}\) C/kg. Hydrogen ions in electrolysis have \(e/m = 9.58 \times 10^7\) C/kg. If the charges are equal, the electron is 1836 times lighter than the lightest atom.

A worked number. Electrons from 2 kV have \(\tfrac12 m_ev^2 = eV\), so \(v = \sqrt{2(e/m_e)V} = 2.65 \times 10^7\) m/s. In a field of 1 mT they curve on a radius \(r = v/((e/m_e)B) = 0.151\) m, and an electric field of \(vB = 26.5\) kV/m straightens them: 265 V across plates 1 cm apart. These are the sizes of the fields in a cathode-ray tube (chapter 2).

Millikan’s charge. Ratios do not give the charge. Millikan, from 1909, sprayed fine oil drops between two horizontal plates and watched single drops through a microscope. A drop picks up a few electrons’ worth of charge \(q\). With a voltage \(V\) across plates a distance \(d\) apart, the upward electric force \(qV/d\) can hold the drop against its weight \(mg\). He found the drop’s mass from how fast it fell without the field (a small sphere falling through air reaches a steady speed fixed by its size), and the charges always came out as whole multiples of one value.

A worked number. A drop of oil of density 900 kg/m\(^3\) and radius 1 μm has mass \(\tfrac43\pi r^3\rho = 3.77 \times 10^{-15}\) kg and weight \(3.70 \times 10^{-14}\) N. To hold it up with one electron’s charge needs a field \(mg/e = 2.31 \times 10^5\) V/m, 2.3 kV across 1 cm. With two electrons, half that. The voltage at which a drop hangs still jumps in steps when the drop catches another electron.

Millikan’s value of 1913, \(1.592 \times 10^{-19}\) C, was 0.6 per cent low, mainly because the viscosity of air he used was slightly wrong. Today \(e = 1.602\,176\,634 \times 10^{-19}\) C exactly, by the SI’s definition since 2019. With \(e/m_e\) this gives \(m_e = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}\) kg.

Charge and Avogadro together. Electrolysis had long shown that one mole of singly charged ions carries the Faraday constant, \(F = 96\,485\) C. Dividing by \(e\) gives \(N_A = F/e = 6.022 \times 10^{23}\): once the electron’s charge was measured, the number of atoms in a mole followed. A current of 1 A is \(1/e = 6.24 \times 10^{18}\) electrons a second, and 1 μA of singly charged ions is \(6.24 \times 10^{12}\) ions a second, the number that runs through every chapter.

3. The electron-volt

The unit of the course. A particle of charge \(e\) that crosses a potential difference of 1 V gains

\[ 1\ \mathrm{eV} = 1.602\,176\,634 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J} . \]

An accelerator works by voltages, so the energy it gives is naturally counted in eV: an electron or proton through 2 kV gains 2 keV, whatever its mass; an alpha particle, of charge \(2e\), gains 4 keV through the same 2 kV. The prefixes run keV (\(10^3\) eV), MeV (\(10^6\)), GeV (\(10^9\)) and TeV (\(10^{12}\)).

A ladder of energies. The electron-volt is well matched to the world of atoms, which is why it is so useful:

What Energy
Thermal energy at room temperature, \(k_BT\) at 300 K 0.026 eV
One photon of visible light 1.6 to 3.3 eV
A chemical bond; the work function of a metal 1 to 5 eV
Ionising a hydrogen atom 13.6 eV
Electrons in a cathode-ray tube 2 to 30 keV
X-ray photons 1 to 100 keV
Alpha particles from radium and polonium 4 to 9 MeV
Binding energy per nucleon in a nucleus about 8 MeV
Rest energy of a proton 938 MeV
A proton in the LHC 6.8 TeV

One temperature in kelvin corresponds to \(k_B/e = 8.617 \times 10^{-5}\) eV; 1 eV of thermal energy is 11 600 K. The gap between the eV of atoms and the MeV of nuclei is the most important fact in this chapter: it is why chemistry cannot touch a nucleus, and why nuclear energy is a million times chemical energy, weight for weight.

Small, and large. One joule is \(6.24 \times 10^{18}\) eV. A proton of 6.8 TeV carries \(6.8 \times 10^{12} \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19} = 1.09 \times 10^{-6}\) J, about the kinetic energy of a flying mosquito (2.5 mg at 0.4 m/s has \(2.0 \times 10^{-7}\) J, 1.25 TeV). Tiny for an object, enormous for one particle. A full LHC beam holds about \(3.2 \times 10^{14}\) protons, and so about 350 MJ, enough to melt more than half a tonne of copper; chapter 21 has to put it somewhere safely.

Mass and momentum in eV. Since \(E = mc^2\) (section 11), a mass can be named by its rest energy: the electron’s \(m_ec^2 = 0.511\) MeV, the proton’s 938.272 MeV. Masses are then written in MeV\(/c^2\) and momenta in MeV\(/c\) (1 GeV\(/c\) \(= 5.344 \times 10^{-19}\) kg m/s). The atomic mass unit is \(1\ \mathrm{u} = 931.494\) MeV\(/c^2\). Writing them this way turns the factors of \(c\) into bookkeeping; chapter 1 (section 2.3) gives the full table.

4. Light as particles

Planck’s lump. A hot body glows, and the spread of its light over frequencies was measured well by 1900. Classical physics, sharing the energy equally among all the ways the light in a box can vibrate, predicted infinitely much light at high frequency. Planck found the right formula by assuming that light of frequency \(f\) exchanges energy with matter only in whole lumps

\[ E = hf, \qquad h = 6.626\,070\,15 \times 10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s} = 4.136 \times 10^{-15}\ \mathrm{eV\,s} . \]

Planck’s constant \(h\) (exact in the SI since 2019) is tiny, which is why the lumps go unnoticed in daily life. Green light of 550 nm has \(f = c/\lambda = 5.45 \times 10^{14}\) Hz and \(hf = 2.25\) eV.

The handy form. Since \(f = c/\lambda\), \(E = hc/\lambda\), and

\[ hc = 1239.84\ \mathrm{eV\,nm} = 1239.84\ \mathrm{MeV\,fm} . \]

A photon’s energy in eV is 1239.84 divided by its wavelength in nanometres: 2.33 eV at 532 nm, 10.2 eV at 121.6 nm, 12.4 keV at 0.1 nm. The same number in MeV and femtometres serves nuclei.

The photoelectric effect. Light on a clean metal knocks out electrons. Lenard found in 1902 that brighter light gives more electrons, not faster ones, and that below a certain frequency none come out at all. A wave would shake the electrons harder the brighter it was. Einstein’s explanation (1905): each electron absorbs one photon, spends an energy \(\phi_W\) (the work function, a few eV) to escape the metal, and keeps the rest:

\[ W_{max} = hf - \phi_W . \]

Bright light means more photons, so more electrons, each with the same energy. Below \(f = \phi_W/h\) no single photon has enough. Zinc, with \(\phi_W \approx 4.3\) eV, needs wavelengths below \(1239.84/4.3 = 288\) nm, ultraviolet; the 253.7 nm line of a mercury lamp gives photons of 4.89 eV and electrons of up to 0.59 eV, stopped by a reverse voltage of 0.59 V. Caesium, at about 2.1 eV, responds to visible light. Millikan’s measurements of 1916 put \(W_{max}\) against \(f\) on a straight line of slope \(h\). The full account, and why photocathodes and photomultipliers are coated as they are, is in chapter 1 and chapter 2.

How many photons. A 1 W laser at 532 nm sends \(1/(2.33 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19}) = 2.68 \times 10^{18}\) photons a second. The lumps are small but countable: a photomultiplier counts single ones.

Photons carry momentum. A photon has no mass, but it has momentum

\[ p = \frac{E}{c} = \frac{h}{\lambda} . \]

This follows from relativity’s \(E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2\) with \(m = 0\) (chapter 1); Maxwell’s theory already gave light a pressure, momentum \(E/c\) for energy \(E\). A 1 W beam absorbed by a black surface pushes on it with \(1/c = 3.3 \times 10^{-9}\) N. In 1923 Arthur Compton found that X-rays scattered from electrons come off with longer wavelengths, exactly as if photon and electron collided like billiard balls with this momentum.

Wave or particle? Both, in a precise sense. Light spreads and interferes as a wave (F5), and the wave fixes the probability of where photons arrive; each arrival is one whole photon at one point. Section 9 says the same of electrons.

5. X-rays

How they are made. Wilhelm Röntgen found X-rays in November 1895 coming from where cathode rays struck the glass of his tube. An X-ray tube does it on purpose: electrons from a hot cathode are accelerated through a voltage \(V\), tens of kilovolts, onto a metal anode, usually tungsten or copper. Two kinds of radiation come out.

The continuous spectrum, and its edge. An electron entering the metal is deflected and slowed by the nuclei’s fields, and an accelerated charge radiates (F4). This bremsstrahlung (“braking radiation”) comes out as photons of every energy up to a sharp limit: one photon can at most take all of the electron’s kinetic energy \(eV\). So

\[ \lambda_{min} = \frac{hc}{eV}, \qquad \lambda_{min}\,[\mathrm{nm}] = \frac{1239.84}{V\,[\mathrm{volts}]} . \]

This is the Duane–Hunt law (1915). A 30 kV tube gives nothing shorter than 41.3 pm; a 100 kV tube, 12.4 pm. The edge does not depend on the anode, only on the voltage, which is why measuring it measured \(h/e\), and why an electron accelerator’s energy fixes the hardest X-rays it can make.

Most of the energy is heat. Only a small fraction of the electron’s energy leaves as X-rays, roughly \(10^{-9}ZV\) with \(V\) in volts and \(Z\) the anode’s atomic number: for tungsten (\(Z = 74\)) at 100 kV, about 0.7 per cent. The rest heats the anode, which is why X-ray tubes have rotating, water-cooled anodes, and why every beam stopped in matter makes heat (chapter 1).

The lines. On top of the continuum sit sharp lines, different for each anode metal. The fast electron knocks an electron out of an inner shell of an anode atom, and an outer electron drops into the hole, emitting a photon of the energy difference (section 7 explains why such energies are fixed). For copper the strongest, K\(\alpha\), is at 8.05 keV, 0.154 nm. Henry Moseley found in 1913 that the K\(\alpha\) energy of each element goes as \((Z - 1)^2\), close to

\[ E_{K\alpha} \approx \tfrac34 \times 13.6\ \mathrm{eV} \times (Z - 1)^2 , \]

which gives 8.00 keV for copper (\(Z = 29\)) and 17.2 keV for molybdenum (\(Z = 42\), measured 17.5 keV). Moseley’s law showed that \(Z\), the charge of the nucleus, not the atomic weight, is what orders the elements.

Measuring a wavelength. In 1912 Max von Laue showed that crystals diffract X-rays, and William Henry and Lawrence Bragg turned it into a measuring instrument: rows of atoms a distance \(d\) apart reflect strongly where \(2d\sin\theta = n\lambda\) (F5). Copper K\(\alpha\) on rock salt (\(d = 0.282\) nm) reflects at \(\theta = 15.9°\). X-rays measure atomic spacings, and atomic spacings measure X-rays.

6. The nuclear atom

The question. Thomson’s electrons are negative and light; atoms are neutral and heavy. Where are the positive charge and the mass? Thomson pictured a diffuse sphere of positive charge with electrons embedded in it. Such a spread-out charge can deflect a fast alpha particle by only a small angle.

Geiger and Marsden. In 1909 they found that about one alpha in 8000 striking a thin platinum foil came back towards the source. Rutherford said it was as if a shell fired at tissue paper had bounced back. In 1911 he showed that a large deflection needs a large force, and so a small distance from a concentrated charge: the positive charge and nearly all the mass sit in a nucleus at the centre. His formula for how many alphas scatter through each angle, which follows from Coulomb’s law and Newton’s mechanics alone, matched Geiger and Marsden’s counts over a huge range of angles; it is derived in chapter 1.

How close. An alpha (charge \(2e\)) aimed straight at a nucleus of charge \(Ze\) slows as it climbs the electric potential energy \(2Ze^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)\) and stops where that equals its kinetic energy \(W\) (F1, section 7, did the integral):

\[ d = \frac{2Z\,e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,W}, \qquad \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 1.440\ \mathrm{MeV\,fm} . \]

The combination \(e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0 = 1.440\) MeV fm is worth memorising; 1 fm, a femtometre or fermi, is \(10^{-15}\) m. A 7.687 MeV alpha of radium C′ on gold (\(Z = 79\)) stops at \(d = 2 \times 79 \times 1.440/7.687 = 29.6\) fm. Since Rutherford’s formula held, the nucleus must be smaller than that. The gold atom is about 0.14 nm in radius; its nucleus, we now know, about 7 fm. The atom is twenty thousand times wider than its nucleus: if the nucleus were a pea, the atom would be a stadium.

What it left unexplained. An electron orbiting a nucleus is accelerating, and classical electromagnetism says it should radiate, lose energy and spiral in, in about \(10^{-11}\) s. Atoms do not collapse, and they emit light only at certain sharp wavelengths. The next section is the first answer.

7. Bohr’s hydrogen atom, and energy levels

The spectrum to explain. Hot hydrogen glows at sharp wavelengths: 656 nm (red), 486 nm, 434 nm, 410 nm, crowding together towards 365 nm. Johann Balmer found in 1885 that they fit \(1/\lambda \propto 1/4 - 1/n^2\) for \(n = 3, 4, 5, \dots\), and Johannes Rydberg generalised it. Nobody knew why.

Bohr’s rule. Bohr (1913) took the electron, of mass \(m_e\), on a circle of radius \(r\) round the proton, and kept Newton’s law: the Coulomb force supplies the force towards the centre,

\[ \frac{m_ev^2}{r} = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2} . \]

Then he added a rule with no classical justification: the angular momentum \(m_evr\) can only be a whole number of units of \(\hbar = h/2\pi\),

\[ m_evr = n\hbar, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots \]

Solving. From the first equation, \(m_ev^2r = e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0\). Divide it by the square of the second, \((m_evr)^2 = n^2\hbar^2\): this gives \(1/(m_er) = (e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0)/(n^2\hbar^2)\), so

\[ r_n = n^2 a_0, \qquad a_0 = \frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2}{m_ee^2} = 0.0529\ \mathrm{nm} . \]

The smallest orbit, the Bohr radius \(a_0\), is 0.053 nm, the right size for an atom. The energy is kinetic plus potential. The first equation says \(\tfrac12 m_ev^2 = \tfrac12 e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)\), half the size of the potential energy \(-e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)\), so the total is

\[ E_n = -\frac{e^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r_n} = -\frac{13.606\ \mathrm{eV}}{n^2} . \]

The energy is negative because the electron is bound: it takes \(+13.6\) eV to pull it from the lowest level, \(n = 1\) (the ground state), to rest far away. That is hydrogen’s measured ionisation energy. In the ground state the electron moves at \(v = 2.19 \times 10^6\) m/s, \(c/137\).

Spectra. When the electron drops from level \(n_2\) to a lower \(n_1\), the atom emits one photon carrying the difference:

\[ hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = 13.606\ \mathrm{eV}\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right) . \]

From 3 to 2: \(13.606 \times (1/4 - 1/9) = 1.890\) eV, so \(\lambda = 1239.84/1.890 = 656.1\) nm, Balmer’s red line. From 2 to 1: 10.20 eV, 121.5 nm, in the ultraviolet (the Lyman-\(\alpha\) line). The series limit, \(n_2 \to \infty\), is \(1239.84/13.606 = 91.1\) nm. Balmer’s formula is explained. The measured red line is at 656.5 nm in vacuum (656.3 nm in air): the 0.05 per cent difference is because the proton is not infinitely heavy and the two move about their common centre, which replaces \(m_e\) by \(m_e m_p/(m_e + m_p)\).

Absorption runs the other way. An atom in level \(n_1\) absorbs only photons of exactly the energy to lift it to a higher level, or anything above its ionisation energy. That is why gases are transparent except at their lines, and why an electron or ion beam crossing a gas excites and ionises it, so that its path glows (chapter 8).

Heavier atoms, and ions. With one electron round a nucleus of charge \(Ze\), every \(e^2\) in the derivation becomes \(Ze^2\): the energies scale as \(Z^2\) and the radii as \(1/Z\). Singly ionised helium binds its last electron with \(4 \times 13.6 = 54.4\) eV, doubly ionised lithium with 122.4 eV. Stripping the last electron from uranium would take, by Bohr’s formula, \(92^2 \times 13.6\) eV \(= 115\) keV (the true value, 132 keV, is higher because that electron moves at over half the speed of light and needs relativity). This is why heavy-ion accelerators strip their ions in stages, through foils, as the ions gain energy.

What Bohr got right, and what not. Bohr’s model gives hydrogen’s levels exactly, by luck as much as insight. It fails for helium and every atom with more than one electron, and its picture of orbits is wrong: quantum mechanics (section 9) replaces them by clouds. But its central idea survives intact. A bound system can hold only certain energies, its energy levels, and it changes between them by absorbing or emitting exactly the difference. Molecules have levels (vibrations at about 0.1 eV, rotations at about 0.001 eV); nuclei have levels (MeV apart, emitting gamma rays, section 13).

One more bound state: the H⁻ ion. A hydrogen atom can hold a second electron, loosely, with 0.754 eV. The negative hydrogen ion H⁻ is accelerated in many machines because its two electrons can be stripped off in a thin foil, turning it into a proton at the right moment (chapter 6 uses this in the tandem, and chapter 21 for injection into rings). Its rest energy is \(m_pc^2 + 2m_ec^2\) less 14.4 eV of binding, 939.294 MeV.

8. Matter waves

De Broglie’s proposal. If light, a wave, has particle properties (\(p = h/\lambda\)), perhaps particles have wave properties. De Broglie (1924) proposed that a particle of momentum \(p\) has a wavelength

\[ \lambda = \frac{h}{p} . \]

Electron diffraction. In 1927 Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer scattered 54 eV electrons from a nickel crystal and saw a strong peak at 50°, as waves reflected from rows of atoms 0.215 nm apart would give. The electron’s momentum is \(p = \sqrt{2m_eW}\), and in practical units, since \(pc = \sqrt{2(m_ec^2)W}\),

\[ \lambda = \frac{hc}{\sqrt{2\,m_ec^2\,W}} = \frac{1239.84\ \mathrm{eV\,nm}}{\sqrt{2 \times 511\,000\ \mathrm{eV} \times 54\ \mathrm{eV}}} = 0.167\ \mathrm{nm}, \]

and the condition for waves from neighbouring rows to arrive in step, \(d\sin\theta = \lambda\), gives \(0.215 \times \sin 50° = 0.165\) nm. The same year George Paget Thomson, J. J.’s son, passed fast electrons through thin metal foils and photographed diffraction rings. For electrons through \(V\) volts (well below 511 kV) the formula becomes \(\lambda\,[\mathrm{nm}] = 1.226/\sqrt{V}\): an electron of 2 keV has \(\lambda = 0.0274\) nm.

Why nobody notices. A ball of 0.16 kg at 40 m/s has \(\lambda = h/p = 1.0 \times 10^{-34}\) m, absurdly small beside anything it could pass through. Wave effects show only when the wavelength is comparable with the structures met, which for electrons of tens of eV are the spacings of atoms.

Higher energy sees smaller things. A wave cannot show detail much finer than its wavelength (F5): a microscope with light of 500 nm cannot see an atom. To see small things one needs short wavelengths, so large momenta, so high energies. Fast particles need relativity’s momentum, \(pc = \sqrt{W^2 + 2Wmc^2}\) (chapter 1), and then \(\lambda = hc/(pc)\):

Probe \(pc\) \(\lambda\) Sees
Visible light, 2.25 eV 2.25 eV 550 nm cells
Electron, 2 keV 45.2 keV 0.027 nm atoms
Electron, 200 keV (electron microscope) 494 keV 2.5 pm atoms, sharply
Alpha, 7.687 MeV 240 MeV 5.2 fm the outside of a nucleus
Proton, 1 GeV 1.70 GeV 0.73 fm a proton’s size
Proton, 6.8 TeV (LHC) 6.8 TeV \(1.8 \times 10^{-4}\) fm inside a proton

This is the first of chapter 1’s four reasons to accelerate, made precise there, with the sharper length \(\hbar/\Delta p\) set by the momentum a probe gives up (De Broglie waves and uncertainty, and section 1.1).

The uncertainty principle. A wave confined to a region of width \(\Delta x\) cannot have a single sharp wavelength: to make a short pulse one must add waves of a spread of wavelengths (F5, the spectrum of a short pulse). So a particle confined to \(\Delta x\) has a spread of momenta \(\Delta p\). Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle (1927) states the limit:

\[ \Delta x\,\Delta p \geq \frac{\hbar}{2} , \]

with \(\Delta x\) and \(\Delta p\) the standard deviations (F6). For estimates the course uses \(\Delta x\,\Delta p \sim \hbar\). It is not a fault of instruments; it is a property of waves. A particle cannot be both in a definite place and moving with a definite momentum. With \(\hbar c = 197.327\) MeV fm \(= 197.327\) eV nm, estimates are quick:

  • An electron in an atom. Confined to \(\Delta x = 0.1\) nm, it has \(\Delta p \sim \hbar/\Delta x\), and a kinetic energy of about \((\Delta p)^2/(2m_e) = (\hbar c)^2/(2m_ec^2\Delta x^2) = 197.3^2/(2 \times 511\,000 \times 0.01) = 3.8\) eV. That is the scale of atomic binding: the uncertainty principle is why atoms do not collapse. Squeezing the electron closer to the proton lowers its potential energy but raises its kinetic energy faster.
  • A proton in a nucleus. Confined to 5 fm: \((\hbar c)^2/(2m_pc^2\Delta x^2) = 197.3^2/(2 \times 938.3 \times 25) = 0.83\) MeV, and more for smaller nuclei. Nuclear energies are MeV because nuclei are femtometres across.
  • An electron in a nucleus. Confined to 5 fm, \(\Delta p\,c \sim 197.3/5 = 39\) MeV, so the electron would be moving near the speed of light with tens of MeV, far more than nuclear binding could hold. Before 1932 nuclei were thought to contain electrons; this argument was one reason to doubt it.

There is also a relation between energy and time: a state that lasts a time \(\Delta t\) has an energy spread of about \(\hbar/\Delta t\). The 2p level of hydrogen lives 1.6 ns and so is spread over \(\hbar/1.6\ \mathrm{ns} = 4 \times 10^{-7}\) eV; a particle that lives \(10^{-23}\) s is spread over tens of MeV.

When can a beam be treated as classical? In an accelerator a proton’s wavelength is far smaller than the beam pipe, the magnets or anything they shape, so the course treats beams as particles on paths, and wave effects return only when particles collide with nuclei or each other. Chapter 1’s pre-requisite gives the test.

9. A short, honest picture of quantum mechanics

This section states results; deriving them needs a course in quantum mechanics (Tong’s lectures in the Sources). What the course needs is the picture.

The wavefunction. In quantum mechanics a particle is described by a wave, the wavefunction \(\psi(x)\), spread over space. It is not a wave of anything material. Born’s rule (1926): the probability of finding the particle in a small interval \(dx\) around \(x\) is \(|\psi(x)|^2\,dx\). The wave evolves smoothly, by Schrödinger’s equation (1926); a measurement finds the particle at one place, with those probabilities. Interference happens because amplitudes add before they are squared: two paths can cancel where either alone would arrive. Every result of sections 7 and 8 follows from this.

Why energies come in levels: the particle in a box. Put a particle of mass \(m\) in a box of length \(L\) with walls it cannot pass. Its wave must vanish at the walls, so, like a string fixed at both ends (F5), only a whole number of half-wavelengths fit: \(L = n\lambda/2\). Then \(p = h/\lambda = nh/(2L)\) and the energy, \(p^2/2m\), can only be

\[ E_n = \frac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots \]

The lowest energy is not zero: a confined particle cannot be still. For an electron in a box of 0.3 nm, \(E_1 = (hc)^2/(8m_ec^2L^2) = 1239.84^2/(8 \times 511\,000 \times 0.09) = 4.2\) eV; for a proton in a box of 10 fm, \(1239.84^2/(8 \times 938.27 \times 100) = 2.0\) MeV. Confinement makes levels, and their spacing grows as the box shrinks. Bohr’s levels are the same thing for the round “box” of the Coulomb attraction.

Tunnelling. A wave that meets a wall it cannot classically pass does not stop dead: it dies away exponentially inside the wall (as light does just beyond a mirror surface in total internal reflection). If the wall is thin, some of the wave reaches the far side, and the particle appears there with a small probability. This is tunnelling. It explains alpha decay (Gamow, Gurney and Condon, 1928): the alpha in a nucleus has too little energy to climb over the Coulomb barrier, but leaks through it. Because the probability depends exponentially on the barrier’s width and height, the half-lives of alpha emitters spread from 164 μs (polonium-214, 7.69 MeV alphas) to 4.5 billion years (uranium-238, 4.20 MeV alphas). It also made the first nuclear reaction by an accelerator possible: protons of a few hundred keV leaked through lithium’s barrier of about 1.2 MeV, with a probability, for 125 keV protons, of about \(5 \times 10^{-3}\) (chapter 5). The full treatment is in chapter 1. Electrons tunnel out of metal surfaces in strong fields too (field emission), which limits the voltages of chapter 4.

Spin. An electron behaves as if it were a spinning charge: it carries an angular momentum of its own, spin, of size \(\tfrac12\hbar\) along any axis it is measured on, and only two results are possible, “up” or “down”. It is not a ball turning; the electron has no measured size. But the picture gives the right consequence: a spinning charge is a small magnet. The electron’s magnetic moment is very nearly one Bohr magneton,

\[ \mu_B = \frac{e\hbar}{2m_e} = 5.788 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV/T} . \]

In a field \(B\) the two orientations differ in energy by \(2\mu_BB\): \(1.16 \times 10^{-4}\) eV in 1 T, tiny beside the 0.026 eV of room temperature. Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach saw a beam of silver atoms split in two in a non-uniform magnetic field in 1922; George Uhlenbeck and Samuel Goudsmit proposed electron spin in 1925. Protons and neutrons have spin \(\tfrac12\) too, with magnetic moments about 1000 times smaller (the proton’s spin flips at 42.58 MHz per tesla, the frequency of medical MRI). The photon has spin 1. Chapter 7 takes spin further (Spin and magnetic moments); polarised beams, with their spins lined up, appear in later chapters.

The exclusion principle. Wolfgang Pauli (1925): no two electrons in an atom can be in the same state. Each level holds at most two electrons, spin up and spin down. So the electrons of a heavy atom cannot all fall into the lowest level; they fill shells outwards, which gives the periodic table its rows, gives each element its chemistry and gives the inner shells the large binding energies seen in X-ray lines. Protons and neutrons in a nucleus fill their own levels in the same way. Electrons in a metal fill levels up to several eV, which sets the work function (chapter 2).

10. The nucleus

Protons and neutrons. A nucleus holds \(Z\) protons, each of charge \(+e\), and \(N\) neutrons, uncharged and of almost the same mass. Together they are nucleons, and their number is the mass number \(A = Z + N\). \(Z\), the atomic number, fixes the charge, so the number of electrons in the neutral atom, so the chemistry: it decides the element. A nucleus is written \(^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X}\), or \(^{A}\mathrm{X}\), since the symbol X already fixes \(Z\). Nuclei of one element with different \(N\) are isotopes: \(^{12}\mathrm{C}\) and \(^{14}\mathrm{C}\) both have 6 protons, with 6 and 8 neutrons; they behave the same in chemistry and differ in mass and in stability. The proton itself is the hydrogen nucleus, \(^1\mathrm{H}\); the deuteron \(^2\mathrm{H}\) (or d) is a proton and a neutron; the alpha particle is \(^4\mathrm{He}\), two of each. Chapter 1 gives more of the naming (Nuclear reactions and Q value).

Before 1932 a nucleus of mass number \(A\) and charge \(Z\) was thought to be \(A\) protons and \(A - Z\) electrons. Chadwick found the neutron in February 1932, as an uncharged radiation knocked out of beryllium by polonium alphas that could throw protons out of paraffin wax with several MeV; the electrons were no longer needed (section 8 had said they could not fit).

Size. Electron scattering, from the 1950s, measured nuclear sizes directly (a high-energy electron is a wave short enough to see a nucleus, section 8). The radius grows as the cube root of \(A\):

\[ R \approx r_0A^{1/3}, \qquad r_0 \approx 1.2\ \mathrm{fm} . \]

Helium-4 comes out at 1.9 fm, lithium-7 at 2.3 fm, iron-56 at 4.6 fm, gold-197 at 7.0 fm, uranium-238 at 7.4 fm. The cube root means the volume, \(\tfrac43\pi R^3\), is proportional to \(A\): every nucleon takes the same space, like marbles packed in a bag. Nuclear matter has a fixed density,

\[ \frac{1\ \mathrm{u}}{\tfrac43\pi r_0^3} = \frac{1.66 \times 10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}}{7.24 \times 10^{-45}\ \mathrm{m}^3} = 2.3 \times 10^{17}\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}, \]

0.14 nucleons per fm\(^3\), whatever the nucleus. A teaspoon of it would weigh a billion tonnes. The value of \(r_0\) is not sharp: about 1.2 fm describes where the charge is, while 1.4 to 1.5 fm is often used for the reach of the nuclear force (chapter 1, section 1.2).

The strong force. Protons repel. Two of them 1 fm apart push each other apart with energy \(1.44\) MeV and a force of \(1.44\ \mathrm{MeV}/(1\ \mathrm{fm})^2 = 231\) N, the weight of a 24 kg mass on a single particle. Something stronger holds them together. The strong nuclear force acts between nucleons, protons and neutrons alike, attracts strongly at about 1 fm, repels if they are pushed much closer, and falls to nothing beyond 2 or 3 fm. Hideki Yukawa proposed in 1935 that it is carried by a particle whose mass sets its range, \(\hbar/(mc)\); for a range of 1.4 fm that is a rest energy of \(197.3/1.4 \approx 140\) MeV, and the pion, of 139.6 MeV, was found in 1947. Because the force is short-ranged, each nucleon feels only its neighbours, which is why the density is constant. Because the Coulomb repulsion reaches across the whole nucleus, heavy nuclei need more neutrons than protons to stay together: lead-208 has 82 protons and 126 neutrons. Today the strong force is understood as a by-product of the force between quarks inside the nucleons, a story for chapter 23.

The weak force. A fourth force, besides gravity, electromagnetism and the strong force, changes a neutron into a proton or back: it causes beta decay (section 13). It is weak and of very short range, and the course meets it only there.

11. Mass and binding energy

\(E = mc^2\). Relativity says that a body at rest has energy \(E_0 = mc^2\), its rest energy, and that energy and mass are the same quantity measured in different units: a body that gives out energy \(E\) loses mass \(E/c^2\). Why this is so, and how it joins with kinetic energy into \(E = \gamma mc^2\), is chapter 1’s subject (Lorentz transformations and Four-momentum); here it is used. One gram is \(10^{-3} \times (2.998 \times 10^8)^2 = 9.0 \times 10^{13}\) J, and one atomic mass unit is 931.494 MeV. In chemistry the change is unmeasurable: a few eV out of tens of GeV per molecule. In nuclear physics it is a fraction of a per cent, and easily weighed.

The mass defect. A nucleus weighs less than the protons and neutrons it is made of. Pulling it apart into free nucleons would take an energy \(B\), the binding energy, and by \(E = mc^2\) the free nucleons have that much more mass. So

\[ B = \big(Z\,m_p + N\,m_n - m_{nucleus}\big)c^2 . \]

Tables give the masses of neutral atoms, which include \(Z\) electrons. Using the hydrogen atom’s mass in place of the proton’s adds the same \(Z\) electrons on both sides, so the electrons cancel (their own binding energies, of eV to keV, are small enough to ignore here):

\[ B = \big(Z\,m(^1\mathrm{H}) + N\,m_n - M_{atom}\big)c^2 . \]

Helium-4. With the atomic masses of the 2020 Atomic Mass Evaluation (AME2020), \(m(^1\mathrm{H}) = 1.007\,825\) u, \(m_n = 1.008\,665\) u and \(M(^4\mathrm{He}) = 4.002\,603\) u:

\[ 2 \times 1.007\,825 + 2 \times 1.008\,665 - 4.002\,603 = 0.030\,377\ \mathrm{u}, \]

and \(0.030\,377 \times 931.494 = 28.30\) MeV, or 7.07 MeV per nucleon. The helium nucleus is 0.76 per cent lighter than its parts. The deuteron, by contrast, is bound by only 2.22 MeV, 1.11 MeV per nucleon. Aston’s finding that four hydrogen atoms outweigh one of helium led Arthur Eddington to suggest in 1920 that the Sun shines by turning hydrogen into helium; it does.

The curve of binding energy. Plot \(B/A\), the binding energy per nucleon, against \(A\):

\(^4\mathrm{He}\) 28.30 7.07
\(^7\mathrm{Li}\) 39.24 5.61
\(^{12}\mathrm{C}\) 92.16 7.68
\(^{16}\mathrm{O}\) 127.62 7.98
\(^{56}\mathrm{Fe}\) 492.26 8.79
\(^{62}\mathrm{Ni}\) 545.26 8.79

The curve rises steeply through the light nuclei (with helium-4 standing out, tightly bound), flattens at about 8 MeV, peaks near iron and nickel at 8.79 MeV (nickel-62 just highest, then iron-58 and iron-56), and falls slowly to 7.6 MeV for uranium. Two simple pictures explain it. Each nucleon is bound only to its neighbours by the short-range force, so \(B\) grows like \(A\); but nucleons at the surface have fewer neighbours, which costs most in small nuclei, where most nucleons are at the surface. And every proton repels every other, a cost that grows like \(Z^2\) and wins in heavy nuclei. Carl Friedrich von Weizsäcker built a formula from these ideas in 1935.

What the curve means. Energy is released by moving towards the peak. Joining light nuclei (fusion) does so: the Sun’s hydrogen to helium, or deuterium and tritium in a fusion reactor. Splitting heavy ones (fission) does too: uranium-235 split into two nuclei near \(A \approx 118\), each bound by about 8.5 MeV per nucleon instead of 7.6, releases roughly \(236 \times (8.5 - 7.6) \approx 200\) MeV. Compare a chemical reaction’s few eV: weight for weight, nuclear fuel gives about a million times more energy than chemical fuel.

12. Reactions, Q values and the Coulomb barrier

Writing a reaction. A projectile \(a\) striking a nucleus \(A\) and leaving \(B\) and \(b\) is written \(a + A \to B + b\), or \(A(a,b)B\). Charge and the number of nucleons are the same before and after; so are energy and momentum, with rest energies counted.

The Q value. The kinetic energy released is the drop in rest energy:

\[ Q = \big(m_a + m_A - m_b - m_B\big)c^2 . \]

If \(Q > 0\), the products fly apart with more kinetic energy than came in; if \(Q < 0\), that energy must be supplied. Atomic masses may be used, as in section 11, because charge is conserved and the electrons balance.

The worked case: Cockcroft and Walton, 1932. Protons on lithium-7 give two alphas:

\[ p + {}^7\mathrm{Li} \to {}^4\mathrm{He} + {}^4\mathrm{He} . \]

With AME2020 atomic masses, before: \(1.007\,825 + 7.016\,003 = 8.023\,828\) u; after: \(2 \times 4.002\,603 = 8.005\,207\) u. The mass falls by \(0.018\,622\) u, 0.23 per cent of the total, and

\[ Q = 0.018\,622 \times 931.494\ \mathrm{MeV} = 17.35\ \mathrm{MeV}. \]

The proton brought only a few hundred keV. The two alphas share the rest energy turned into motion: they are equal in mass and fly apart with equal and opposite momenta (momentum before was nearly zero), so each takes half, 8.67 MeV, more than any alpha from radium. Cockcroft and Walton, on 14 April 1932, saw their flashes on a zinc sulphide screen; their range in air matched the energy, and the reaction was the first check of \(E = mc^2\) with a nuclear reaction. Chapter 5 tells the story and chapter 1 the general method, including how much of a projectile’s energy is available on a target at rest.

A reaction that costs energy. Rutherford’s transmutation of 1919, \(\alpha + {}^{14}\mathrm{N} \to {}^{17}\mathrm{O} + p\), has \(Q = -1.19\) MeV: the alpha has to pay for it.

Energies in tables. Tables often give the mass excess, \(\Delta = (M - A\,\mathrm{u})c^2\), rather than the mass. Since \(A\) balances in a reaction, \(Q\) is the sum of the \(\Delta\)’s before less the sum after. With \(\Delta(^1\mathrm{H}) = 7.289\) MeV, \(\Delta(^7\mathrm{Li}) = 14.907\) MeV and \(\Delta(^4\mathrm{He}) = 2.425\) MeV, \(Q = 7.289 + 14.907 - 2 \times 2.425 = 17.35\) MeV again, with less arithmetic.

The Coulomb barrier. For a positive projectile to react with a nucleus it must come within reach of the strong force, about where the two surfaces touch. On the way it has to climb the electric repulsion. The barrier’s height is the potential energy at contact, with radii from section 10:

\[ V_C = \frac{zZe^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,(R_1 + R_2)}, \]

for charges \(ze\) and \(Ze\). For a proton on lithium-7, with \(r_0 = 1.2\) fm, \(R_1 + R_2 = 1.2 \times (1 + 7^{1/3}) = 3.50\) fm and \(V_C = 3 \times 1.440/3.50 = 1.24\) MeV. For an alpha on gold it is 25.6 MeV, well above any natural alpha: that is why Rutherford’s alphas scattered from gold by Coulomb’s law alone and never touched the nucleus. Classically a proton needs over a megavolt to get into lithium; tunnelling (section 9) lets in a few protons of a few hundred keV, and an accelerator supplies very many protons. The table of barriers, and the energy a moving target takes away, are in chapter 1 (section 1.2). Neutrons, having no charge, feel no barrier, which is why they are so good at causing reactions.

13. Radioactivity

Three kinds of ray. Henri Becquerel found in 1896 that uranium salts fog a photographic plate in the dark. Marie and Pierre Curie isolated polonium and radium in 1898. Rutherford in 1899 sorted the rays by how easily they are stopped: alpha rays stop in a sheet of paper or a few centimetres of air, beta rays in a few millimetres of aluminium; Paul Villard found in 1900 the far more penetrating gamma rays. Rutherford and Frederick Soddy showed in 1902 that a radioactive atom turns into an atom of another element as it decays. Each kind is now understood:

  • Alpha decay. A heavy nucleus emits a helium-4 nucleus: \(^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X} \to {}^{A-4}_{Z-2}\mathrm{Y} + \alpha\). Polonium-210 decays to lead-206 with \(Q = 5.407\) MeV; the alpha takes \(Q \times 206/210 = 5.304\) MeV and the lead recoils with the rest, since the two share momentum equally and the lighter gets more speed. Alphas from one decay all have one energy: a line.
  • Beta decay. A neutron in the nucleus turns into a proton, emitting an electron and an antineutrino: \(n \to p + e^- + \bar\nu\). The nucleus keeps \(A\) and gains one in \(Z\): carbon-14 becomes nitrogen-14. (In \(\beta^+\) decay a proton turns into a neutron, emitting a positron and a neutrino.) The electrons come out with every energy up to the \(Q\) value, because the unseen neutrino takes the rest; Pauli proposed the neutrino in 1930 for exactly this reason, and it was detected in 1956. A free neutron decays this way with a mean life of about 880 s, releasing \((m_n - m_p - m_e)c^2 = 0.782\) MeV.
  • Gamma decay. A nucleus left in an excited level drops to a lower one, emitting a photon of the difference, as an atom does (section 7) but with MeV instead of eV. Cobalt-60 beta-decays to excited nickel-60, which emits gammas of 1.173 and 1.333 MeV, a standard source for calibration and for radiotherapy.

The decay law. Every unstable nucleus has the same chance \(\lambda_d\) per second of decaying, whatever its age. That is F1’s equation \(dN/dt = -\lambda_dN\), so

\[ N(t) = N_0\,e^{-\lambda_d t}, \qquad t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda_d}, \qquad \tau = \frac{1}{\lambda_d} . \]

The half-life \(t_{1/2}\) is the time for half of any sample to go; the mean life \(\tau\) is 1.44 times longer. Half-lives span an enormous range: polonium-214, 164 μs; polonium-210, 138.4 days; cobalt-60, 5.27 years; carbon-14, 5700 years; radium-226, 1600 years; uranium-238, 4.47 billion years. Which nucleus decays when is pure chance; how many decay in a second, from a large sample, is close to certain, with the fluctuation of a random count (F6, Poisson).

Activity. The number of decays per second is the activity, \(\mathcal{A} = \lambda_dN\), in becquerels (1 Bq = 1 decay per second). The older unit, the curie, was the activity of one gram of radium-226; it is now fixed at 1 Ci \(= 3.7 \times 10^{10}\) Bq.

A worked number. One milligram of polonium-210 holds \(N = (10^{-3}/209.98) \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 2.87 \times 10^{18}\) atoms, and \(\lambda_d = \ln 2/(138.4 \times 86\,400\ \mathrm{s}) = 5.80 \times 10^{-8}\) s\(^{-1}\), so \(\mathcal{A} = 1.66 \times 10^{11}\) Bq, 4.5 Ci. A curie of polonium is 0.22 mg, a speck. After a year, \(e^{-\lambda_d t} = 16.0\) per cent of it is left.

Living with it. A human body holds about 140 g of potassium, of which 0.0117 per cent of the atoms are radioactive potassium-40 (\(t_{1/2} = 1.25 \times 10^9\) years): about 4400 Bq. Activity is everywhere; what matters is how much energy it deposits, and where (chapter 28).

The accelerators before accelerators. From 1899 to 1932 the only fast particles available were those of radioactive sources and of cosmic rays (found by Victor Hess, on balloon flights, in 1912). The alphas of polonium-210 (5.304 MeV), radium C′ (polonium-214, 7.687 MeV) and thorium C′ (polonium-212, 8.785 MeV) found the nucleus and the first transmutation. They had three faults that the accelerator removed. Their energy was fixed by nature. They went out in all directions, so only a small fraction reached a target. And they were few: one curie, \(3.7 \times 10^{10}\) alphas a second spread over the whole sphere, carries \(3.7 \times 10^{10} \times 2e = 12\) nA of charge, while a modest accelerator gives a microampere, \(6.24 \times 10^{12}\) protons a second, in a single narrow beam. To match it with alphas would take 170 Ci of source, aimed perfectly. Chapter 1’s The wall tells how this limit was felt.

14. Antimatter

Dirac’s prediction, Anderson’s track. Paul Dirac’s relativistic equation for the electron (1928) had solutions of negative energy, which he came by 1931 to interpret as a new particle: an anti-electron, of the electron’s mass and opposite charge. In 1932 Carl Anderson, photographing cosmic-ray tracks in a cloud chamber in a magnetic field, found a track that curved like a positive particle but was far too thin and long-ranged to be a proton. He called it the positron. Every particle has an antiparticle of the same mass and opposite charge (a neutral particle may be its own); the antiproton was made at Berkeley’s Bevatron in 1955 (chapter 1, section 1.3).

Pair creation. A photon passing close to a nucleus can turn into an electron and a positron. Charge is conserved (\(0 = -1 + 1\)), and the energy must at least pay both rest energies:

\[ E_\gamma \geq 2m_ec^2 = 1.022\ \mathrm{MeV} . \]

(The nucleus is needed to take up momentum, as a photon alone cannot turn into a pair at rest and conserve both energy and momentum; being heavy, it takes almost no energy.) Patrick Blackett and Giuseppe Occhialini photographed such pairs in 1933. Above about 10 MeV in heavy materials pair creation is the main way photons are absorbed, which is how the electromagnetic showers of detectors multiply.

Annihilation. A positron meeting an electron at rest annihilates into two photons of 0.511 MeV each, going in opposite directions (two, so that their momenta cancel). The wavelength of each is \(1239.84\ \mathrm{MeV\,fm}/0.511\ \mathrm{MeV} = 2426\) fm \(= 2.43\) pm. Medical PET scanners detect these pairs of photons from positron-emitting isotopes made by small cyclotrons (chapter 28).

Making antimatter on purpose. Positrons are made in quantity by sending an electron beam into a heavy target: bremsstrahlung photons make pairs. Antiprotons need a proton beam of several GeV on a target, since an antiproton must be made with an extra proton (chapter 1, section 1.3). Collisions of matter and antimatter beams are the subject of chapter 23, and cooling the antiprotons so they can be collected, of chapter 24.

A few remarks for reading the table.

  • “Stable alone.” A positron or antiproton lives forever in a vacuum, and annihilates when it meets its partner. Keeping antiprotons for days in a ring with a good vacuum (chapter 3) is routine.
  • Short lives are longer in flight. A muon lives 2.197 μs at rest, so \(c\tau = 659\) m. A muon of 1 GeV kinetic energy has \(\gamma = 1 + 1000/105.66 = 10.5\) and travels on average 6.9 km before decaying, because its clock runs slow (chapter 1). That is why muons made high in the atmosphere reach the ground.
  • Ions. An ion is named by its nucleus and its charge state: \(^{208}\)Pb\(^{82+}\) is lead with all 82 electrons removed. Its energy is quoted per nucleon, or as the energy a proton would have in the same magnets multiplied by \(Z\). In the LHC’s lead runs, the magnets set for 6.8 TeV protons hold lead ions of \(82 \times 6.8 = 557.6\) TeV, which is \(557.6/208 = 2.68\) TeV per nucleon.
  • What is fundamental. The electron, muon, neutrinos and photon are, as far as is known, points with no parts. The proton, neutron and pions are made of quarks, held by the strong force; this is what collisions at GeV and TeV energies probe (chapter 23).

Exercises

  1. A cube of copper has sides of 1 cm. How many atoms does it hold, and how far apart are they? Gold has a density of 19.3 g/cm\(^3\) and a molar mass of 196.97 g; how thick is a gold foil 1 μm thick, counted in atoms?
Answer

Copper: \(8.96/63.546 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 8.49 \times 10^{22}\) atoms, spaced \((8.49 \times 10^{28}\ \mathrm{m}^{-3})^{-1/3} = 0.228\) nm. Gold: \(n = 19.3/196.97 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 5.90 \times 10^{22}\) per cm\(^3\), spacing 0.257 nm, so 1 μm is about 3900 atoms thick. Geiger and Marsden’s foils were of this order, thin enough that most alphas met at most one close nucleus.

  1. In a Thomson experiment, a beam goes straight through crossed fields of 20 kV/m and 0.75 mT. With the electric field off, the beam curves on a radius of 20.2 cm. Find the speed, \(e/m\), and the voltage that accelerated the electrons.
Answer

\(v = \mathcal{E}/B = 2 \times 10^4/7.5 \times 10^{-4} = 2.67 \times 10^7\) m/s. \(e/m = v/(rB) = 2.67 \times 10^7/(0.202 \times 7.5 \times 10^{-4}) = 1.76 \times 10^{11}\) C/kg. From \(\tfrac12 mv^2 = eV\), \(V = v^2/(2e/m) = 2.02\) kV.

  1. An X-ray tube with a copper anode runs at 40 kV. What is the shortest wavelength it emits? Can it produce copper’s K\(\alpha\) line at 8.05 keV? Use Moseley’s rule to estimate the K\(\alpha\) energy of tungsten (\(Z = 74\)); the measured value is 59.3 keV. Why is the estimate low?
Answer

\(\lambda_{min} = 1239.84/40\,000\) nm \(= 31.0\) pm. Yes: electrons of 40 keV have far more than the 9 keV or so needed to empty copper’s innermost shell. Moseley: \(0.75 \times 13.6 \times 73^2\) eV \(= 54.4\) keV, 8 per cent low. The rule treats the inner electron as moving in the field of a charge \((Z - 1)e\) by Bohr’s non-relativistic formula; in tungsten that electron moves at about half the speed of light, and relativity raises its binding, as for uranium in section 7.

  1. Using Bohr’s formula, find the wavelength of the line emitted when hydrogen drops from \(n = 4\) to \(n = 2\). What is the longest wavelength that can ionise a hydrogen atom already in the level \(n = 2\)? What energy binds the electron of \(\mathrm{Li}^{2+}\)?
Answer

\(13.606 \times (1/4 - 1/16) = 2.551\) eV, so \(\lambda = 1239.84/2.551 = 486.0\) nm, the blue-green Balmer line (measured 486.1 nm in air). From \(n = 2\) the binding is \(13.606/4 = 3.40\) eV, so photons of \(\lambda < 1239.84/3.40 = 364.5\) nm ionise it. \(\mathrm{Li}^{2+}\) has \(Z = 3\): \(9 \times 13.606 = 122.5\) eV.

  1. Find the de Broglie wavelength of (a) an electron of 100 keV, using \(pc = \sqrt{W^2 + 2Wmc^2}\), and compare with the non-relativistic \(pc = \sqrt{2Wmc^2}\); (b) a 5.304 MeV alpha from polonium-210 (\(m_\alpha c^2 = 3727.4\) MeV); (c) a neutron of 0.025 eV, the energy of thermal motion at room temperature.
Answer
  1. \(pc = \sqrt{0.1^2 + 2 \times 0.1 \times 0.511} = 0.335\) MeV, \(\lambda = 1239.84\ \mathrm{MeV\,fm}/0.335\ \mathrm{MeV} = 3700\) fm \(= 3.70\) pm. Without relativity \(pc = 0.320\) MeV and \(\lambda = 3.88\) pm, 5 per cent too long: at 100 keV an electron already needs relativity. (b) \(pc = \sqrt{5.304^2 + 2 \times 5.304 \times 3727.4} = 198.9\) MeV, \(\lambda = 6.23\) fm, comparable with a nucleus. (c) \(pc = \sqrt{2 \times 939.57 \times 10^6 \times 0.025}\) eV \(= 6854\) eV, \(\lambda = 1239.84/6854 = 0.181\) nm, the spacing of atoms in a crystal: slow neutrons diffract from crystals.
  1. Use \(\Delta x\,\Delta p \sim \hbar\) to estimate the kinetic energy of a nucleon confined in a nucleus of radius 3 fm, and the momentum spread of a proton beam whose particles are confined, sideways, to 1 mm. Is the second a limit anyone needs to worry about?
Answer

Nucleon: \((\hbar c)^2/(2m_pc^2\Delta x^2) = 197.3^2/(2 \times 938.3 \times 9) = 2.3\) MeV; a fuller count, with the nucleons filling levels by the exclusion principle, gives their average energy as about 20 MeV, but the scale is MeV. Beam: \(\Delta p\,c \sim 197.3\ \mathrm{MeV\,fm}/10^{12}\ \mathrm{fm} = 2.0 \times 10^{-10}\) MeV, 0.2 meV\(/c\). Real beams have transverse momentum spreads of keV\(/c\) and more, millions of times larger, so the uncertainty principle sets no practical limit on beam quality; beams are classical (section 8).

  1. From the AME2020 masses \(m(^1\mathrm{H}) = 1.007\,825\) u, \(m_n = 1.008\,665\) u and \(M(^2\mathrm{H}) = 2.014\,102\) u, find the deuteron’s binding energy. What is the least photon energy that can split a deuteron? (Ignore the small recoil.)
Answer

\(1.007\,825 + 1.008\,665 - 2.014\,102 = 0.002\,388\) u, and \(0.002\,388 \times 931.494 = 2.22\) MeV. A photon of 2.22 MeV or more can split it, \(\gamma + d \to p + n\). The gammas of cobalt-60 (1.33 MeV at most) cannot; Chadwick and Maurice Goldhaber used the 2.6 MeV gammas of thorium C″ to do it in 1934, which weighed the neutron.

  1. The fusion reaction \(d + t \to \alpha + n\) uses deuterium and tritium. With atomic masses \(M(^2\mathrm{H}) = 2.014\,102\) u, \(M(^3\mathrm{H}) = 3.016\,049\) u, \(M(^4\mathrm{He}) = 4.002\,603\) u and \(m_n = 1.008\,665\) u, find \(Q\). For slow reactants, how is it shared between the alpha and the neutron?
Answer

\(2.014\,102 + 3.016\,049 - 4.002\,603 - 1.008\,665 = 0.018\,883\) u, \(Q = 17.59\) MeV. Momenta are equal and opposite, so \(W = p^2/2m\) shares the energy in inverse proportion to the masses: the neutron, of mass 1, takes \(\tfrac45 Q = 14.07\) MeV, the alpha, of mass 4, \(\tfrac15 Q = 3.52\) MeV. Small accelerators of a few hundred kV sending deuterons onto tritium targets are still used as neutron generators of 14 MeV.

  1. How many milligrams of polonium-210 make 1 Ci? If all its alphas could be collected, what current would they carry? How many curies of polonium would match a 1 μA proton beam in particles per second?
Answer

One milligram is 4.49 Ci (section 13), so 1 Ci is \(1/4.49 = 0.22\) mg. Its \(3.7 \times 10^{10}\) alphas a second, each of charge \(2e\), carry \(3.7 \times 10^{10} \times 3.204 \times 10^{-19} = 1.19 \times 10^{-8}\) A, 12 nA, but spread over all directions. A 1 μA proton beam is \(6.24 \times 10^{12}\) particles a second: \(6.24 \times 10^{12}/3.7 \times 10^{10} = 169\) Ci, 38 mg of polonium, in a sphere of directions rather than a beam. This is the gap the first accelerators closed.

    1. What is the shortest wavelength of a photon that can make an electron–positron pair near a nucleus? (b) A positron annihilates at rest with an electron. What are the energies, wavelengths and directions of the photons? (c) The LHC’s magnets hold 6.8 TeV protons. What energy per nucleon do they hold for fully stripped lead-208, and for a deuteron?
Answer
  1. \(E \geq 1.022\) MeV, so \(\lambda \leq 1239.84\ \mathrm{MeV\,fm}/1.022\ \mathrm{MeV} = 1213\) fm \(= 1.21\) pm. (b) Two photons of 0.511 MeV, 2.43 pm, in opposite directions, so that the total momentum stays zero. (c) A magnet bends by momentum per charge, so at high energy it holds charge \(Z\) at \(Z\) times the proton’s energy: lead, \(82 \times 6.8 = 557.6\) TeV, or 2.68 TeV per nucleon; the deuteron, charge 1 and two nucleons, 6.8 TeV, or 3.4 TeV per nucleon. Ions with fewer charges per nucleon get less energy per nucleon from the same magnets.
