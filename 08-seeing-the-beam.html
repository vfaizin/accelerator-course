In one page

The wall. A beam is invisible. Until about 1930 the main ways to know it were the charge it left on an electroscope, the heat it left in a thermojunction, and flashes counted by eye on a zinc sulphide screen at no more than about 90 a minute. Machines that made a microampere, \(6 \times 10^{12}\) particles a second, and later beams that circulated for hours and could not be stopped to be measured, needed instruments that read the beam without destroying it, and counters that kept up.

The idea. Everything is read from two effects. A beam carries a field, and the field drives an image current on the wall around it: a transformer, a wall-current monitor or a pair of electrodes reads that current, its time structure and its centre without touching the beam. And a particle that enters matter loses energy to its electrons: a cup collects its charge, a screen turns the energy into light, a gas or a crystal of silicon turns it into a counted pulse.

The equations to remember. Here \(\mathcal{E}\) is an electric field, as everywhere in the course, and \(E\) an energy (in section 12 only, the effective dose); \(\phi\) is an azimuth, not an RF phase.

\[ I = \dot{N} q, \qquad 1\ \mu\mathrm{A} = 6.24 \times 10^{12}\ \text{singly charged particles per second} \]

\[ \frac{\Delta U}{\Sigma U} = \frac{4\sin(\alpha/2)}{\alpha}\,\frac{x}{a} \approx \frac{2x}{a} \]

for two electrodes of angular width \(\alpha\) in a round pipe of radius \(a\) (m), with the beam at small displacement \(x\) (m).

\[ \sigma_x^2 = \varepsilon\,\beta_x + (D_x\,\delta)^2 \]

the measured rms width \(\sigma_x\) (m) from emittance \(\varepsilon\) (m rad), optics \(\beta_x\) (m), dispersion \(D_x\) (m) and momentum spread \(\delta\).

\[ -\frac{dW}{\rho_m\,dx} = K z^2 \frac{Z}{A}\frac{1}{\beta^2}\left[\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{2 m_e c^2 \beta^2\gamma^2 W_{max}}{I_e^2} - \beta^2\right] \]

the Bethe formula for the mean energy loss per unit mass thickness \(\rho_m\,dx\) (\(\rho_m\) the density of the material), with \(K = 0.307\) MeV cm\(^2\)/mol and \(I_e\) the mean excitation energy of the material.

\[ H = H_0\,10^{-d/\mathrm{TVL}} \]

Numbers to know. One microampere times one megavolt is one watt in the cup. Cups work only where the beam stops within about a centimetre, below about 100 MeV per nucleon. Transformers resolve about a microampere, a good cup 10 pA. Position monitors read to about 100 µm bunch by bunch and to about 1 µm averaged, in electron machines. A 150 MeV proton travels 15.8 cm in water and deposits most densely at the end; a 5.5 MeV alpha travels 4.2 cm in air. A minimum-ionising particle frees about 23,000 electrons in 300 µm of silicon. Natural background is about 2.4 mSv a year; the recommended limit for radiation workers is 20 mSv a year. A tenth-value layer of concrete is about 0.5 m for high-energy bremsstrahlung and about 1.15 m for high-energy neutrons.

The wall

A beam of charged particles in a vacuum gives no light of its own (except electrons bent hard enough, much later; chapter 20). What can be observed is what the beam does: the charge it carries, the heat it leaves, the light, ions and chemical changes it makes in matter it strikes, and the field it carries with it.

The first measurements used charge. In 1895 Jean Perrin in Paris let cathode rays into a metal cup connected to an electroscope, and the cup charged negatively (chapter 2). In 1897 J. J. Thomson let the rays fall through a slit into an insulated cylinder on an electrometer, which gave the total charge, with a thermojunction behind the slit to give the total energy. The cup with an electrometer, and the calorimeter, are still the absolute standards of beam current and beam power.

For single particles there was the eye. On 15 May 1903 William Crookes’s spinthariscope was first shown in public, at a Royal Society soirée in London: a speck of radium salt before a zinc sulphide screen and a lens, in which what looked like a steady glow broke up into separate flashes, one for each alpha. For twenty-five years nuclear physics counted these flashes. The observer sat in the dark about 30 minutes until the eye could see them, counted for about a minute and handed over; Geiger and Marsden put the limit of certain counting at 90 a minute (chapter 1). Counts drifted as observers tired, and in the 1920s the laboratories in Vienna and Cambridge disagreed about disintegrations that one saw and the other did not.

Electrical counting began in Manchester. In 1908 Rutherford and Geiger let alphas enter a gas-filled tube with a wire along its axis and a high voltage between wire and tube; the ion pairs each alpha made multiplied in the strong field near the wire into a pulse large enough to kick an electrometer. It counted alphas one by one. In 1928 at Kiel, Geiger and his student Walther Müller made a tube in which any ionising particle set off a discharge, so that electrons and gamma rays were counted as well as alphas. Yet a counter is only as fast as its recorder: in 1932, the year accelerators began to split nuclei, Cockcroft and Walton counted lithium’s alphas at first by eye on a screen, and within weeks electrically as well (chapter 5); in the same year C. E. Wynn-Williams at the Cavendish published a thyratron “scale of two” circuit that divided a count rate by two at each step so that a mechanical register could follow it.

The machines then posed problems that neither cups nor counters solved. A cup must stop the beam, and a cup that stops a beam cannot measure a beam that is meant to go on, or one that circulates in a ring. A cup works only if the beam stops inside it: above about 100 MeV per nucleon a proton runs centimetres into copper and its nuclear reactions throw charge out again. A beam of a hundred microamperes at a few hundred MeV deposits tens of kilowatts in whatever it hits. And beams came to need more than a current: a position to a fraction of a millimetre, a size, an emittance, an energy, a bunch length, a tune. Each of these had to be read from outside the beam, through its field, or from a sample so thin that the beam hardly noticed.

The physics

Pre-requisite (continued): Lorentz transformations, time dilation, length contraction

Pre-requisite. This continues Lorentz transformations, time dilation, length contraction in chapter 1, which gave the factor \(\gamma\), inertial frames, time dilation and length contraction. It adds how electric and magnetic fields change from one frame to another, and uses that to find the field of a charge moving at constant velocity: a disc flattened by \(\gamma\), whose transverse field is \(\gamma\) times the static one and lasts a time of about \(b/(\gamma v)\). Section 1 starts from this field and section 2 uses its time integral. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 2, §24 (Lorentz transformation of the field) and §38 (the field of a uniformly moving charge), and Tong, Electromagnetism, §5.3 (the electromagnetic tensor and its worked examples).

The idea. Charge is the same in every frame: every observer who counts the electrons on a plate gets the same number. Lengths along the motion are not the same. So a charged plate seen moving along its own surface carries more charge per square metre than it does at rest, and its field is stronger. That fact and Gauss’s law give the rule for how fields transform.

Two capacitors. Take a parallel-plate capacitor at rest in a frame \(K'\), with surface charges \(\pm\sigma'\) (C/m\(^2\)) on its plates. By Gauss’s law the field between the plates is \(\sigma'/\varepsilon_0\), perpendicular to them. Look at it from the laboratory frame \(K\), in which \(K'\) moves with speed \(v\) along \(x\).

  • Plates parallel to the motion, so that the field is along \(y\), perpendicular to \(\mathbf{v}\). Each plate is shortened along \(x\) by the factor \(\gamma\); its width along \(z\) is unchanged; its charge is unchanged. So the surface charge is \(\sigma = \gamma\sigma'\), and the field is \(\gamma\) times larger.
  • Plates perpendicular to the motion, so that the field is along \(x\), parallel to \(\mathbf{v}\). The plates lie in the \(yz\) plane and are not contracted. The gap is contracted, but the field of a charged sheet does not depend on the distance from it. So \(\sigma = \sigma'\) and the field is unchanged.

In the first case the moving plates are also sheets of current, with surface current density \(K_s = \sigma v\) (A/m) running in opposite directions on the two plates. Between two opposite current sheets Ampère’s law gives a magnetic field \(\mu_0K_s = \mu_0\sigma v\), perpendicular to both \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) (the direction a cross product \(\mathbf{v}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) points). Since \(\mu_0 = 1/(\varepsilon_0c^2)\), this is \(v\mathcal{E}_\perp/c^2\). Together, for a field that is purely electric in its own rest frame (\(\mathbf{B}' = 0\)):

\[ \mathcal{E}_\parallel = \mathcal{E}'_\parallel, \qquad \mathcal{E}_\perp = \gamma \mathcal{E}'_\perp, \qquad \mathbf{B} = \frac{\mathbf{v}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}{c^2}. \]

Landau and Lifshitz (§24) derive the general rule, for any \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}'\) and \(\mathbf{B}'\), from the transformation of the field tensor; with \(\mathbf{B}' = 0\) it reduces to these three statements.

The field of a moving charge. Put a charge \(q\) at rest at the origin of \(K'\), and let \(K'\) move with speed \(v\) along \(x\). An observer at rest in the laboratory sits at a perpendicular distance \(b\) (m) from the line of motion, at \(x = 0\), \(y = b\), and the charge passes \(x = 0\) at \(t = 0\). In \(K'\) the observer moves; at laboratory time \(t\) his coordinates in \(K'\) are \(x' = \gamma(x - vt) = -\gamma vt\) and \(y' = b\). Coulomb’s law in \(K'\) gives there

\[ \mathcal{E}'_y = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{b}{\left(b^2 + \gamma^2v^2t^2\right)^{3/2}}, \qquad \mathcal{E}'_x = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{-\gamma vt}{\left(b^2 + \gamma^2v^2t^2\right)^{3/2}}. \]

Transforming with \(\mathcal{E}_\perp = \gamma \mathcal{E}'_y\) and \(\mathcal{E}_\parallel = \mathcal{E}'_x\):

\[ \mathcal{E}_\perp(t) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\gamma b}{\left(b^2 + \gamma^2v^2t^2\right)^{3/2}}, \qquad \mathcal{E}_\parallel(t) = -\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\gamma vt}{\left(b^2 + \gamma^2v^2t^2\right)^{3/2}}. \]

The first is the formula section 1 starts from. Four things can be read off it.

  • The peak. At \(t = 0\), \(\mathcal{E}_\perp = \gamma q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0b^2)\): \(\gamma\) times the static Coulomb field at the same distance.
  • The duration. Write \(u = \gamma vt/b\). Then \(\mathcal{E}_\perp\) is proportional to \((1 + u^2)^{-3/2}\), which falls to half its peak where \((1 + u^2)^{3/2} = 2\), at \(u = \pm0.766\). The full width at half maximum is \(1.53\,b/(\gamma v)\): the pulse gets shorter as \(\gamma\) grows.
  • The time integral. With \(dt = b\,du/(\gamma v)\),

\[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}_\perp\,dt = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\gamma b}{b^3}\,\frac{b}{\gamma v}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{du}{(1 + u^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{2q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,bv}, \]

since the last integral is 2. To see it, substitute \(u = \tan\chi\): then \(du = d\chi/\cos^2\chi\) and \(1 + u^2 = 1/\cos^2\chi\), so \((1 + u^2)^{-3/2}\,du = \cos^3\chi\,d\chi/\cos^2\chi = \cos\chi\,d\chi\), and as \(u\) runs from \(-\infty\) to \(\infty\), \(\chi\) runs from \(-\pi/2\) to \(\pi/2\); the integral of \(\cos\chi\) over that range is \(\sin(\pi/2) - \sin(-\pi/2) = 2\). The height grows as \(\gamma\) and the width shrinks as \(1/\gamma\), and \(\gamma\) cancels. This is the integral section 2 multiplies by the electron’s charge to get Bohr’s impulse. - The longitudinal part is odd in \(t\). It pushes one way while the charge approaches and the other way after it has passed, and its time integral is zero.

The flattened disc. The same calculation, done for every point at one instant, gives the field at distance \(r\) from the charge’s present position and at angle \(\psi\) to its velocity (Landau and Lifshitz, §38):

\[ \mathcal{E} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\,\frac{1 - \beta^2}{\left(1 - \beta^2\sin^2\psi\right)^{3/2}}, \]

pointing straight away from the present position. At \(\psi = 90°\) this is \(\gamma\) times the Coulomb field; along the line of motion, \(\psi = 0\), it is \(1/\gamma^2\) times. For large \(\gamma\), write \(\psi = 90° - \chi\) with \(\chi\) small, so that \(\sin\psi = \cos\chi\). By Taylor’s series, \(\cos^2\chi \approx 1 - \chi^2\), so \(1 - \beta^2\cos^2\chi \approx (1 - \beta^2) + \beta^2\chi^2\); and since \(\beta^2\) is close to 1 for large \(\gamma\), this is \((1 - \beta^2) + \chi^2 = \gamma^{-2}(1 + \gamma^2\chi^2)\). The numerator \(1 - \beta^2\) is \(\gamma^{-2}\), and \(\gamma^{-2}/\gamma^{-3} = \gamma\), so

\[ \mathcal{E} \approx \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\,\frac{\gamma}{\left(1 + \gamma^2\chi^2\right)^{3/2}}, \]

which falls to half at \(\chi = \pm0.766/\gamma\). The field lives in a disc of angular half-thickness about \(1/\gamma\) around the plane through the charge perpendicular to its motion. The magnetic field circles the line of motion with \(cB = \beta \mathcal{E}_\perp\).

At work. Take \(b = 25\) mm, a typical beam-pipe radius.

Protons at 150 MeV 0.5066 1.160 142 ps 218 ps
Protons at 1 GeV 0.8750 2.066 46 ps 71 ps

For the slow ions the “disc” is hardly flattened at all (\(\gamma^3 = 1.005\)), and the field at the wall of a single ion lasts more than 2 ns whatever the bunch is doing. This is the quantitative form of the last paragraph of section 1: a pick-up cannot show bunch structure finer than this. For the 1 GeV electron the disc has a half-thickness of \(0.766/\gamma = 0.39\) mrad, and the field along the line of motion is \(\gamma^{-2} = 2.6 \times 10^{-7}\) of Coulomb’s. A single proton makes a static field of only 2.3 µV/m at 25 mm, so the signals of section 4 and 5 come from the \(10^9\) to \(10^{11}\) charges of a bunch acting together.

Exercise A1. A 150 MeV proton passes 25 mm from an electrode. Find \(\beta\), \(\gamma\), the ratio of the peak transverse field to the static one, and the full width at half maximum of the pulse.

Answer

\(\gamma = 1 + 150/938.27 = 1.160\), \(\beta = \sqrt{1 - 1/\gamma^2} = 0.5066\). The peak is \(\gamma = 1.16\) times the static field. The width is \(1.533\,b/(\gamma\beta c) = 1.533 \times 0.025/(1.160 \times 0.5066 \times 2.998 \times 10^8) = 218\) ps.

Exercise A2. The same proton passes a free electron at \(b = 1\) nm. Using the time integral, find the transverse impulse the electron receives and the energy it takes.

Answer

\(\Delta p = e\int \mathcal{E}_\perp dt = 2e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0bv)\). With \(e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) = 2.307 \times 10^{-28}\) J m and \(v = 1.519 \times 10^8\) m/s: \(\Delta p = 2 \times 2.307 \times 10^{-28}/(10^{-9} \times 1.519 \times 10^8) = 3.04 \times 10^{-27}\) kg m/s. The energy is \(\Delta p^2/(2m_e) = 5.07 \times 10^{-24}\) J \(= 32\) µeV. Section 2 adds up such tiny transfers over all the electrons in a slab.

Exercise A3. For a 1 GeV electron, how thick is the field disc, and how strong is the field straight ahead compared with Coulomb’s?

Answer

\(\gamma = 1958\). Half-thickness \(0.766/\gamma = 0.39\) mrad. Straight ahead (\(\psi = 0\)) the field is \((1 - \beta^2) = 1/\gamma^2 = 2.6 \times 10^{-7}\) of the Coulomb field. In practice nothing reaches a pick-up ahead of the bunch.

Pre-requisite (continued): Images and two-dimensional complex potentials

Pre-requisite. This continues Images and two-dimensional complex potentials in chapter 4, which gave the method of images for a charge near a conducting plane and near a sphere. It adds the two-dimensional case a beam pipe needs: a line of charge inside a grounded round pipe, its image, the charge it draws onto the wall (the formula of section 1), and why a static, two-dimensional calculation is right for a fast beam. Sections 1 and 5 use all of it. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, §2.1.3 (the line charge) and §2.4.3 (the method of images), and Chao and Tigner, Handbook, §7.4.2 (beam position monitors, where positions are computed from two-dimensional electrostatics); it uses the field transformation of the section above.

A line of charge. A long straight line carries charge \(\lambda\) per metre (C/m). Gauss’s law on a coaxial cylinder of radius \(s\) and length \(l\) gives \(\mathcal{E}\cdot2\pi sl = \lambda l/\varepsilon_0\), so

\[ \mathcal{E} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0s}, \qquad V = -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln s + \text{constant}, \]

the potential \(V\) following from \(\mathcal{E} = -dV/ds\). This is the field inside a coaxial line from chapter 1, now for a line that need not sit on the axis.

The image in a cylinder. A line charge \(\lambda\) runs parallel to the axis of a grounded round pipe of radius \(a\), at distance \(r\) from the axis and azimuth \(\theta\). Guess an image: a line of charge \(-\lambda\) on the same radial line, outside the pipe, at distance \(r_{im} = a^2/r\) from the axis. Take a point on the wall at azimuth \(\phi\), the angle of section 1. Its squared distances from the beam and from the image are, by the cosine rule,

\[ s_1^2 = a^2 + r^2 - 2ar\cos(\phi - \theta), \qquad s_2^2 = a^2 + \frac{a^4}{r^2} - \frac{2a^3}{r}\cos(\phi - \theta) = \frac{a^2}{r^2}\,s_1^2 . \]

So \(s_2 = (a/r)\,s_1\) at every point of the wall. The potential of the two lines there is

\[ -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\ln s_1 - \ln s_2\right) = -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{r}{a}, \]

the same all round. Adding a constant changes no field, so subtract this value and the wall is at zero: grounded. The pair satisfies Laplace’s equation inside the pipe (the image is outside), has the right charge inside, and has the right value on the boundary; by the uniqueness theorem it is the field inside the pipe. It is the two-dimensional version of chapter 4’s sphere, where the image sat at \(R^2/D_q\). As the beam moves to the centre, \(r \to 0\), the image runs off to infinity and the field becomes the plain coaxial one.

The charge on the wall. Just outside a conductor’s surface (here, in the vacuum just inside the pipe) the field is normal to it, and the surface carries charge \(\varepsilon_0\) times the field leaving it (chapter 1). Here the field points outwards, into the metal, so the induced charge is negative: \(\sigma = -\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}_r\) at \(r = a\). Each line contributes a radial component equal to its field strength times the cosine of the angle between its own direction and the radius. For the beam that is \((\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0)(a - r\cos(\phi - \theta))/s_1^2\); for the image, with the opposite sign, \((\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0)(a - r_{im}\cos(\phi - \theta))/s_2^2\). Using \(s_2^2 = (a^2/r^2)s_1^2\) and \(r_{im} = a^2/r\), the second term is \((\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0)(r^2/a - r\cos(\phi - \theta))/s_1^2\). Subtracting, the cosine terms cancel:

\[ \mathcal{E}_r(a, \phi) = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{a - r^2/a}{s_1^2} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 a}\,\frac{a^2 - r^2}{a^2 + r^2 - 2ar\cos(\phi - \theta)}, \]

\[ \sigma(\phi) = -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi a}\,\frac{a^2 - r^2}{a^2 + r^2 - 2ar\cos(\phi - \theta)} . \]

The fraction is called the Poisson kernel. Its average round the circle is exactly 1 (checked numerically), so the total induced charge per metre of pipe is \(-\lambda\), as Gauss’s law demands: a surface just inside the metal encloses no net charge. With the beam centred the wall charge is uniform, \(-\lambda/(2\pi a)\). With the beam off centre it crowds onto the near side.

From charge to current. The beam’s charge moves at speed \(v\), so the beam current is \(I_{beam} = \lambda v\). The wall charge follows the beam along the pipe, so the wall carries a surface current \(\sigma v\) per metre of circumference:

\[ j_{im}(\phi) = \sigma v = -\frac{I_{beam}}{2\pi a}\,\frac{a^2 - r^2}{a^2 + r^2 - 2ar\cos(\phi - \theta)}. \]

This is section 1’s formula. The chapter writes it without the minus sign and says in words that the image current flows opposite to the beam. Expanding to first order in \(r/a\) gives \(1 + (2r/a)\cos(\phi - \theta)\) for the kernel, which is where section 5 starts.

Why statics in two dimensions is right. A bunch is a moving, three-dimensional charge, and the wall’s charge has to flow along the pipe to keep up with it. Two steps show when the static, two-dimensional answer still holds.

  1. Go to the frame moving with the bunch. There the bunch is at rest and, by length contraction run backwards, \(\gamma\) times longer than in the laboratory: a bunch of rms length \(\sigma_z\) becomes \(\gamma\sigma_z\) long. If \(\gamma\sigma_z \gg a\), then near any slice the bunch looks like an endless line, and the static two-dimensional field above is right in that frame. (A smooth round pipe looks the same at every \(z\), so the fact that it slides past in this frame does not change the problem.)
  2. Come back to the laboratory. The transverse field grows by \(\gamma\) (the section above), and so does the charge per metre, by length contraction. The two factors cancel in the formula: written with the laboratory’s \(\lambda\), the field of each slice is the same two-dimensional field.

So the condition is \(\sigma_z \gg a/\gamma\). For \(a = 25\) mm, \(a/\gamma\) is 25.0 mm for ions at 1.4 MeV per nucleon, 21.6 mm for 150 MeV protons, 12.1 mm for 1 GeV protons and 13 µm for 1 GeV electrons. A 1 GeV proton bunch tens of centimetres long satisfies it easily. An ion bunch at 1.4 MeV per nucleon with \(\sigma_t = 0.3\) ns is \(\sigma_z = \beta c\sigma_t = 4.9\) mm long, five times shorter than \(a/\gamma\): the condition fails, and the wall sees a smoothed version of the bunch, as section 1 says.

At work. In a pipe of radius 25 mm with the beam 5 mm off centre, the image sits 125 mm from the axis; the wall current density on the near side is \((a + r)/(a - r) = 1.5\) times the centred value, and on the far side \((a - r)/(a + r) = 0.67\) times: a ratio of 2.25 across the pipe. For a centred 1 A beam the density is \(1/(2\pi \times 0.025) = 6.4\) A per metre of circumference everywhere.

1. The field of a moving charge, and the image current

A charge \(q\) moving at speed \(v = \beta c\) carries an electric field that, in the laboratory, is squeezed into a thin disc perpendicular to its motion. At a perpendicular distance \(b\) the transverse field is (Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 2, §38)

\[ \mathcal{E}_\perp(t) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\gamma b}{\left(b^2 + \gamma^2 v^2 t^2\right)^{3/2}}, \]

with \(t = 0\) at closest approach. Its peak is \(\gamma\) times the static field and its duration about \(b/(\gamma v)\), so the time integral is independent of \(\gamma\):

\[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \mathcal{E}_\perp\,dt = \frac{2q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\, b v}. \]

This integral, multiplied by the electron’s charge, is the impulse that the particle gives to a free electron at distance \(b\) and is the start of the Bethe formula (section 2).

Inside a conducting pipe the field lines end on the wall, where they gather a negative image charge that travels with the beam. For an ultrarelativistic beam the disc is thin and the image current on the wall has the same time structure as the beam current, with opposite sign. For a thin beam at radius \(r\) and azimuth \(\theta\) in a round pipe of radius \(a\), the image current per unit length of circumference is the two-dimensional electrostatic solution for a line charge inside a grounded cylinder (Forck, section 5.4):

\[ j_{im}(\phi) = \frac{I_{beam}}{2\pi a}\,\frac{a^2 - r^2}{a^2 + r^2 - 2ar\cos(\phi - \theta)}, \]

where \(j_{im}\) is in A/m of circumference, \(I_{beam}\) the beam current (A) and \(\phi\) the azimuth on the wall. It integrates to \(I_{beam}\) round the wall. When the beam is off centre the current crowds onto the near side. These two facts, that the wall carries the beam’s current and that its distribution tells where the beam is, underlie the transformer, the wall-current monitor and the position monitor. The same image currents, flowing through every change of pipe cross-section, make the wakefields of chapter 22.

The approximation fails for slow beams. When \(\gamma\) is close to 1 the disc is not thin: the field of a bunch reaches a pick-up before the bunch does and after it has gone, so a pick-up blurs a short bunch. At \(\beta = 0.055\) (1.4 MeV per nucleon) a pick-up cannot resolve bunch structure of a fraction of a nanosecond (Forck, section 6.7).

Pre-requisite (continued): Four-momentum, energy-momentum relation, invariant mass

Pre-requisite. This continues Four-momentum, energy-momentum relation, invariant mass in chapter 1, which gave the four-momentum, its invariant square \(s\), the centre-of-mass frame and the threshold condition. It adds the elastic collision of a fast particle with an electron at rest: the four-momentum transfer, its invariant, and from them the largest energy \(W_{max}\) and the largest momentum the electron can receive. Section 2 puts \(W_{max}\) inside the logarithm of the Bethe formula and uses the largest momentum transfer for the cut-off \(b_{min}\). Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 2, §13 (elastic collisions of particles), with the non-relativistic limit from vol. 1, §17.

The idea. In the centre-of-mass frame an elastic collision (one that keeps the total kinetic energy, F3, section 6) only turns the two momenta: each keeps its size, and the energies do not change. The most the electron can get is when its momentum is turned right round. Measure that in an invariant way, and carry it back to the laboratory.

The set-up. A particle of mass \(m\), energy \(E = \gamma mc^2\) and momentum \(p = \gamma\beta mc\) hits an electron at rest. Four-momenta are written \(P = (E/c, \mathbf{p})\), with the invariant square \(P\cdot P = E^2/c^2 - \mathbf{p}^2 = m^2c^2\). The electron’s is \(P_e = (m_ec, \mathbf{0})\) before and \(P'_e = (E'_e/c, \mathbf{p}'_e)\) after, where \(E'_e = m_ec^2 + W_e\) and \(W_e\) is the kinetic energy it receives.

The momentum transfer. Define \(\Delta = P'_e - P_e\), the four-momentum given to the electron. Its square is

\[ \Delta\cdot\Delta = P'_e\cdot P'_e + P_e\cdot P_e - 2P'_e\cdot P_e = 2m_e^2c^2 - 2m_eE'_e = -2m_eW_e , \]

where the dot product \(P'_e\cdot P_e = (E'_e/c)(m_ec) - \mathbf{p}'_e\cdot\mathbf{0} = m_eE'_e\) was worked out in the laboratory, where the electron started at rest. So

\[ W_e = -\frac{\Delta\cdot\Delta}{2m_e}. \]

The left side is a laboratory energy; the right side is an invariant, the same in every frame. So it can be worked out in the centre-of-mass frame.

In the centre-of-mass frame. Both particles have momenta of the same size \(p_{cm}\), opposite. The electron’s momentum is turned through an angle \(\theta_{cm}\) and its energy is unchanged, so \(\Delta\) has no time part, and its space part has size \(2p_{cm}\sin(\theta_{cm}/2)\). Hence \(\Delta\cdot\Delta = -4p_{cm}^2\sin^2(\theta_{cm}/2)\), largest in size for a head-on reversal, \(\theta_{cm} = \pi\):

\[ W_{max} = \frac{2p_{cm}^2}{m_e}. \]

Finding \(p_{cm}\). In the laboratory the electron has no momentum; the centre-of-mass frame moves at \(\beta_{cm} = pc/(E + m_ec^2)\) with \(\gamma_{cm} = (E + m_ec^2)/(\sqrt{s}\,c)\), where \(s = (P + P_e)\cdot(P + P_e)\) is chapter 1’s invariant and \(\sqrt{s}\,c\) is the total energy in the centre-of-mass frame. Seen from that frame the electron moves backwards with momentum \(p_{cm} = \gamma_{cm}\beta_{cm}m_ec = p\,m_ec/\sqrt{s}\). And

\[ s = m^2c^2 + m_e^2c^2 + 2m_eE = m^2c^2\left[1 + \frac{2\gamma m_e}{m} + \left(\frac{m_e}{m}\right)^2\right]. \]

Putting \(p_{cm}^2 = p^2m_e^2c^2/s\) and \(p = \gamma\beta mc\) into \(W_{max} = 2p_{cm}^2/m_e\):

\[ W_{max} = \frac{2m_ec^2\beta^2\gamma^2}{1 + 2\gamma m_e/m + (m_e/m)^2}, \]

section 2’s formula.

Limits. For a heavy particle, \(2\gamma m_e/m \ll 1\) and \(W_{max} \approx 2m_ec^2\beta^2\gamma^2\). If it is also slow, this is \(2m_ev^2 = \tfrac12m_e(2v)^2\): the electron bounces off a heavy body moving at \(v\) and leaves at \(2v\), as a ball bounces off an oncoming wall (Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 1, §17, give the largest fraction transferred as \(4mm_e/(m + m_e)^2\) of the energy, which is the same thing). The largest momentum transfer is \(2p_{cm} \approx 2\gamma m_ev\), and by the uncertainty relation the impact parameter cannot be resolved below \(\hbar\) over it: \(b_{min} \approx \hbar/(2\gamma m_ev)\), section 2’s quantum cut-off.

Pre-requisite (continued): Driven oscillator, resonance and quality factor

Pre-requisite. This continues Driven oscillator, resonance and quality factor in chapter 7, which showed how a force in step with an oscillator’s motion feeds it energy cycle after cycle. It adds the opposite case: a single pulse of force acting on an oscillator at rest. It gives an exact formula for the energy the oscillator takes, and shows that a pulse much shorter than the oscillator’s period acts as on a free particle, while one much longer gives almost nothing. Section 2 uses this for the outer cut-off \(b_{max}\) of the stopping formula. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 1, §22 (forced oscillations, including the energy transferred by a force that acts for a finite time).

The idea. An electron bound in an atom behaves, for this purpose, like a mass on a spring with some natural angular frequency \(\omega_0\) (rad/s). A passing particle gives it a push lasting a time of order \(b/(\gamma v)\). If the push is over before the spring has had time to act, the electron moves as if free. If the push is slow, the electron is displaced gently, follows the force, and settles back when the force goes: nothing is left over. The dividing line is a push lasting about \(1/\omega_0\).

The exact energy. The electron, of mass \(m\), obeys

\[ m\ddot{x} + m\omega_0^2x = F(t), \]

and is at rest before the pulse. Without the force this is simple harmonic motion, whose energy \(\tfrac12m\dot{x}^2 + \tfrac12m\omega_0^2x^2\) stays constant. Combine position and velocity into one complex number, \(\xi = \dot{x} + i\omega_0x\). Then

\[ \dot\xi = \ddot{x} + i\omega_0\dot{x} = \frac{F}{m} - \omega_0^2x + i\omega_0\dot{x} = \frac{F}{m} + i\omega_0\left(\dot{x} + i\omega_0x\right) = \frac{F}{m} + i\omega_0\xi . \]

Multiply by \(e^{-i\omega_0t}\). By the product rule, \(d(\xi e^{-i\omega_0t})/dt = (\dot\xi - i\omega_0\xi)e^{-i\omega_0t}\), which by the line above is \((F/m)e^{-i\omega_0t}\). Integrating from before the pulse, when \(\xi = 0\), up to time \(t\),

\[ \xi(t)\,e^{-i\omega_0t} = \frac{1}{m}\int_{-\infty}^{t}F(t')\,e^{-i\omega_0t'}\,dt'. \]

The oscillator’s energy is \(\tfrac12m(\dot{x}^2 + \omega_0^2x^2) = \tfrac12m|\xi|^2\) (the squared size of a complex number is the sum of the squares of its real and imaginary parts), and \(|e^{-i\omega_0t}| = 1\) by Euler’s formula. After the pulse, therefore,

\[ \Delta W = \frac{1}{2m}\left|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(t)\,e^{-i\omega_0t}\,dt\right|^2 . \]

This is exact for a linear oscillator, whatever the shape of the pulse.

The two limits. If the pulse lasts much less than \(1/\omega_0\), the exponential is close to 1 throughout it, and \(\Delta W = (\int F\,dt)^2/(2m)\): the impulse squared over twice the mass, exactly as for a free particle. This is Bohr’s free-electron estimate. If the pulse lasts much longer than \(1/\omega_0\), the exponential turns through many cycles while \(F\) hardly changes; positive and negative contributions cancel, and for a smooth pulse \(\Delta W\) falls faster than any power of the duration. The oscillator has followed the force and been left as it was: the response is adiabatic.

The passing charge. For the field of the section above, \(F = e\mathcal{E}_\perp(t)\) with a width of order \(b/(\gamma v)\). The integral can be done in closed form; the result is the free-electron energy multiplied by \((\xi_bK_1(\xi_b))^2\), where \(\xi_b = \omega_0b/(\gamma v)\) is the ratio of the collision time to \(1/\omega_0\) and \(K_1\) is a standard function (a modified Bessel function, tabulated like the sine; only the values in the table below are needed here). Its values, checked against a direct numerical integration of the formula above:

\(\xi_b = \omega_0b/(\gamma v)\) 0.1 0.5 1 2 3 5
Fraction of the free-electron energy 0.971 0.686 0.362 0.078 0.015 0.0004

The fraction is close to 1 while \(\xi_b\) is small, half at \(\xi_b = 0.76\), and below 1 per cent beyond \(\xi_b = 3.2\). So, to logarithmic accuracy, an electron at impact parameter \(b\) takes energy as if free when \(b < \gamma v/\omega_0\) and essentially none beyond:

\[ b_{max} \approx \frac{\gamma v}{\bar\omega}, \]

with \(\bar\omega\) a suitable average of the atom’s frequencies; \(\hbar\bar\omega\) is close to the mean excitation energy \(I_e\). The factor \(\gamma\) is why the logarithm in the stopping formula keeps rising slowly at high energy: the field’s pulse gets shorter, so electrons farther away are reached suddenly enough to take energy.

At work. For a 150 MeV proton in water, take \(\hbar\bar\omega = I_e = 75\) eV, so \(\bar\omega = 1.14 \times 10^{17}\) rad/s. With \(\gamma v = 1.160 \times 1.519 \times 10^8 = 1.76 \times 10^8\) m/s, \(b_{max} = 1.55\) nm, a few atomic diameters. The inner cut-off from the section above is \(b_{min} = 0.33\) pm. Their ratio is 4700 and its logarithm 8.46, the same as \(\ln(2\gamma^2m_ev^2/\hbar\bar\omega)\) in section 2. The full bracket of the Bethe formula, which includes the \(-\beta^2\) term and the exact quantum treatment, is 8.20: the classical picture with these two cut-offs gets the logarithm right to 3 per cent. For a 5.5 MeV alpha in air (\(I_e = 85.7\) eV) \(b_{max}\) is only 0.13 nm: the slow alpha’s pulse is long, and only the nearest electrons are struck suddenly.

2. Energy loss in matter: the Bethe formula

Every intercepting instrument and every particle detector depends on how a fast charged particle loses energy in matter. Almost all of the loss, for a proton or an ion, goes to the electrons of the material, in many small collisions.

The classical estimate (Bohr, 1913). Niels Bohr’s paper of 1913 on the slowing of charged particles gave this argument. A particle of charge \(ze\) passes a free electron at impact parameter \(b\) (m). By the integral above, the electron receives a transverse impulse

\[ \Delta p = \frac{2 z e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\, b v} \]

and so an energy \(\Delta W(b) = \Delta p^2/2m_e\). Count the electrons in a thin shell: in a path \(dx\) (m), the electrons at impact parameters between \(b\) and \(b + db\) fill a ring of radius \(b\), width \(db\) and area \(2\pi b\,db\) (F2, section 8), drawn out to length \(dx\); there are \(n_e\,2\pi b\,db\,dx\) of them, with \(n_e\) the electron density (m\(^{-3}\)). Each takes

\[ \frac{\Delta p^2}{2m_e} = \frac{1}{2m_e}\left(\frac{2ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0bv}\right)^2 = \frac{2z^2}{m_ev^2b^2}\left(\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\right)^2, \]

so the ring takes this times \(n_e\,2\pi b\,db\,dx\), which is proportional to \(db/b\). Adding the rings, with \(\int db/b = \ln(b_{max}/b_{min})\) (F1, section 7), the energy lost per unit path is

\[ -\frac{dW}{dx} = \int_{b_{min}}^{b_{max}} \frac{\Delta p^2}{2 m_e}\, n_e\,2\pi b\,db = \frac{4\pi n_e z^2}{m_e v^2}\left(\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\right)^2 \ln\frac{b_{max}}{b_{min}}. \]

The integral diverges at both ends, and each end is cut off by physics. At large \(b\) the collision lasts \(b/(\gamma v)\); if that is longer than the time \(1/\bar\omega\) characteristic of the electron’s motion in its atom (\(\bar\omega\) a mean angular frequency of that motion, rad/s), the electron follows the field adiabatically and gains nothing, so \(b_{max} \approx \gamma v/\bar\omega\). At small \(b\) the impulse approximation fails: classically when the transfer reaches its maximum, quantum mechanically when \(b\) falls below \(\hbar\) divided by the largest momentum the electron can receive, \(2\gamma m_e v\), so \(b_{min} \approx \hbar/(2\gamma m_e v)\). With the quantum cut-off the logarithm becomes \(\ln(2\gamma^2 m_e v^2/\hbar\bar\omega)\), and \(\hbar\bar\omega\) becomes a mean excitation energy of the atom.

The Bethe formula. Bethe did the calculation in quantum mechanics, in the Born approximation (which treats the passing particle’s pull on the atom as a small disturbance, valid when the particle is fast compared with the atom’s electrons), in 1930, and extended it to relativistic speeds in 1932. The form used today (Particle Data Group, Passage of particles through matter, eq. 34.5; the derivation is in Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 3, §149 and §150) is, as a mass stopping power,

\[ -\frac{dW}{\rho_m\,dx} = K z^2 \frac{Z}{A}\frac{1}{\beta^2}\left[\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{2 m_e c^2 \beta^2 \gamma^2 W_{max}}{I_e^2} - \beta^2 - \frac{\delta(\beta\gamma)}{2}\right] \]

with:

  • \(\rho_m\) the density of the material (written \(\rho\) in the PDG; here \(\rho_m\) to keep it apart from the bending radius \(\rho\)), so that \(\rho_m\,dx\) is mass per area; the field states the result in MeV cm\(^2\)/g (1 MeV cm\(^2\)/g = 0.1 MeV m\(^2\)/kg);
  • \(K = 4\pi N_A r_e^2 m_e c^2 = 0.307075\) MeV cm\(^2\)/mol, with \(N_A\) Avogadro’s number and \(r_e = e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0m_ec^2) = 2.818 \times 10^{-15}\) m the classical electron radius (the distance at which the Coulomb energy of two electrons equals \(m_ec^2\); a convenient length, not the electron’s size);
  • \(z\) the charge number of the particle, \(Z\) and \(A\) the atomic number and molar mass (g/mol) of the material;
  • \(W_{max}\) the largest energy one collision can give a free electron,

\[ W_{max} = \frac{2 m_e c^2 \beta^2\gamma^2}{1 + 2\gamma m_e/m + (m_e/m)^2}, \]

which is \(2 m_e c^2\beta^2\gamma^2\) unless \(2\gamma m_e/m\) is no longer small compared with 1, \(m\) the particle’s mass; - \(I_e\) the mean excitation energy of the material (written \(I\) in the literature; here \(I_e\) to keep it apart from current): 75 eV for water in the NIST tables, 85.7 eV for air, 173 eV for silicon, 322 eV for copper; - \(\delta(\beta\gamma)\) the density-effect correction, which matters only at high energy.

Bohr inside Bethe. The front factor is Bohr’s. Write \((e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0)^2 = r_e^2(m_ec^2)^2\) and \(m_ev^2 = m_ec^2\beta^2\); Bohr’s \(4\pi n_ez^2(e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0)^2/(m_ev^2)\) becomes \(4\pi r_e^2m_ec^2\,n_ez^2/\beta^2\). A material of density \(\rho_m\) and molar mass \(A\) has \(\rho_mN_A/A\) atoms per unit volume, each with \(Z\) electrons, so \(n_e = \rho_mN_AZ/A\), and dividing by \(\rho_m\) gives exactly \(Kz^2(Z/A)/\beta^2\). What Bethe’s quantum calculation changed is the bracket: with \(W_{max} \approx 2m_ec^2\beta^2\gamma^2\) the half-logarithm is \(\ln(2m_ec^2\beta^2\gamma^2/I_e) = \ln(2\gamma^2m_ev^2/I_e)\), Bohr’s logarithm with the quantum cut-off, and \(I_e\) in place of \(\hbar\bar\omega\). Because the result is divided by \(\rho_m\), the “mass stopping power” (energy lost per gram per square centimetre crossed) is nearly the same for all materials: it depends on the material only through \(Z/A\), about \(\tfrac12\) for everything but hydrogen, and through \(I_e\) inside a logarithm.

The formula holds to a few per cent for \(0.1 \lesssim \beta\gamma \lesssim 1000\). Below that the particle’s speed is comparable to the speeds of the atomic electrons: shell corrections (the inner electrons, which move fastest, stop taking part), the Barkas and Bloch terms (small corrections in odd and higher powers of \(z\), so that a positive and a negative particle lose slightly different amounts), and for ions the capture of electrons all matter, and tables (PSTAR, ASTAR, SRIM) are used. Above it, radiation takes over for light particles.

The shape. The loss goes as \(1/\beta^2\) at low speed: a slow particle spends longer near each electron. It reaches a broad minimum near \(\beta\gamma \approx 3\) to \(3.5\), about 2 MeV cm\(^2\)/g in most materials (1.664 in silicon, 1.981 in water, 1.403 in copper), and then rises slowly. A particle near the minimum is called minimum-ionising.

At 10 MeV the formula is 0.7 per cent high, at 1 MeV 3.5 per cent high, as the neglected low-energy corrections grow. For a 5.5 MeV alpha in air it gives 741 MeV cm\(^2\)/g against ASTAR’s 712, 4 per cent high.

Range. A particle that loses energy continuously stops after the continuous-slowing-down range (CSDA range). Each small step of energy \(dW'\) is lost over a distance \(dW'/(-dW'/dx)\), and adding the steps from the starting energy down to zero gives

\[ R(W) = \int_0^{W} \frac{dW'}{-dW'/dx}. \]

Integrating the Bethe formula from 10 MeV upward, starting from the tabulated range at 10 MeV, gives for protons in water 4.08 cm at 70 MeV, 7.71 cm at 100 MeV, 15.77 cm at 150 MeV and 25.95 cm at 200 MeV; PSTAR gives 4.08, 7.72, 15.77 and 25.96. Between 50 and 250 MeV the tabulated ranges follow \(R \propto W^{1.76}\).

The Bragg peak. Because the loss rises as the particle slows, a proton deposits energy most densely just before it stops. William Henry Bragg and Richard Kleeman found the rise at the end of alpha tracks in 1904 and measured ranges in many gases in 1905. Following a 150 MeV proton into water with the tabulated stopping power: at the entrance it loses 5.4 MeV/cm; at 10 cm depth, now at 85 MeV, 8.2 MeV/cm; at 15 cm, at 28 MeV, 20 MeV/cm; at 15.7 cm, at 7.3 MeV, 59 MeV/cm. The stopping power at 1 MeV is 48 times that at the entrance. In a real beam the peak is broadened, because the ranges of individual protons scatter about the mean (range straggling), and the beam loses protons to nuclear reactions on the way. Robert R. Wilson proposed in 1946 to use the peak to treat tumours; dose delivery is chapter 28.

Electrons. An electron loses energy by the same collisions, with a modified formula because it can give half its energy to the electron it hits and is deflected strongly. Above a critical energy (the energy at which the two kinds of loss are equal), about 20 MeV in copper (19.4 MeV in the PDG tables), it loses more by radiating bremsstrahlung in the fields of nuclei, and its track becomes a shower: the photons make electron-positron pairs, which radiate again, and so on. So a cup for electrons is larger than one for ions, made of a light material to slow them gently, and wrapped in lead to catch what escapes (Forck, section 2.5), and the radiation from stopped electrons is mostly photons (section 13).

3. The Faraday cup

The picture. An insulated metal block stops the beam, and an ammeter between it and ground reads the current. Nothing else measures current absolutely and so simply: the block counts charges.

Secondary electrons. When a particle strikes the block, it knocks out secondary electrons, mostly with energies below 10 eV, in a distribution peaked forward of the surface (Forck, section 2.4). If they leave the cup, a positive beam reads too high: each escaping electron adds one charge. With a yield of two electrons per ion, an unsuppressed cup reads three times the true current. Three remedies, often combined:

  • Depth. A cup much longer than its opening loses few electrons through it.
  • A suppressor electrode. A ring at the entrance held negative, at some hundreds of volts, well above the electrons’ energy, turns them back into the cup. The field is weakest on the axis, where most electrons start, so the voltage is set by measuring the current against suppression voltage until it stops changing.
  • A magnetic field. Permanent magnets across the cup make electrons spiral. The radius of the circle is \(p_\perp/(eB)\) (F4, section 10), and a slow electron of kinetic energy \(W_\perp = p_\perp^2/2m_e\) across the field has \(p_\perp = \sqrt{2m_eW_\perp}\), so the radius is

\[ r_c = \frac{\sqrt{2 m_e W_\perp}}{eB}, \]

with \(W_\perp\) the electron’s kinetic energy across the field (J) and \(B\) the field (T). For 10 eV and 10 mT, \(r_c = 1.07\) mm, small against a cup of 50 mm. The GSI cups use cobalt-samarium magnets giving about 0.1 T.

What it reads. The current \(I = \dot{N} q\); for a singly charged beam, 1 µA is \(6.24 \times 10^{12}\) particles per second. A cup with a low-noise current amplifier reads down to about 10 pA even for a steady beam, five orders of magnitude below a DC transformer (Forck, section 2.4). It reads correctly only if the beam stops in it and if no charge enters or leaves except the beam: electrons from residual gas or from the beam pipe, if they reach the cup, are counted as beam.

Heat. The beam’s power ends in the cup. One microampere at one megavolt is one watt: 1 µA of 10 MeV protons deposits 10 W in a layer 0.245 mm deep in copper; 100 µA of 20 MeV protons from a cyclotron, 2 kW, needs water cooling. A pulsed beam is worse than its average: during the pulse the surface heats far above the mean temperature. The cooling water must be demineralised, and even so it conducts a little, so a cooled cup is less accurate than an uncooled one.

Where cups stop. For energies below 100 MeV per nucleon the range in copper is about a centimetre or less (1.3 cm for a 100 MeV proton) and a cup is practical. Above it the range is too long, nuclear reactions throw charged secondaries out of the cup, and other methods take over. Electrons need much deeper cups and are measured with cups only in the first sections of an electron linear accelerator.

Pre-requisite: Magnetic circuits and iron

Pre-requisite. What happens to a magnetic field in iron and similar materials: magnetisation and the field \(H\), the relative permeability \(\mu_r\) that lets a ring of such material gather a beam’s weak field, the magnetic circuit (Ampère’s law written with \(H\), and flux carried round a closed core), saturation, the magnetic domains whose jerky motion makes Barkhausen noise, and magnetostriction. Section 4 uses all of them for the current transformer and the DC transformer; the magnetic circuit returns for every iron magnet in later chapters. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, §8.2 (magnetic fields in matter: magnetisation, bound currents, the field \(H\), permeability, ferromagnetism), Livingston and Blewett, §8-2 (the magnetic circuit), and Chao and Tigner, Handbook, §7.4.1 (the high-permeability ring core of current transformers, and the DC transformer that drives it into saturation); domains, Barkhausen noise and magnetostriction are given only in passing in these books and are explained here from the domain picture.

Magnetisation. Atoms with unpaired electrons carry small magnetic moments. In a field they line up, partly or wholly, and the material acquires a magnetisation \(\mathbf{M}\), the magnetic moment per unit volume (A/m). Lined-up moments act like current loops; inside the material neighbouring loops cancel, and what is left is a current flowing round the surface (Tong calls these bound currents). That surface current adds its own field. To keep account of it, define

\[ \mathbf{H} = \frac{\mathbf{B}}{\mu_0} - \mathbf{M} \quad (\text{A/m}). \]

Then Ampère’s law, written with \(\mathbf{H}\), counts only the currents in wires and beams (free currents) and ignores the bound ones:

\[ \oint\mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = I_{free}. \]

In many materials \(\mathbf{M}\) is proportional to \(\mathbf{H}\), and then \(\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mu_r\mathbf{H}\), with \(\mu_r\) the relative permeability, a pure number. For copper, aluminium, water and glass \(\mu_r\) differs from 1 by parts in \(10^5\). For soft iron it is several thousand; for transformer steels \(10^3\) to \(10^4\); for ferrites \(10^2\) to \(10^4\); for the amorphous and nanocrystalline cobalt and iron alloys used in beam transformers \(10^4\) to \(10^5\) at low frequency.

The ring around a beam. A beam of current \(I\) passes through a ring-shaped core. By symmetry \(\mathbf{H}\) circles the beam and has the same size all round a circle of radius \(r\), so Ampère’s law gives \(H\cdot2\pi r = I\):

\[ H = \frac{I}{2\pi r}, \qquad B = \frac{\mu_0\mu_rI}{2\pi r}. \]

In air, with \(\mu_r = 1\), this is the field of a straight wire, from Ampère’s law in chapter 2 (and F4, section 12). The line integral counts only the current threading the ring, not where it passes, which is why a transformer’s reading barely depends on the beam’s position. Numbers for 1 µA at \(r = 80\) mm: \(H = 2.0\) µA/m; in air \(B = 2.5\) pT, against the Earth’s 50 µT; in a core with \(\mu_r = 10^5\), \(B = 0.25\) µT. The flux through a core of rectangular cross-section, inner and outer radii \(r_i\) and \(r_o\) and length \(l\) along the beam, is

\[ \Phi = \int_{r_i}^{r_o}\frac{\mu_0\mu_rI}{2\pi r}\,l\,dr = \frac{\mu_0\mu_rIl}{2\pi}\ln\frac{r_o}{r_i}, \]

which for the GSI core of section 4 (\(r_i = 70\) mm, \(r_o = 90\) mm, \(l = 16\) mm, \(\mu_r = 10^5\)) is \(8.0 \times 10^{-11}\) Wb per microampere. That flux, \(10^5\) times what the same ring of air would hold, is what a winding on the core senses.

The magnetic circuit. Two rules are enough for a closed core. First, Ampère’s law: the line integral of \(H\) round the core equals the ampere-turns it encloses, \(NI\). Second, magnetic flux has no ends (\(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0\)), so the flux \(\Phi = BA\) is the same through every cross-section of the core, as current is the same through every part of a series circuit. For a core of iron path length \(l_{Fe}\) with a gap \(g\), both of area \(A\):

\[ NI = H_{Fe}l_{Fe} + H_{gap}g = \frac{\Phi}{A}\left(\frac{l_{Fe}}{\mu_0\mu_r} + \frac{g}{\mu_0}\right). \]

Livingston and Blewett point out the analogy with Ohm’s law: flux plays the part of current, \(NI\) of the electromotive force, and each section has a “reluctance” \(l/(\mu_0\mu_rA)\) in place of a resistance. Two lessons follow.

  • In a magnet the gap costs almost everything. For 1 T in a 50 mm gap, \(NI = Bg/\mu_0 = 39\,800\) ampere-turns. Two metres of iron at \(\mu_r = 1000\) add \(B\,l_{Fe}/(\mu_0\mu_r) = 1600\), 4 per cent. Livingston and Blewett’s example: 1 T across 10 cm needs \(7.95 \times 10^4\) ampere-turns, 79.5 A in a 1000-turn coil.
  • In a transformer any gap is ruinous. A tape-wound ring with a 0.5 m path and \(\mu_r = 10^5\) behaves, with a gap of only 10 µm, as if \(\mu_r\) were \(l/(l/\mu_r + g) = 3.3 \times 10^4\): the gap holds twice as much “reluctance” as the whole ring. That is why beam transformers use unbroken wound rings. (The ceramic break in the beam pipe of section 4 is a different thing: it is in the vacuum chamber, not the core, and forces the image current to flow outside the ring.)

Domains and saturation. Why are some materials so permeable? In iron, cobalt, nickel and their alloys, neighbouring atomic moments line up spontaneously, through a quantum-mechanical interaction between the electrons, over regions called domains, typically micrometres to millimetres across. Each domain is magnetised to the full saturation value; in an unmagnetised piece the domains point in different directions and cancel. A small applied \(H\) moves the walls between domains, so that domains pointing along \(H\) grow at the expense of the others. A small cause makes a large magnetisation: that is the large \(\mu_r\).

When the favourable domains have taken over the whole sample, \(M\) can grow no more. This is saturation. Beyond it \(B\) rises only as \(\mu_0H\), and the effective \(\mu_r\) collapses. Saturation flux densities are about 2.15 T for pure iron, about 2 T for silicon steels, 0.4 to 0.5 T for ferrites and about 0.6 T for the cobalt-based amorphous alloys of section 4. For a core with \(\mu_r = 10^5\), saturation at 0.6 T needs only \(H = B/(\mu_0\mu_r) = 4.8\) A/m, which at \(r = 80\) mm is \(2\pi rH = 2.4\) ampere-turns (in practice \(\mu_r\) falls before saturation, so this is an order of magnitude). A few ampere-turns of modulation current therefore drive a DC transformer’s core through saturation, and a DC beam of an ampere is enough to shift its operating point noticeably. The DC transformer turns that shift into a measurement.

Domain walls also stick on impurities and grain boundaries, so the magnetisation depends on the history: the curve of \(B\) against \(H\) is a loop (hysteresis), and the field needed to bring \(B\) back to zero is the coercive field. Materials for transformers are chosen “soft”, with small coercive fields, below a few A/m.

Barkhausen noise. A wall that sticks does not move smoothly when \(H\) rises: it waits, then jumps to the next pinning site. Each jump reverses a small volume suddenly, and the flux through the core changes by a small step. A winding on the core turns each step into a short voltage spike, \(V = N\,d\Phi/dt\). Heinrich Barkhausen heard them in 1919 as clicks in a telephone receiver connected to a coil around iron being magnetised. A DC transformer drives each core round the whole loop once per modulation cycle, from one saturation to the other and back, so it makes thousands of such jumps every cycle, at random. Their sum is a noise that no amount of care with the electronics removes; it sets the floor of a microampere or so quoted in section 4.

Magnetostriction. Turning the magnetisation of a domain slightly stretches or squeezes the crystal lattice, so a magnetic material changes length when magnetised. The fractional change at saturation is of order \(10^{-5}\): about \(-7 \times 10^{-6}\) for iron, \(-3 \times 10^{-5}\) for nickel, and close to zero for some cobalt-based amorphous alloys. On a ring 0.5 m round, 30 parts per million is 15 µm. The effect works backwards too: stress changes the magnetisation and the permeability. So a core shaken by a vacuum pump or a cooling fan has a permeability that wobbles at the vibration frequency, and the flux of any field it carries wobbles with it. In a transformer that reads microamperes this shows up as false current at the pump’s frequency. Hence low-magnetostriction core materials (Chao and Tigner, §6.18.1, count the lack of magnetostriction among the virtues of an amorphous, nanocrystalline core material, there in magnetic pulse compression) and mechanical isolation of the transformer.

Pre-requisite. This continues Faraday induction, inductance and magnetic energy in chapter 1, which gave Faraday’s law, \(\oint\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = -d\Phi_B/dt\), and magnetic flux in webers. It adds three things a current transformer is made of: the inductance of \(N\) turns on a ring core, which grows as \(N^2\); transformer action, by which the beam, a one-turn primary, drives a current \(I_{beam}/N\) in the secondary; and the voltage induced in a winding whose flux is being swept, as in the DC transformer. Section 4 uses all three. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, §4.1 (Faraday’s law) and §4.1.2 (inductance and magnetostatic energy), and Chao and Tigner, Handbook, §7.4.1 (current transformers); it uses the core field of Magnetic circuits and iron.

Inductance of a winding on a ring. Wind \(N\) turns evenly on a ring core of relative permeability \(\mu_r\), rectangular cross-section, inner and outer radii \(r_i\) and \(r_o\), length \(l\), and pass a current \(I\). The turns thread a circle of radius \(r\) inside the core \(N\) times, so Ampère’s law gives \(H = NI/(2\pi r)\) and \(B = \mu_0\mu_rNI/(2\pi r)\). The flux through one turn, the core’s cross-section, is the integral of the previous section with \(I\) replaced by \(NI\):

\[ \Phi = \frac{\mu_0\mu_rNIl}{2\pi}\ln\frac{r_o}{r_i}. \]

The inductance is the flux linked by the whole winding per ampere. The winding links the flux \(N\) times, so

\[ L = \frac{N\Phi}{I} = \frac{\mu_0\mu_r}{2\pi}\,lN^2\ln\frac{r_o}{r_i}, \]

section 4’s formula. The \(N^2\) has a plain reason: \(N\) turns make \(N\) times the field, and that field is linked \(N\) times. For the GSI core with \(N = 10\), \(L = 8.04\) mH; the same core with 100 turns would have 0.80 H. The energy stored in the core at current \(I\) is \(\tfrac12LI^2\), the magnetic energy of chapter 1.

The beam as a one-turn primary. Now let the beam, current \(I_{beam}\), pass through the ring, and close the \(N\)-turn winding through a resistor \(R\), carrying current \(I_s\) in the direction that opposes the beam’s (Lenz’s law). Ampère’s law round the core counts both: the core holds the flux of a net current \(I_{beam} - NI_s\),

\[ \Phi = \frac{L}{N^2}\left(I_{beam} - NI_s\right), \]

since \(L/N^2\) is the flux per ampere-turn. Faraday’s law in the winding (F4, section 14) gives the voltage that drives \(I_s\) through \(R\): each of the \(N\) turns has \(d\Phi/dt\) induced in it, so \(RI_s = N\,d\Phi/dt\). Putting in \(\Phi\) from the line above,

\[ RI_s = \frac{L}{N}\frac{dI_{beam}}{dt} - L\frac{dI_s}{dt}. \]

If the beam current changes quickly compared with the time \(L/R\), the \(R\) term is small beside the other two, so \(L\,dI_s/dt \approx (L/N)\,dI_{beam}/dt\), and

\[ I_s = \frac{I_{beam}}{N}. \]

That is transformer action. The secondary’s ampere-turns cancel the beam’s, the net flux in the core stays small, and the voltage across the load is \(U = RI_s = (R/N)I_{beam}\): the sensitivity \(R/N\) of section 4. For a 1 mA beam, \(N = 10\) and \(R = 50\) Ω, \(I_s = 0.1\) mA and \(U = 5\) mV. The same equation also says what happens to slow changes, which the next section takes up: the transformer cannot hold a steady secondary current, and its output sags.

By the right-hand rule the field \(H = I_{beam}/(2\pi r)\) circles anticlockwise inside the core; the winding of \(N\) turns is closed through the load \(R\), across which the output voltage is read. A sense winding. A winding of \(N\) turns that carries no current measures the flux through the core: \(V = N\,d\Phi/dt\). In a DC transformer the modulation current sweeps each core from one saturation to the other and back. With a core cross-section of 1 cm\(^2\) swinging from \(-0.6\) T to \(+0.6\) T, the flux changes by \(1.2 \times 10^{-4}\) Wb; if this takes half a cycle at 1 kHz, 0.5 ms, a 100-turn sense winding sees on average \(100 \times 1.2 \times 10^{-4}/5 \times 10^{-4} = 24\) V. Two identical cores swept in opposite senses give equal and opposite voltages, and their sum is zero. A DC beam current adds the same \(H\) to both, so one core saturates a little earlier and the other a little later in each cycle; the sum is no longer zero and has a component at twice the modulation frequency. That is the signal the feedback loop of section 4 nulls.

Pre-requisite (continued): RLC circuits and phasors

Pre-requisite. This continues RLC circuits and phasors in chapter 4, which gave Ohm’s law, the RC discharge and its time constant, phasors and impedances, and the ringing of an RLC loop (with more in chapter 5). It adds the inductor’s time constant \(L/R\) and the exponential droop it gives a transformer, resistors in parallel, and the stray capacitance and inductance that slow every fast circuit. Section 4 uses them for the droop and rise times of transformers and for the resistors of the wall-current monitor. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, §4.1.2 and §4.1.3 (inductance and resistance), and Chao and Tigner, Handbook, §7.4.1 (the droop rate \(R/L\) of a current transformer and the corner frequencies of the wall-current monitor).

The RL time constant. Take the transformer equation of the section above and switch on a beam of constant current \(I_0\) at \(t = 0\). During the jump \(dI_{beam}/dt\) is huge, and integrating the equation across it gives a jump in \(I_s\) of \(I_0/N\): the secondary takes up the full transformed current at once. After the jump \(dI_{beam}/dt = 0\), and the equation becomes the first-order differential equation of a decay, whose solution is an exponential:

\[ RI_s + L\frac{dI_s}{dt} = 0, \qquad I_s(t) = \frac{I_0}{N}\,e^{-t/\tau_{droop}}, \qquad \tau_{droop} = \frac{L}{R}. \]

The output voltage \(RI_s\) sags exponentially although the beam is steady. A circuit picture helps: the transformer is a current source \(I_{beam}/N\) feeding \(R\) and \(L\) in parallel. A fast change cannot pass through an inductor at once, so it goes into \(R\); but a steady current builds up in the inductor (the magnetising current of the core) at the rate \(U/L\), and whatever goes into \(L\) is lost from \(R\). The time constant \(L/R\) (henries over ohms gives seconds) is the inductive partner of chapter 4’s \(RC\).

At work. The GSI passive transformer (\(L = 8.04\) mH, \(R = 50\) Ω) has \(\tau_{droop} = 0.16\) ms. A 10 µs pulse ends \(1 - e^{-10/161} = 6\) per cent low; a 100 µs pulse 46 per cent low; a 1 ms pulse is gone. To keep droop below 1 per cent the pulse must be shorter than \(-\tau\ln0.99 = 1.6\) µs. To see the millisecond pulses of a linear accelerator, \(L/R\) must be made seconds long: more turns raise \(L\) as \(N^2\), and an amplifier (Vacuum tubes and amplifiers, continued, below) brings the load down to the winding’s own resistance of about 1 Ω. With 100 turns on the same core, \(L = 0.80\) H and \(\tau_{droop} = 0.8\) s, and a 5 ms pulse droops by only 0.6 per cent before any further correction.

Figure 8.6. What a transformer shows for a flat beam pulse 100 µs long, as a fraction of the transformed current \(RI_0/N\), from \(e^{-t/\tau}\) during the pulse. The passive GSI transformer (\(\tau = L/R = 0.16\) ms) sags to 0.54 by the end of the pulse, 46 per cent low; when the beam stops, its output drops by the full step and so goes negative, then recovers with the same time constant, because the current stored in the core’s inductance has to die away. The active transformer (\(\tau = 0.8\) s) is flat to about 0.01 per cent over the pulse.

Resistors in parallel. Resistors side by side share the same voltage, and their currents add: \(I = V/R_1 + V/R_2 + \dots\). So the combination behaves as one resistor with

\[ \frac{1}{R_{par}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \dots, \qquad R_{par} = \frac{R}{n}\ \text{for } n \text{ equal resistors}. \]

The wall-current monitor of section 4 bridges its ceramic gap with \(n\) resistors evenly spaced round the pipe. The whole image current passes through them in parallel, so \(U = (R/n)I_{beam}\): 50 resistors of 50 Ω give 1 Ω, and each carries a fiftieth of the current. Spreading them evenly keeps the reading independent of where the beam is and keeps the inductance of the path low.

Stray capacitance and inductance. No real component is pure. The turns of a winding lie close to each other and to the core and case, so a capacitance \(C_S\) appears across the load; leads and turns have an inductance \(L_S\) in series. Across \(R\), the stray capacitance must be charged through \(R\) before the output can change, which takes a time of order \(RC_S\): with \(R = 50\) Ω and \(C_S = 10\) pF (assumed), \(RC_S = 0.5\) ns. With \(L_S\) in the path as well, the circuit can ring at \(1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_SC_S})\), and its fastest response is of order \(\sqrt{L_SC_S}\): 25 nH and 10 pF give 0.5 ns and ringing at 318 MHz. These are the rise times of section 4. Chao and Tigner note that rise times below about 1 ns are hard to reach in transformers for exactly this reason, the capacitance between turns and to ground.

Pre-requisite: Impulse response, transfer functions, impedance

Pre-requisite. How to describe what a linear instrument does to a signal: its transfer function (output over input at each frequency), its low and high cut-off frequencies and so its bandwidth, its step and impulse responses, and the rule that ties a rise time to the upper cut-off, \(t_r \approx 0.35/f_{high}\). Section 4 states the performance of transformers and the wall-current monitor in exactly these terms, and section 5 and later chapters use the same language, including impedance as the transfer function from a current to a voltage. Taught from Kuffel, Zaengl and Kuffel, §3.6.2 and §3.6.3 (the transfer characteristic of a measuring system, its unit step response, and the superposition or Duhamel integral), Chao and Tigner, Handbook, §7.4.1 and §7.4.2 (corner frequencies \(\omega_{low} = R/L\) and \(\omega_{high} = 1/RC\) of transformers and wall-current monitors; the transfer impedance of a button), and Lee, chapter 3, exercise 3.6.5 (an RLC circuit struck by a current impulse).

Linear systems. (The circuit sections of this chapter write the imaginary unit as \(j\), as chapter 4 does; it is the \(i\) of F5, section 1.) An instrument is linear if doubling the input doubles the output and the response to two inputs together is the sum of the responses to each; it is time-invariant if delaying the input only delays the output. Transformers, pick-ups, cables and amplifiers working within their range are both. For such a system a sinusoidal input gives a sinusoidal output at the same frequency, only scaled and shifted. With the phasors of chapter 4, the whole behaviour is one complex function of frequency, the transfer function

\[ H(\omega) = \frac{\hat{V}_{out}}{\hat{V}_{in}}, \]

whose size is the ratio of amplitudes and whose angle is the phase shift. When the input is a current and the output a voltage, the same ratio has units of ohms and is called a (transfer) impedance, \(Z(\omega) = \hat{U}/\hat{I}_{beam}\). A transformer’s sensitivity \(R/N\) in V/A is its transfer impedance in the middle of its band; the “impedance” of a beam pipe in chapter 22 is the same idea seen from the beam’s side.

The low-pass: stray capacitance. A load \(R\) with a capacitance \(C\) across it, driven through \(R\) (or fed by a current source), is the simplest high-frequency limit. Treating \(R\) and \(C\) as a divider, \(H = Z_C/(R + Z_C)\) with \(Z_C = 1/(j\omega C)\):

\[ H(\omega) = \frac{1}{1 + j\omega RC}, \qquad |H| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (\omega RC)^2}} . \]

At low frequency \(|H| = 1\); at high frequency it falls as \(1/\omega\). At \(\omega = 1/RC\), \(|H| = 1/\sqrt2\): the output power, which goes as the square of the voltage, has halved. Engineers write power ratios in decibels, \(10\log_{10}(P_{out}/P_{in})\) dB, and a half is \(-3.01\) dB, so this point is called the \(-3\) dB point. It defines the upper cut-off,

\[ f_{high} = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}. \]

The high-pass: droop. The transformer of the section above is a current source \(I_{beam}/N\) feeding \(R\) and \(L\) in parallel. The share that reaches \(R\) is \(Z_L/(R + Z_L)\) with \(Z_L = j\omega L\), so

\[ \frac{\hat{U}}{\hat{I}_{beam}} = \frac{R}{N}\,\frac{j\omega\tau}{1 + j\omega\tau}, \qquad \tau = \frac{L}{R}, \]

which is \(R/N\) at high frequency and falls to zero at DC. Its \(-3\) dB point is the lower cut-off,

\[ f_{low} = \frac{1}{2\pi\tau_{droop}} = \frac{R}{2\pi L}. \]

A real transformer has both: its transfer impedance is flat at \(R/N\) between \(f_{low}\) and \(f_{high}\), the band, and falls outside it. The width of the flat region, roughly \(f_{high}\) when \(f_{low} \ll f_{high}\), is the bandwidth.

Step response and rise time. Feed the low-pass a sudden step from 0 to 1. The capacitor charges through \(R\), and the output is \(1 - e^{-t/RC}\). It passes 10 per cent at \(t = RC\ln(10/9) = 0.105\,RC\) and 90 per cent at \(t = RC\ln10 = 2.303\,RC\). The 10 to 90 per cent rise time is

\[ t_r = RC\ln9 = 2.20\,RC = \frac{\ln9}{2\pi f_{high}} = \frac{0.35}{f_{high}}. \]

The rule \(t_r \approx 0.35/f_{high}\) is exact for this circuit and close for most well-behaved instruments. The high-pass’s step response is the droop, \(e^{-t/\tau}\): after a tenth of \(\tau\) the output is still at 90 per cent. Kuffel, Zaengl and Kuffel describe high-voltage dividers in the same way, by a measured unit step response, because a step contains all frequencies at once.

Impulse response. The response to a very short input of unit area is the impulse response \(h(t)\); for the low-pass it is \((1/RC)\,e^{-t/RC}\), the derivative of the step response. Any input can be cut into thin slices, each a short pulse of area \(x(t')\,dt'\), and by linearity the output is the sum of their delayed responses:

\[ y(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t}h(t - t')\,x(t')\,dt'. \]

This is the convolution (Kuffel’s Duhamel integral is the same sum written with the step response). The transfer function is the Fourier transform of the impulse response; that link is made in Fourier series and transforms before section 9.

Stages in series. Signals pass through a pick-up, a cable, an amplifier and an oscilloscope. Transfer functions multiply. Rise times add roughly in quadrature, \(t_r \approx \sqrt{t_1^2 + t_2^2 + \dots}\); for two identical RC stages the exact 10 to 90 per cent time is \(3.36\,RC\) against the quadrature estimate \(3.11\,RC\), close enough for planning.

At work.

  • The GSI passive transformer. \(\tau_{droop} = 0.16\) ms gives \(f_{low} = 1.0\) kHz (0.8 kHz from the measured 0.2 ms; Forck quotes the lower edge of the band as 2 kHz, the same order). Its upper edge of 300 MHz gives \(t_r = 0.35/300\ \mathrm{MHz} = 1.2\) ns, the quoted 1 ns.
  • The wall-current monitor. With \(R/n = 1\) Ω and an assumed gap capacitance of 50 pF, \(f_{high} = 1/(2\pi \times 1 \times 50 \times 10^{-12}) = 3.2\) GHz. With ferrite rings giving an assumed 15 µH, \(f_{low} = 1/(2\pi \times 15 \times 10^{-6}) = 11\) kHz. These match section 4’s “near 10 kHz” and “several GHz”.
  • What a bunch needs. A Gaussian bunch of rms duration \(\sigma_t\) has a spectrum that falls to half power at \(f = 0.13/\sigma_t\) (shown in the Fourier section). A 1 ns bunch needs about 130 MHz, which the GSI transformer has; a bunch 1 cm long, 33 ps, needs 4 GHz, which only a wall-current monitor or a faster pick-up can give.

Pre-requisite: Random signals and noise, spectral density

Pre-requisite. How to describe and measure a random signal: its mean and root-mean-square value, its spectral density (mean square per hertz of bandwidth), why noise grows as the square root of the bandwidth, and the thermal noise of a resistor, \(\sqrt{4k_BTR\,\Delta f}\), derived here from equipartition. Section 4 uses it to compare a transformer’s resistor with its amplifier; section 5 uses it for button signals and for the gain from narrow-band processing; later chapters (Schottky signals, stochastic cooling) build on the same ideas. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 5, §118 and §122 (correlations of fluctuations in time and their spectral resolution) and §124 (the fluctuation-dissipation theorem, of which a resistor’s noise is one case), and Chao and Tigner, Handbook, §7.4.1 (noise as the limit of current transformers) and §7.4.2 (bandwidth and signal-to-noise ratio in position-monitor receivers). The derivation below is a short form of Nyquist’s argument of 1928.

A random signal. The voltage across a resistor, read on a fast enough instrument, wanders at random about zero. Its mean is zero, so describe it by the mean of its square, \(\langle v^2\rangle\), the average over a long time, or by the root-mean-square value \(v_{rms} = \sqrt{\langle v^2\rangle}\). Two noises from independent sources add in mean square, \(\langle(v_1 + v_2)^2\rangle = \langle v_1^2\rangle + \langle v_2^2\rangle\), because the cross term \(\langle v_1v_2\rangle\) averages to zero. This is the same rule as variances adding for independent errors (chapter 1).

Spectral density. Pass the noise through a filter that lets through only a narrow band \(\Delta f\) around a frequency \(f\). The mean square that comes out is proportional to \(\Delta f\) for a narrow band, and the constant of proportionality is the spectral density \(S_v(f)\):

\[ \langle v^2\rangle_{\Delta f} = S_v(f)\,\Delta f \quad (S_v \text{ in V}^2/\text{Hz}). \]

Noise whose \(S_v\) is the same at all frequencies is called white. Through a filter of transfer function \(H(f)\) (the previous section), the output’s mean square is \(\int S_v|H|^2\,df\). Because the mean square goes as \(\Delta f\), the rms goes as \(\sqrt{\Delta f}\), and noise is quoted in units such as nV/\(\sqrt{\text{Hz}}\).

The noise of a resistor. A resistor \(R\) at temperature \(T\) has electrons in thermal motion, and their random drift makes a random voltage. Its size follows from equilibrium. Connect a capacitor \(C\) across the resistor and leave the pair at temperature \(T\). The capacitor’s energy \(\tfrac12CV^2\) is one quadratic term in the energy of a system in thermal equilibrium, and such a term has the mean value \(\tfrac12k_BT\) (equipartition: the same reasoning that gives \(\langle p_x^2\rangle/2m = \tfrac12k_BT\) for a molecule in the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution). So

\[ \langle V^2\rangle = \frac{k_BT}{C}. \]

Now describe the resistor as a noiseless \(R\) in series with a noise source of white spectral density \(S\). Seen from the capacitor, \(R\) and \(C\) form the low-pass of the previous section, with \(|H|^2 = 1/(1 + (f/f_c)^2)\) and \(f_c = 1/(2\pi RC)\). The capacitor’s mean square voltage is therefore

\[ \langle V^2\rangle = S\int_0^{\infty}\frac{df}{1 + (f/f_c)^2} = S\,\frac{\pi}{2}f_c = \frac{S}{4RC}, \]

using \(\int_0^\infty du/(1 + u^2) = \pi/2\). Equating the two expressions, \(S/(4RC) = k_BT/C\), and \(C\) cancels:

\[ S = 4k_BTR, \qquad v_n = \sqrt{4k_BTR\,\Delta f}. \]

The capacitor was only a probe; the result belongs to the resistor alone. It holds while \(hf \ll k_BT\), which at room temperature means up to several terahertz. It is one case of the fluctuation-dissipation theorem, which Landau and Lifshitz (§124) derive in general, without reference to circuits: anything that dissipates energy, such as a resistance, also fluctuates, in proportion to the dissipation and the temperature.

Numbers. For 50 Ω at 300 K, \(\sqrt{4k_BTR} = 0.91\) nV/\(\sqrt{\text{Hz}}\). Over 300 MHz this is 15.8 µV; over 100 MHz 9.1 µV; over 1 MHz 0.91 µV; over 10 kHz 91 nV. The capacitor’s own \(\sqrt{k_BT/C}\), the “kT/C noise”, is 20 µV for 10 pF, whatever the resistance.

Narrow bands and averaging. A signal at one frequency passes a filter of any width centred on it unchanged, while the noise that gets through falls as \(\sqrt{\Delta f}\). So narrowing the band from 100 MHz to 10 kHz cuts the noise voltage by \(\sqrt{10^4} = 100\). The price is time resolution: a filter of width \(\Delta f\) responds in a time of order \(1/\Delta f\), here 0.1 ms, so the reading is no longer bunch by bunch. Averaging \(N\) independent readings is the same trade in another form: the rms falls as \(1/\sqrt{N}\).

At work: the position monitor. Two electrodes each give a signal \(U\), each with independent noise of rms \(\sigma_n\). The difference over sum then has noise \(\sqrt2\,\sigma_n/(2U)\), and with the sensitivity \(S\) of section 5 the position noise is \(\sigma_x = \sqrt2\,\sigma_n/(S \cdot 2U)\). For buttons with \(S = 7.91\) per cent per millimetre (79.1 per metre) and the thermal noise of 50 Ω over 100 MHz:

Signal per electrode Bandwidth \(\sigma_x\)
0.12 V (about \(10^9\) particles) 100 MHz 0.7 µm
1.2 mV (a weak bunch) 100 MHz 68 µm
1.2 mV 10 kHz 0.7 µm

Resistor noise alone is not what limits monitors in practice, as section 5 says; but the table shows the scaling, and why narrow-band processing reaches micrometres where broadband reaches tens of micrometres.

Amplifiers add noise. Every amplifier adds noise of its own, quoted as an equivalent noise voltage density at its input or as a noise temperature \(T_n\): the amplifier on a source resistance \(R\) is as noisy as that resistance would be at \(T + T_n\). For the GSI transformer, the quoted resolution of 40 µA rms at 4 V/A is 160 µV over 300 MHz, or 9.2 nV/\(\sqrt{\text{Hz}}\): ten times the 50 Ω resistor’s density, equivalent to \(T_n \approx 3 \times 10^4\) K. The resistor alone would allow 4 µA. Section 4’s conclusion, that the amplifier and not the resistor sets the floor, is this comparison.

Pre-requisite (continued): Vacuum tubes and amplifiers

Pre-requisite. This continues Vacuum tubes and amplifiers in chapter 6, which gave the triode as a controlled valve and the logarithmic amplifier. It adds the operational amplifier with feedback: the virtual ground at its input, the current-to-voltage amplifier that reads a Faraday cup and makes a transformer “active”, and how the feedback resistor and the amplifier’s own noise set the smallest current that can be read. Section 4 uses the virtual ground for the active transformer; section 3 and the bench section use the current amplifier on cups. Taught from Kuffel, Zaengl and Kuffel, §3.5.3 and §7.2.1 (operational amplifiers in measuring circuits, holding a screen at an artificial ground), and Chao and Tigner, Handbook, §7.4.1 (the feedback technique that extends a transformer’s low-frequency response, and its cost in noise); it uses the noise section above.

The operational amplifier. An operational amplifier is a voltage amplifier with two inputs, marked \(+\) and \(-\), whose output is \(V_{out} = A(V_+ - V_-)\), with a gain \(A\) of \(10^5\) to \(10^6\) at low frequency, and whose inputs draw almost no current. On its own so large a gain is useless: any input above a few microvolts drives the output to its limit. It becomes useful with feedback, a connection from the output back to the \(-\) input.

The current-to-voltage amplifier. Earth the \(+\) input. Connect a resistor \(R_f\) from the output to the \(-\) input, and feed the current \(I\) to be measured into the \(-\) input. No current enters the amplifier, so all of \(I\) flows on through \(R_f\):

\[ I = \frac{V_- - V_{out}}{R_f}, \qquad V_{out} = -AV_- . \]

Eliminate \(V_-\): \(I = -V_{out}(1 + 1/A)/R_f\), so

\[ V_{out} = -IR_f\,\frac{A}{1 + A} \approx -IR_f, \qquad V_- = \frac{IR_f}{1 + A}. \]

The output reads the current through \(R_f\), whatever the source. The \(-\) input sits within a few microvolts of earth although nothing connects it to earth: a virtual ground. The source sees a load of only \(V_-/I = R_f/(1 + A)\). With \(A = 10^5\) and \(R_f = 1\) MΩ, 1 µA gives \(-1.0\) V at the output while the input moves by 10 µV; the source sees 10 Ω.

Uses in this chapter. A Faraday cup feeds such an amplifier, with \(R_f\) switched from 1 MΩ (1 µA to 1 V) to 1 GΩ (1 nA to 1 V) as the bench section describes; 10 pA on 1 GΩ gives 10 mV. In the active transformer the secondary winding is connected to the virtual ground. The winding then sees almost no load, only its own wire resistance \(R_L\) of about an ohm, so the droop time \(L/R_L\) becomes long (the RL section above), while the output, \(-I_sR_f\), still has the sensitivity \(R_f/N\) chosen by the designer.

What sets the floor. The feedback resistor is itself a noise source. Its thermal noise voltage \(\sqrt{4k_BTR_f\Delta f}\) appears at the output, which is the same as a noise current \(\sqrt{4k_BT\Delta f/R_f}\) added to the input. The signal grows as \(R_f\) and this noise only as \(\sqrt{R_f}\), so large feedback resistors are used for small currents:

\(R_f\) Noise current density at 300 K Over 10 Hz
1 MΩ 0.13 pA/\(\sqrt{\text{Hz}}\) 0.41 pA
1 GΩ 4.1 fA/\(\sqrt{\text{Hz}}\) 13 fA

To this the amplifier adds its own input noise voltage and current, and the capacitance of the input cable turns the input noise voltage into a noise current that grows with frequency. A large \(R_f\) is also slow: with a stray 0.5 pF across 1 GΩ the time constant is 0.5 ms. That is why the most sensitive cup readings (about 10 pA, section 3) are averaged over a fraction of a second, and why a fast transformer reads only microamperes: the floor of every current measurement is a noise density times the square root of the bandwidth it needs.

4. Transformers and the wall-current monitor

The picture. A ring of magnetic material around the beam is a transformer whose primary is the beam itself, one turn. A winding of \(N\) turns on the ring is the secondary (Figure 8.5). The beam’s magnetic field is tiny, \(\mu_0 I/2\pi r\), which for 1 µA at 10 cm is 2 pT, against the Earth’s 50 µT; the ring gathers it from all round, so the reading barely depends on where the beam passes.

The passive transformer. The ring’s inductance is

\[ L = \frac{\mu_0\mu_r}{2\pi}\,l N^2 \ln\frac{r_o}{r_i}, \]

with \(\mu_r\) the relative permeability, \(l\) the ring’s length along the beam (m), \(r_i\) and \(r_o\) its inner and outer radii (m). The secondary current is \(I_{beam}/N\); across a load \(R\) it makes a voltage \(U = (R/N)\,I_{beam}\), so the sensitivity is \(R/N\) (V/A). The circuit of \(L\), \(R\) and the stray capacitance \(C_S\) of the winding passes faithfully only a band of frequencies:

\[ \tau_{droop} = \frac{L}{R}, \qquad \tau_{rise} = R C_S \ (\text{or } \sqrt{L_S C_S} \text{ with the stray inductance } L_S). \]

A long flat pulse sags exponentially with time constant \(\tau_{droop}\), because a transformer cannot pass DC; a sharp edge is rounded over \(\tau_{rise}\).

Worked numbers (GSI). The GSI passive transformer has a ring of cobalt-iron amorphous alloy with \(\mu_r \approx 10^5\) below 100 kHz, radii 70 and 90 mm, length 16 mm, 10 turns and a 50 Ω load (Forck, table 2.1). Then \(L = 8.0\) mH, \(\tau_{droop} = L/R = 0.16\) ms, against the measured 0.2 ms; the sensitivity \(R/N = 5\) V/A, against the quoted 4 V/A. Its bandwidth is 2 kHz to 300 MHz, with a rise time of 1 ns, enough to see single bunches extracted from the synchrotron. Thermal noise in the 50 Ω load over 300 MHz is \(\sqrt{4 k_B T R\,\Delta f} = 16\) µV at 300 K, equivalent to 4 µA of beam; the quoted resolution is 40 µA rms, so the amplifier, not the resistor, sets the floor.

The active transformer. For pulses of milliseconds the droop is too fast. An operational amplifier holds the secondary at virtual ground, so the load seen by the winding is only the wire’s resistance \(R_L\), about 1 Ω, and \(\tau_{droop} \approx L/R_L\); an extra winding compensates the known droop further. The GSI linear-accelerator transformer reads 1 µA to 100 mA with a droop under 0.5 per cent over a 5 ms pulse and a resolution of 0.2 µA rms over a 1 MHz bandwidth. These transformers, at the source, along the accelerator and in the ring, are the instruments most used in operation: they show where beam is lost.

The DC transformer. A beam stored for hours is a DC current, which no transformer passes. The DC current transformer (K. Unser’s paper of 1981 describes the high-resolution form) uses two identical rings driven into saturation, \(B_{sat} \approx 0.6\) T, twice in each cycle of a modulation current at 1 to 10 kHz, in opposite senses. Without beam the two rings’ flux changes cancel in a sense winding. A DC beam current biases both rings the same way, so one saturates earlier and the other later in each cycle, and the sum signal appears at twice the modulation frequency. A feedback current in a third winding cancels the beam’s field; that feedback current is the measurement. The GSI synchrotron’s DC transformer reads 300 µA to 1 A with a resolution of 2 µA, from DC to 20 kHz, with an offset drift under 15 µA a day free-running (Forck, table 2.3). The floor is set by Barkhausen noise, the jerky reorientation of magnetic domains as the cores are driven through saturation. Magnetic shielding, a ceramic break in the pipe so that the image current flows outside the ring, and a low-magnetostriction core so that pump vibration is not read as current, are all needed.

The wall-current monitor. Break the pipe with a ceramic gap and bridge it with \(n\) resistors of value \(R\) around the circumference. The image current flows through them in parallel and makes \(U = (R/n)\,I_{beam}\). With 10 to 100 resistors of 10 to 100 Ω, \(R/n \approx 1\) Ω, so 1 A of bunch current gives 1 V. Ferrite rings outside force the current through the resistors and set the low cut-off near 10 kHz; the gap’s capacitance sets the high one at several GHz. It shows the bunch shape directly, down to bunch lengths of a nanosecond, and is the usual instrument for watching bunches in a proton synchrotron (chapter 19).

Pre-requisite: Transmission lines, matching, S-parameters

Pre-requisite. How a fast signal travels along a cable, or along an electrode beside the beam: the transmission line, described by its inductance and capacitance per metre; why it carries a pulse of any shape unchanged at the speed \(1/\sqrt{L'C'}\), with voltage and current in the fixed ratio \(Z_0\), its characteristic impedance; what happens at the far end, where a mismatch reflects part of the pulse and a matched load absorbs all of it; and how long a pulse takes to travel. Section 5 uses all of it for 50 Ω cables and for the stripline monitor. (S-parameters, the third topic of this heading, are not needed in this chapter.) Taught from Lee, chapter 1, exercise 13 (capacitance and inductance per metre of a coaxial line, \(R_c = \sqrt{L/C}\), and a table of common cables) and chapter 3, §VI.2 and exercise 3.6.3 (the lossless line’s equations and its travelling waves); Kuffel, Zaengl and Kuffel, §3.6.5 (a divider’s lead as a line with a surge impedance and a travel time); and Chao and Tigner, Handbook, §7.4.2 (the response of a stripline monitor).

The picture. Two conductors run side by side: the inner and outer of a coaxial cable, or a metal strip and the beam-pipe wall. A current going out along one returns along the other. Each short length \(dz\) has an inductance \(L'\,dz\) in series, because the current makes a magnetic field between the conductors, and a capacitance \(C'\,dz\) between them, because they carry opposite charges. \(L'\) (H/m) and \(C'\) (F/m) are the inductance and capacitance per metre.

The equations. Across a length \(dz\) the voltage falls by the inductive drop, \(L'\,dz\,\partial I/\partial t\), and the current falls by what is spent charging the capacitance, \(C'\,dz\,\partial V/\partial t\). Dividing by \(dz\) (with partial derivatives, since \(V\) and \(I\) depend on both \(z\) and \(t\)):

\[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial z} = -L'\frac{\partial I}{\partial t}, \qquad \frac{\partial I}{\partial z} = -C'\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}. \]

Differentiate the first with respect to \(z\) and use the second:

\[ \frac{\partial^2V}{\partial z^2} = L'C'\,\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial t^2}. \]

This is the wave equation, the same as for a stretched string. Any function of the form \(V = f(t - z/v)\), with \(v = 1/\sqrt{L'C'}\), satisfies it, whatever the shape of \(f\) (substitute and see: each \(z\)-derivative brings \(-1/v\), each \(t\)-derivative 1). A pulse travels down the line at speed \(v\) without changing shape. Put \(V = f(t - z/v)\) into the second equation: \(\partial I/\partial z = -C'f'\), so \(I = C'vf = f/Z_0\), with

\[ Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}} \quad (\Omega). \]

For a wave travelling forward, voltage and current are in the fixed ratio \(Z_0\); for one travelling backward, \(f(t + z/v)\), \(I = -V/Z_0\). A source connected to a long line therefore sees a pure resistance \(Z_0\), although the line dissipates nothing: the energy goes away down the line.

The coaxial line. For inner radius \(a\), outer radius \(b\) and an insulator of relative permittivity \(\varepsilon_r\), Gauss’s law gives the capacitance and Ampère’s law the field \(\mu_0I/(2\pi r)\) between the conductors, whose flux per metre gives the inductance (chapter 1 for the coaxial field):

\[ C' = \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r}{\ln(b/a)}, \qquad L' = \frac{\mu_0}{2\pi}\ln\frac{b}{a}. \]

Then \(L'C' = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r\), so \(v = c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}\), independent of the geometry, and

\[ Z_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\mu_0}{\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r}}\,\ln\frac{b}{a} = \frac{59.96\ \Omega}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\ln\frac{b}{a}. \]

At work. A 50 Ω cable with polyethylene (\(\varepsilon_r = 2.25\)) needs \(\ln(b/a) = 1.25\), \(b/a = 3.49\); signals travel at \(0.667c\), 5.0 ns per metre; \(C' = 100\) pF/m and \(L' = 0.25\) µH/m. Lee’s table gives 93.5 pF/m for RG58/U; with \(Z_0 = 50\) Ω that implies \(L' = Z_0^2C' = 0.23\) µH/m and a delay of 4.7 ns/m. A 50 Ω line in vacuum needs \(b/a = 2.30\). Thirty metres of cable from the tunnel to the electronics delay a signal by 150 ns, which is why bunch-by-bunch electronics must know its cable lengths to the centimetre.

The end of the line. A line of impedance \(Z_0\) ends in a load \(Z_L\). At the end, the incident wave \(V_i\) and a reflected wave \(V_r\) together must obey the load’s own law, \(V = Z_LI\):

\[ V_i + V_r = Z_L\,\frac{V_i - V_r}{Z_0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Gamma = \frac{V_r}{V_i} = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0}. \]

  • Matched, \(Z_L = Z_0\): \(\Gamma = 0\). The load takes the pulse exactly as an endless line would. Nothing comes back.
  • Open, \(Z_L \to \infty\): \(\Gamma = +1\); the pulse returns with the same sign.
  • Shorted, \(Z_L = 0\): \(\Gamma = -1\); it returns inverted.
  • A 75 Ω load on a 50 Ω line: \(\Gamma = 0.2\), so 4 per cent of the power comes back.

It is the same rule as for a wave on a string meeting a change of impedance (F5, section 11). A line mismatched at both ends rings: echoes bounce back and forth with period \(2l/v\) and spoil the waveform. This is why pick-ups, cables and amplifiers are all built for one impedance, 50 Ω, and terminated in it; Kuffel’s divider leads ring for the same reason unless damped.

The stripline monitor. A metal strip of length \(l\), covering an angle \(\varphi\) of the pipe’s circumference, forms with the pipe wall a line designed for \(Z_0 = 50\) Ω, with a 50 Ω port at each end. In vacuum its waves travel at \(c\), the speed of a relativistic bunch. Follow a bunch through.

  1. The bunch reaches the upstream end. Its image current, a fraction \(\varphi/2\pi\) of it, must now flow on the strip. Looking into the strip from that point, the current sees two lines of \(Z_0\), the upstream port and the strip running downstream, and splits equally. Half goes out of the upstream port at once: a voltage \(Z_0(\varphi/4\pi)I_{beam}\).
  2. The other half travels down the strip at \(c\), keeping pace with the bunch.
  3. At the downstream end the bunch leaves the strip and its image current leaves too. That launches an equal and opposite pair of waves. The one going downstream cancels exactly the wave that arrived with the bunch: nothing comes out of the downstream port. The one going upstream travels back and leaves the upstream port, inverted, a time \(2l/c\) after the first pulse.

So the upstream port gives a doublet (Chao and Tigner),

\[ V(t) = \frac{\varphi Z_0}{4\pi}\left[I_{beam}(t) - I_{beam}\!\left(t - \frac{2l}{c}\right)\right], \]

and the downstream port gives nothing. A bunch travelling the other way gives its doublet at the other port: the stripline is directional, which is why colliders use it to tell their two beams apart. The response has zeros at frequencies that are multiples of \(c/(2l)\).

At work. A strip 15 cm long gives pulses 1.0 ns apart and nulls at multiples of 1.0 GHz. For an electron bunch of \(10^{10}\) particles with rms length 3 mm, the peak current is \(Ne c/(\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_z) = 64\) A (the Gaussian peak); a strip covering 60° gives \(\varphi Z_0/4\pi = 4.2\) Ω, so a pulse of about 270 V lasting some tens of picoseconds, which cables and filters then lengthen and shrink.

Answer

\(\ln(b/a) = 50\sqrt{2.1}/59.96 = 1.208\), \(b/a = 3.35\). Delay \(\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}/c = 4.8\) ns/m.

Exercise K2. A pulse of 1 V on a 50 Ω line meets a 25 Ω load. What is reflected, and what voltage appears across the load?

Answer

\(\Gamma = (25 - 50)/(25 + 50) = -1/3\): a pulse of \(-0.33\) V comes back. The load sees \(V_i + V_r = 0.67\) V.

Exercise K3. A stripline is 10 cm long. When does its second pulse arrive, and at what frequency is its first null?

Answer

\(2l/c = 0.2/3.00 \times 10^8 = 0.67\) ns after the first; the first null is at \(c/(2l) = 1.5\) GHz. Shorter strips give faster doublets, with their best response at higher frequency.

Pre-requisite (continued): Resonant cavity modes

Pre-requisite. This continues Resonant cavity modes in chapter 7, which described a standing wave in a cavity and the modes of a chain of cells. It adds the field patterns and frequencies of a pillbox cavity’s two lowest modes, the monopole TM\(_{010}\) and the dipole TM\(_{110}\), and shows why a passing bunch excites the dipole mode in proportion to its offset from the axis and not at all when it is centred. Section 5 uses this for the cavity position monitor; chapter 13 treats cavities in full. Taught from Wangler, §1.12 (the TM\(_{010}\) mode of a pillbox), §11.17 (the TM\(_{110}\) field components) and §10.1 (the fundamental theorem of beam loading), Lee, chapter 3, §VI.1 (the pillbox cavity), and Chao and Tigner, Handbook, §7.4.2 (cavity position monitors).

Naming the modes. A pillbox is a closed metal cylinder of radius \(R_c\) and length \(l_c\). Its resonant fields with only a longitudinal electric field, \(\mathcal{E}_z\), are the transverse-magnetic (TM) modes. The labels TM\(_{mnp}\) count variations: \(m\) round the azimuth, \(n\) the radial zero that meets the wall, \(p\) half-waves along the axis (\(p = 0\) means uniform along \(z\)). In each mode the radial shape of \(\mathcal{E}_z\) is a Bessel function \(J_m(kr)\): the solution, finite on the axis, of the radial part of the wave equation in cylindrical coordinates. \(J_0\) starts at 1 on the axis and first crosses zero at \(kr = 2.405\); \(J_1\) starts at 0, rises as \(kr/2\) near the axis, peaks at 0.582 at \(kr = 1.84\), and first crosses zero at \(kr = 3.832\). The tangential \(\mathcal{E}_z\) must vanish on the metal wall at \(r = R_c\), so \(kR_c\) must be one of these zeros, and the frequency is \(f = kc/(2\pi)\).

TM\(_{010}\), the monopole. \(\mathcal{E}_z = \mathcal{E}_0J_0(kr)\cos\omega t\) with \(k = 2.405/R_c\), so

\[ f_{010} = \frac{2.405\,c}{2\pi R_c}, \]

independent of the length. The field is strongest on the axis and the same all round: a centred and an off-centre bunch excite it almost equally. It is the accelerating mode of chapter 13, and in a position monitor it measures the bunch’s charge and gives the reference phase.

TM\(_{110}\), the dipole. (Wangler, §11.17)

\[ \mathcal{E}_z = \mathcal{E}_0J_1(kr)\cos\phi\,\cos\omega t, \qquad k = \frac{3.832}{R_c}, \qquad f_{110} = \frac{3.832\,c}{2\pi R_c}. \]

\(\mathcal{E}_z\) is zero on the axis and has opposite signs on the two sides; the magnetic field on the axis is transverse, which is why this mode deflects beams and is the cause of beam breakup (Wangler, §11.17). There are two such modes, with \(\cos\phi\) and \(\sin\phi\), one for each transverse plane. Near the axis \(J_1(kr) \approx kr/2\), so

\[ \mathcal{E}_z \approx \mathcal{E}_0\,\frac{k}{2}\,r\cos\phi = \mathcal{E}_0\,\frac{k}{2}\,x : \]

the dipole mode’s longitudinal field grows linearly with \(x\). The ratio \(f_{110}/f_{010} = 3.832/2.405 = 1.59\).

Why the excitation is proportional to the offset. A charge \(q\) crossing an empty cavity leaves energy in each mode. Wangler’s §10.1 shows, by energy conservation alone, that the voltage \(V_b\) it leaves in a mode is proportional to \(q\), and that the charge itself loses \(qV_b/2\), half what a second charge would see. The stored energy in the mode is \(U = \alpha V_b^2\), where \(V_b\) is measured along the charge’s own path and \(\alpha\) depends on the mode’s shape and on where the path runs. Energy conservation, \(U = qV_b/2\), then gives \(V_b = q/(2\alpha)\) and

\[ U = \frac{q^2}{4\alpha}. \]

For TM\(_{110}\) at fixed amplitude, the voltage along a path at offset \(x\) is proportional to \(x\) (since \(\mathcal{E}_z\) is), so \(\alpha\), stored energy over voltage squared, goes as \(1/x^2\). Hence \(U \propto q^2x^2\), and the mode’s field amplitude, proportional to \(\sqrt{U}\), goes as \(qx\): linear in the offset, zero on the axis, and changing sign when the beam crosses to the other side. A probe coupled to the dipole field reads \(qx\); a second, monopole cavity reads \(q\) and the phase; their ratio is \(x\), with its sign from the relative phase (Chao and Tigner, §7.4.2).

At work. A cavity monitor whose dipole mode is at 6.5 GHz has \(R_c = 3.832c/(2\pi \times 6.5\ \mathrm{GHz}) = 28.1\) mm. Its own monopole mode is at 4.08 GHz, and a beam 1 µm off axis excites it about ten thousand times more strongly in amplitude; it is kept out of the reading by its different frequency and by couplers that pick up only the dipole field’s pattern. For a beam 1 µm off axis, \(J_1(kx)\) is \(1.2 \times 10^{-4}\) of its peak. A button pair at \(a = 25\) mm sees a comparable fraction, \(\Delta U/\Sigma U = 2x/a = 8 \times 10^{-5}\), but as a small difference between two large signals, each with its own gain error and drift. In the cavity the micrometre signal is the whole signal, and amplifying it amplifies nothing else; that is the source of the cavity monitor’s resolution.

Exercise L1. What radius gives a pillbox a TM\(_{010}\) frequency of 1.3 GHz, and where is its TM\(_{110}\) mode?

Answer

\(R_c = 2.405c/(2\pi f) = 2.405 \times 3.00 \times 10^8/(2\pi \times 1.3 \times 10^9) = 88.3\) mm. TM\(_{110}\) is at \(1.3 \times 3.832/2.405 = 2.07\) GHz.

Exercise L2. A beam moves from 1 µm to 10 µm off axis in a cavity monitor. By what factors do the dipole mode’s amplitude and stored energy change?

Answer

Amplitude goes as \(x\): a factor 10. Stored energy goes as \(x^2\): a factor 100. Electronics read amplitude, so the response is linear in position.

Exercise L3. Why can a centred beam not excite TM\(_{110}\), whatever its charge?

Answer

On the axis \(\mathcal{E}_z\) of the TM\(_{110}\) mode is zero, so the voltage along a centred path is zero, \(\alpha\) is infinite and the energy left, \(q^2/(4\alpha)\), is zero. In words: the mode’s field is odd in \(x\), and a charge on the axis pushes the two halves equally and oppositely.

5. Beam position monitors

The difference over sum. Put two small electrodes, buttons, in the wall at azimuths 0 and \(\pi\), each of angular width \(\alpha\), and let the beam be at \((r, \theta)\) with \(r \ll a\). Expand the image-current density to first order in \(r/a\):

\[ j_{im}(\phi) \approx \frac{I_{beam}}{2\pi a}\left[1 + \frac{2r}{a}\cos(\phi - \theta)\right]. \]

The current on the electrode at \(\phi = 0\) is \(a\int_{-\alpha/2}^{\alpha/2} j_{im}\,d\phi\) (\(a\,d\phi\) is the length of wall in \(d\phi\)). The 1 integrates to \(\alpha\). For the second term, \(\int_{-\alpha/2}^{\alpha/2}\cos(\phi - \theta)\,d\phi = \sin(\alpha/2 - \theta) + \sin(\alpha/2 + \theta) = 2\sin(\alpha/2)\cos\theta\), by the sum formula for sines. So

\[ I_R = \frac{I_{beam}\alpha}{2\pi}\left[1 + \frac{2r}{a}\cos\theta\,\frac{\sin(\alpha/2)}{\alpha/2}\right], \]

and on the electrode at \(\phi = \pi\) the same with the sign of the second term reversed. The voltages are proportional to these currents, so with \(x = r\cos\theta\),

\[ \frac{\Delta U}{\Sigma U} = \frac{U_R - U_L}{U_R + U_L} = \frac{4\sin(\alpha/2)}{\alpha}\,\frac{x}{a} \equiv S\,x, \]

where \(S\) is the position sensitivity (per metre; usually quoted in per cent per millimetre). For small buttons \(S \to 2/a\). Dividing by the sum removes the beam’s intensity and bunch shape: the ratio depends only on geometry. This is why the difference over sum, not the difference, is the standard.

A worked number. For \(a = 25\) mm and \(\alpha = 30^\circ\), \(S = 7.91\) per cent per millimetre. Evaluating the exact integral of \(j_{im}\) shows where the linear formula fails: a beam at \(x = 5\) mm is read as 4.81 mm, at 10 mm as 8.62 mm; and a beam on the diagonal at \(x = y = 5\) mm is read as \(x = 4.66\) mm. Forck fits \(S = 7.4\) per cent per millimetre over the central region for this geometry. Beyond about a quarter of the radius a correction map, computed or measured on a test bench with a wire carrying a signal, is applied. A “shoe-box” monitor, a box cut diagonally into two triangular electrodes, is linear over its whole width, with \(S = 1/a\) for half-aperture \(a\); it suits the large beams of proton rings.

Signal size. A button of radius \(r_b\) covers an area \(\pi r_b^2\) of the circumference \(2\pi a\), so it carries an image charge \(\lambda\,\pi r_b^2/(2\pi a)\), with \(\lambda\) the beam’s charge per unit length (C/m), \(\lambda = I_{beam}/(\beta c)\). As the bunch passes this charge changes, so for a bunch long compared with the button it delivers a current proportional to the time derivative of the beam current, into a 50 Ω cable. For an assumed Gaussian bunch of \(10^{11}\) protons with rms length 10 cm, buttons 20 mm across in a pipe of radius 25 mm, the peak bunch current is 19 A and the peak button voltage about 12 V; for \(10^9\) particles, about 0.12 V; a weak beam gives millivolts. The thermal noise of 50 Ω over 100 MHz is 9 µV, so the noise in the resistor is rarely the limit: in practice the electronics, the cables, temperature drifts and the electrical and mechanical offset of the electrodes are. Forck quotes about 100 µm bunch by bunch with broadband processing in electron machines, and about 1 µm when the signal is filtered to a narrow band around one harmonic of the bunch frequency.

Stripline monitors. For short relativistic bunches the electrode is made a strip of length \(l\), forming with the pipe a transmission line of 50 Ω matched at both ends. A passing bunch induces a pulse at the upstream port; a second pulse of opposite sign follows a time \(2l/c\) later, when the image charge leaves the strip. A bunch travelling the other way gives its signal at the other port. This directivity is why striplines are used in colliders, where two beams pass the same monitor in opposite directions.

Cavity monitors. A beam passing off centre through a pillbox cavity excites its dipole mode, TM\(_{110}\), with an amplitude proportional to its offset times its charge, and nothing at all when centred. A second cavity’s monopole mode, TM\(_{010}\), gives the charge and the phase reference. Because the dipole signal vanishes at the centre instead of being a small difference of large signals, cavity monitors reach the best resolution, and are used where beams are smallest, in linear colliders and free-electron lasers (chapter 25, chapter 26). Cavities are treated in chapter 13.

Pre-requisite: Angular distribution and spectrum of radiation

Pre-requisite. How to describe radiation by direction and by frequency: the energy radiated per unit angular frequency and per unit solid angle, \(d^2W/(d\omega\,d\Omega)\); how it follows from the radiation field of an accelerated charge; why a charge whose velocity changes suddenly radiates a flat spectrum; why a relativistic particle’s radiation lies within an angle of about \(1/\gamma\) of its direction; and how to turn energy into a number of photons, \(dN = dW/(\hbar\omega)\). Section 6 uses all of it for optical transition radiation, and derives its formula here; chapter 20 builds synchrotron radiation on the same ground. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 2, §66 (the field at large distances and the intensity per solid angle), §69 (radiation of low frequency in collisions, where the velocity changes suddenly) and §73 (radiation from a rapidly moving charge), and Tong, Electromagnetism, §7.4 (radiation from a single particle).

Energy carried off. Far from a source the field is a wave: \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are perpendicular to each other and to the direction \(\mathbf{n}\) of travel, and \(B = \mathcal{E}/c\). The energy flow, the Poynting vector, then has size \(S = \mathcal{E}B/\mu_0 = \varepsilon_0c\mathcal{E}^2\) (W/m\(^2\)). A solid angle is to a sphere what an angle is to a circle: a patch of area \(A\) on a sphere of radius \(R\) subtends \(A/R^2\) steradians (sr), and the whole sphere is \(4\pi\) sr (chapter 1 used it for cross-sections). So a detector at distance \(R\) subtending a solid angle \(d\Omega\) has area \(R^2d\Omega\), so the energy per unit solid angle (J/sr) that passes it is

\[ \frac{dW}{d\Omega} = \varepsilon_0c\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(RE(t)\right)^2dt . \]

The radiation field falls as \(1/R\), so \(RE\) does not depend on the distance.

The radiation field of a charge. For a point charge \(q\) moving with velocity \(\boldsymbol{\beta}c\) and acceleration \(\dot{\boldsymbol\beta}c\), the part of its field that falls as \(1/R\) is (Landau and Lifshitz, §63 and §73; Tong, §7.4.3)

\[ \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{rad} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0cR}\,\frac{\mathbf{n}\times\left[(\mathbf{n} - \boldsymbol\beta)\times\dot{\boldsymbol\beta}\right]}{(1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta)^3}, \]

with everything on the right evaluated at the retarded time \(t'\), when the light now arriving left the charge: \(t = t' + R(t')/c\). Two features matter here. Only acceleration radiates: a charge in uniform motion has no \(1/R\) field, which is why the field of the moving charge travels with it and is not radiated. And the denominator \((1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta)^3\) becomes tiny for directions close to the velocity when \(\beta \to 1\): for a small angle \(\theta\) from the velocity, \(1 - \beta\cos\theta \approx (\gamma^{-2} + \theta^2)/2\). Radiation from a fast particle is thrown forward into a cone of angle about \(1/\gamma\).

Splitting by frequency. Write the pulse \(RE(t)\) as a sum of oscillations. Its Fourier transform \(A(\omega) = \int RE(t)\,e^{i\omega t}dt\) says how much of each angular frequency it contains, and Parseval’s theorem (proved in Fourier series and transforms, before section 9) splits the time integral of its square among the frequencies: \(\int(RE)^2dt = (1/\pi)\int_0^\infty|A(\omega)|^2d\omega\). Therefore

\[ \frac{d^2W}{d\omega\,d\Omega} = \frac{\varepsilon_0c}{\pi}\,|A(\omega)|^2 , \]

the energy per unit angular frequency (J per rad/s) per steradian, counting positive frequencies only. Integrate over all directions for the spectrum \(dW/d\omega\); integrate over frequency for the angular distribution.

A sudden change of velocity. Change the integration variable in \(A(\omega)\) from the observer’s time \(t\) to the charge’s time \(t'\), with \(dt = (1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta)\,dt'\). One power of \((1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta)\) cancels, and what remains is an exact time derivative (check by differentiating the right side, with \(\mathbf{n}\) fixed):

\[ \frac{\mathbf{n}\times\left[(\mathbf{n} - \boldsymbol\beta)\times\dot{\boldsymbol\beta}\right]}{(1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta)^2} = \frac{d}{dt'}\left[\frac{\mathbf{n}\times(\mathbf{n}\times\boldsymbol\beta)}{1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta}\right]. \]

So

\[ A(\omega) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c}\int\frac{d}{dt'}\left[\frac{\mathbf{n}\times(\mathbf{n}\times\boldsymbol\beta)}{1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta}\right]e^{i\omega t}\,dt' . \]

Suppose the velocity changes from \(\boldsymbol\beta_1\) to \(\boldsymbol\beta_2\) in a time short compared with \(1/\omega\) (as the observer sees it). Then \(e^{i\omega t}\) hardly changes during the change, can be taken outside, and the integral of a derivative is the difference of the end values (Landau and Lifshitz, §69):

\[ \frac{d^2W}{d\omega\,d\Omega} = \frac{q^2}{16\pi^3\varepsilon_0c}\left|\frac{\mathbf{n}\times(\mathbf{n}\times\boldsymbol\beta_2)}{1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta_2} - \frac{\mathbf{n}\times(\mathbf{n}\times\boldsymbol\beta_1)}{1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\boldsymbol\beta_1}\right|^2 . \]

The frequency has dropped out: a sudden change radiates a flat spectrum, up to frequencies of order one over the duration of the change.

A charge that stops. Take \(\boldsymbol\beta_1 = \beta\hat{\mathbf{z}}\) and \(\boldsymbol\beta_2 = 0\), and let \(\theta\) be the angle between \(\mathbf{n}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{z}}\). The size of \(\mathbf{n}\times(\mathbf{n}\times\boldsymbol\beta_1)\) is \(\beta\sin\theta\), so

\[ \frac{d^2W}{d\omega\,d\Omega} = \frac{q^2}{16\pi^3\varepsilon_0c}\,\frac{\beta^2\sin^2\theta}{(1 - \beta\cos\theta)^2} \approx \frac{q^2}{4\pi^3\varepsilon_0c}\,\frac{\theta^2}{(\gamma^{-2} + \theta^2)^2} \]

for \(\gamma \gg 1\) and small \(\theta\). Nothing goes exactly forward (\(\sin\theta = 0\)); setting the derivative of \(\theta^2/(\gamma^{-2} + \theta^2)^2\) to zero gives the peak at \(\theta = 1/\gamma\), where the function equals \(\gamma^2/4\). This is the hollow cone of half-angle \(1/\gamma\).

Transition radiation. A charge crosses from vacuum into a perfect conductor. Inside the metal the field is zero. Outside, before the crossing, the field is that of the charge plus its image (chapter 4’s method): a charge \(-q\) at the mirror point, moving towards the surface with the mirrored velocity. At the moment of crossing both meet at the surface, and from then on the vacuum side holds no field: as far as the vacuum is concerned, both charges have stopped suddenly. Look back into the vacuum along a direction \(\mathbf{n}\) at angle \(\theta\) from the backward normal. Along the line of sight the real charge’s velocity component is \(-\beta\cos\theta\) and the image’s is \(+\beta\cos\theta\); the image’s velocity and its charge are both reversed, so its term in the formula above has the same sign as the real charge’s. Their two terms add:

\[ \beta\sin\theta\left[\frac{1}{1 + \beta\cos\theta} + \frac{1}{1 - \beta\cos\theta}\right] = \frac{2\beta\sin\theta}{1 - \beta^2\cos^2\theta}, \]

and so

\[ \frac{d^2W}{d\omega\,d\Omega} = \frac{q^2}{4\pi^3\varepsilon_0c}\,\frac{\beta^2\sin^2\theta}{(1 - \beta^2\cos^2\theta)^2} \approx \frac{q^2}{4\pi^3\varepsilon_0c}\,\frac{\theta^2}{(\gamma^{-2} + \theta^2)^2}, \]

the formula of section 6 (there quoted from Jackson). The light comes back in a hollow cone of half-angle \(1/\gamma\) about the backward direction; for a foil at 45° to the beam, the backward direction is the direction of mirror reflection, 90° from the beam. The spectrum is flat as long as the metal behaves as a perfect conductor, which is up to its plasma frequency (the next section).

From energy to photons. Light of angular frequency \(\omega\) comes in photons of energy \(\hbar\omega\). The number of photons in a band is the energy in it divided by the energy of each:

\[ dN = \frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\,\frac{dW}{d\omega}\,d\omega . \]

A flat \(dW/d\omega\) thus gives a number that falls as \(1/\omega\), and \(N \propto \int d\omega/\omega = \ln(\omega_2/\omega_1) = \ln(\lambda_1/\lambda_2)\): equal numbers in each octave. With \(e^2/(4\pi^3\varepsilon_0c) = \alpha_f\hbar/\pi^2\), where \(\alpha_f = e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c) = 1/137.036\) is the fine-structure constant, the transition radiation of one electron is

\[ \frac{d^2N}{d\omega\,d\Omega} = \frac{\alpha_f}{\pi^2\omega}\,\frac{\beta^2\sin^2\theta}{(1 - \beta^2\cos^2\theta)^2}. \]

Integrated over the backward hemisphere, the angular factor gives \(2\pi[\ln(2\gamma) - \tfrac12]\) for large \(\gamma\) (checked numerically: 30.076 against 30.076 at 50 MeV), so the photons between wavelengths \(\lambda_2\) and \(\lambda_1\) number

\[ N = \frac{2\alpha_f}{\pi}\left[\ln(2\gamma) - \frac12\right]\ln\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2}. \]

At work. One photon of green light, 500 nm, carries 2.48 eV, so 1 nJ of it is \(2.5 \times 10^9\) photons. For transition radiation between 400 and 700 nm:

Pre-requisite: Debye shielding and plasma frequency

Pre-requisite. The plasma frequency \(\omega_p = \sqrt{n_ee^2/(\varepsilon_0m_e)}\) of a gas of free electrons: the frequency at which the electrons oscillate when pushed aside, and the frequency below which a metal reflects an electromagnetic wave and above which it lets it in. Section 6 uses it as the upper limit of the flat spectrum of transition radiation from a metal foil; the same frequency limits which microwaves can enter the plasma of an ion source. (Debye shielding, the other half of this heading, is not needed in this chapter.) Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, §8.6.1 to §8.6.3 (the Drude model, electromagnetic waves in conductors, plasma oscillations); it uses the field of a displaced slab of charge from chapter 1.

Free electrons. In a metal the outer electrons of each atom leave it and move freely through the lattice of positive ions, which they neutralise on average. Their number density \(n_e\) (m\(^{-3}\)) is the number of atoms per cubic metre times the electrons each gives: aluminium (2700 kg/m\(^3\), 26.98 g/mol, three electrons per atom) has \(n_e = 1.81 \times 10^{29}\) m\(^{-3}\); copper (8960 kg/m\(^3\), 63.55 g/mol, one per atom) \(8.49 \times 10^{28}\) m\(^{-3}\). A plasma in an ion source is the same in kind, a neutral mixture of free electrons and ions, at far lower density, \(10^{16}\) to \(10^{18}\) m\(^{-3}\).

The oscillation. Shift all the electrons in a slab sideways by a small distance \(\xi\), holding the ions still. One face is left with uncovered positive ions and the other gains extra electrons: surface charges \(\pm n_ee\xi\) per unit area, whose field inside the slab is \(n_ee\xi/\varepsilon_0\), as for chapter 1’s displaced slab. Each electron feels a force \(-e\mathcal{E}\) pulling it back:

\[ m_e\ddot\xi = -\frac{n_ee^2}{\varepsilon_0}\,\xi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot\xi = -\omega_p^2\xi, \qquad \omega_p = \sqrt{\frac{n_ee^2}{\varepsilon_0m_e}} . \]

The electron gas has a natural frequency of its own, the plasma frequency. It depends only on the density.

A wave meets the electrons. Let a wave with field \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}e^{-i\omega t}\) act on the free electrons, ignoring collisions for the moment. Each obeys \(m_e\ddot{\mathbf{x}} = -e\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\), so \(\mathbf{x} = e\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}/(m_e\omega^2)\): the electrons move against the force’s direction at every instant, half a cycle out of step. The polarisation, the dipole moment per volume, is

\[ \mathbf{P} = -n_ee\mathbf{x} = -\frac{n_ee^2}{m_e\omega^2}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\varepsilon_0\,\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}, \]

so the electron gas has a relative permittivity

\[ \varepsilon_r(\omega) = 1 - \frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2}. \]

A wave in a medium has wavenumber \(k\) with \(k^2 = \varepsilon_r\omega^2/c^2\), that is

\[ c^2k^2 = \omega^2 - \omega_p^2 . \]

  • Above the plasma frequency, \(\omega > \omega_p\): \(k\) is real and the wave travels through. The metal is transparent.
  • Below it, \(\omega < \omega_p\): \(k^2\) is negative, \(k = i\kappa\) with \(\kappa = \sqrt{\omega_p^2 - \omega^2}/c\), and the field inside dies away as \(e^{-\kappa z}\) over a depth of about \(c/\omega_p\). No energy can be carried in, so the wave is reflected. The electrons move so as to cancel the field inside, which is just what a perfect conductor does.

Collisions, which the simple picture leaves out, matter only if an electron collides within one cycle. In metals the time between collisions is about \(10^{-14}\) s, against a cycle of \(1.7 \times 10^{-15}\) s at 500 nm (\(\omega\tau \approx 38\)), so in the visible they can be ignored. At low frequencies they dominate and the metal behaves as an ordinary resistive conductor, which also reflects.

At work: the foil. For aluminium, \(\omega_p = 2.40 \times 10^{16}\) rad/s, \(\hbar\omega_p = 15.8\) eV, and the corresponding wavelength \(2\pi c/\omega_p = 79\) nm, in the far ultraviolet. At 500 nm, \(\varepsilon_r = -39.5\) and the field reaches only \(1/\kappa = 12.7\) nm into the metal. A foil of 1 µm, about 80 such depths thick, therefore acts as a perfect mirror and a perfect conductor across the whole visible band: the transition radiation of the section above is flat there. Above \(\omega_p\) the foil lets the field through, the sudden change of section 6’s derivation no longer happens, and the radiation falls away. (For copper the simple model gives \(\hbar\omega_p = 10.8\) eV, but its colour shows that bound electrons absorb in the visible too: the free-electron picture is cruder for copper than for aluminium, one reason aluminium foils are used.)

At work: an ion source. A plasma of \(10^{17}\) electrons per cubic metre has \(f_p = \omega_p/2\pi = 2.8\) GHz. Microwaves of 2.45 GHz, the frequency of many microwave ion sources (chapter 7), cannot enter a plasma denser than the critical density \(n_c = \varepsilon_0m_e\omega^2/e^2 = 7.4 \times 10^{16}\) m\(^{-3}\); they are reflected from its surface as light is from aluminium.

6. Profiles

Screens. A plate that glows where the beam hits it, watched by a camera. It is the most direct of all instruments and is found everywhere from the source to the target. It intercepts the beam and is several millimetres thick, so it is used in transfer lines and at the end of linear machines, never in a ring’s circulating beam. Properties of common materials (Forck, table 3.1):

Material Composition Peak emission Decay time
Chromolux Al\(_2\)O\(_3\):Cr 700 nm 100 ms
YAG Y\(_3\)Al\(_5\)O\(_{12}\):Ce 550 nm 0.2 µs
P43 phosphor Gd\(_2\)O\(_2\)S:Tb 545 nm 1 ms
P46 phosphor Y\(_3\)Al\(_5\)O\(_{12}\):Ce powder 530 nm 0.3 µs

A screen must give light in proportion to the energy deposited. It does not when it saturates, when its light is absorbed and scattered inside it, or when it is damaged. A YAG crystal at GSI showed a beam of only about \(10^6\) ions at 4 MeV per nucleon. The doped alumina ceramic Chromolux is robust and much used, but slow, and absorbs its own light. Phosphor powders on glass or metal are cheap in large sizes. The zinc sulphide of the spinthariscope and the willemite of the oscilloscope (chapter 2) belong to the same family.

Optical transition radiation. When a charged particle crosses a boundary between vacuum and a metal, its field must rearrange from the vacuum form to the form inside the metal, and the change radiates. Ginzburg and Frank predicted this in 1945; Goldsmith and Jelley observed it in the visible in 1959. For a perfectly conducting foil, the energy radiated backwards per unit frequency and solid angle at angle \(\theta\) from the reflected direction is, in the relativistic limit (quoted; see Jackson, Classical Electrodynamics, chapter 13),

\[ \frac{d^2 W}{d\omega\, d\Omega} = \frac{e^2}{4\pi^3\varepsilon_0 c}\,\frac{\theta^2}{\left(\gamma^{-2} + \theta^2\right)^2}. \]

It is flat in frequency up to the plasma frequency of the metal, so the number of photons in a band is \(dN = (dW/d\omega)\,d\omega/\hbar\omega\), proportional to \(\ln(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)\). It peaks at \(\theta = 1/\gamma\). Integrating the exact form over the hemisphere, an electron gives, between 400 and 700 nm, 0.0124 photons at 50 MeV, 0.0142 at 100 MeV and 0.0202 at 1 GeV: one photon for 81, 70 and 49 electrons. A lens that accepts 50 mrad collects one photon for about 340 electrons at 50 MeV and about 94 at 1 GeV, consistent with Forck’s “100 to 1000 beam particles yield 1 photon”. The light is linear in the beam, independent of foil thickness (foils of 1 µm or less of aluminium are used, at 45° to the beam), and the foil scatters the beam very little. It is weak, so intensified cameras are needed, and protons give a useful cone only above about 10 GeV. Wartski and colleagues applied it to beam diagnostics in 1975.

Grids and wires. A grid of thin wires, about 1 mm apart, each read by its own amplifier, gives a profile at one instant from the secondary-electron current of each wire. A single wire swept through the beam gives better resolution with one channel, at the cost of taking the profile over time. The wire is carbon or silicon carbide, down to about 10 µm, chosen because \(dW/dx \propto Z/A\) and because carbon survives high temperatures; flying wires at CERN cross the beam at up to 10 m/s. At high energy the signal is the shower of secondaries from the wire, counted by a scintillator outside the pipe. The wire has a size of its own. For a cylinder of radius \(r_w\), weighting each position by the chord length,

\[ \sigma_w^2 = \frac{\int_{-r_w}^{r_w} u^2\,2\sqrt{r_w^2 - u^2}\,du}{\int_{-r_w}^{r_w} 2\sqrt{r_w^2 - u^2}\,du} = \frac{r_w^2}{4}, \]

so \(\sigma_{beam}^2 = \sigma_{meas}^2 - r_w^2/4\). A wire 4 µm in diameter (\(r_w = 2\) µm) adds 1 µm in quadrature: it changes a 10 µm beam by 0.5 per cent and a 1 µm beam by 41 per cent. With slow linear scanners and 4 µm wires, about 1 µm resolution has been reached at electron accelerators (Forck, section 3.3).

Residual-gas monitors. The beam ionises the gas left in the vacuum. A field of about 1 kV/cm sweeps the ions (or electrons) sideways onto a detector, which records where they were made, and so the beam’s profile, without touching the beam. Estimate the signal from the Bethe formula: hydrogen at \(10^{-9}\) mbar and 300 K has \(2.4 \times 10^{13}\) molecules per cubic metre; (from \(n = p/k_BT\), F6, section 10); with the minimum-ionising loss of 4.103 MeV cm\(^2\)/g and 37 eV per ion pair (the energy \(W_i\) spent for each pair, section 11), a proton makes \(9.0 \times 10^{-12}\) pairs per centimetre. For an assumed \(10^{11}\) protons circulating at 1 MHz past 10 cm of monitor, that is 9 ions per turn, \(9 \times 10^6\) a second, a current of 1.4 pA. So the ions are multiplied by a microchannel plate (a thin glass plate pierced by millions of channels about 10 µm across, each a tiny electron multiplier: a particle striking the channel wall knocks out electrons, which strike the wall further on and knock out more; gain about \(10^3\) per plate, \(10^6\) for two) and imaged on a phosphor. These monitors are in nearly every proton and ion synchrotron, where they show the beam shrinking during acceleration as the normalised emittance is conserved.

Synchrotron light. An electron bent in a dipole radiates, and imaging that light gives the profile without contact. Its resolution is limited by diffraction, because the light comes in a narrow cone: Forck gives a resolution \(\sigma_{res} \approx 0.6\,(\lambda^2\rho)^{1/3}\). At LEP, with a bending radius of 3.1 km, this is 350 µm at 250 nm, and the diffraction contribution there was about 300 µm. The light itself is chapter 20.

Pre-requisite (continued): Matrices, determinants, eigenvalues

Pre-requisite (continued). Chapter 7 first taught matrices and determinants; this section gathers, in the form beam optics uses them, the few facts about \(2 \times 2\) matrices that beam optics needs: a matrix acting on a column of two numbers, the product of two matrices and why its order matters, the transpose and the rule \((AB)^T = B^TA^T\), the determinant, its meaning as the factor by which a matrix changes areas, \(\det(AB) = \det A\det B\), and the inverse. Section 7 writes the beam’s size, divergence and correlation as a matrix and uses all of these; chapter 16 builds strong focusing on them. (Eigenvalues, the third topic of this heading, are not needed in this chapter.) Taught from Penrose, The Road to Reality, §13.3 (linear transformations and matrices) and §13.4 (determinants and traces), and Lee, chapter 2, §II.1 (transfer matrices) and §II.5 (the σ-matrix and its determinant).

A matrix acts on a column. In beam optics a particle’s transverse state is two numbers, its position \(x\) (m) and its angle \(x'\) (rad), written as a column

\[ X = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ x' \end{pmatrix}. \]

A \(2 \times 2\) matrix \(M\) turns it into a new column by the rule “row times column”:

\[ MX = \begin{pmatrix} m_{11} & m_{12} \\ m_{21} & m_{22} \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ x' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} m_{11}x + m_{12}x' \\ m_{21}x + m_{22}x' \end{pmatrix}. \]

Every new number is a fixed combination of the old ones, with no constant term and no squares. That is what linear means, and it is why matrices describe drifts and lenses: the response to two particles’ offsets added is the sum of the responses.

Products. Apply \(A\) first and then \(B\): \(B(AX)\). Working the rule through, this equals \((BA)X\), where the product matrix has elements

\[ (BA)_{ij} = \sum_k B_{ik}A_{kj}, \]

row \(i\) of \(B\) times column \(j\) of \(A\). The matrix applied first stands on the right. The order matters. A lens of focal length 2 m followed by a 5 m drift, and the same drift followed by the lens, give

\[ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 5 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -0.5 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1.5 & 5 \\ -0.5 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -0.5 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 5 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 5 \\ -0.5 & -1.5 \end{pmatrix}, \]

two different beam lines. (The meaning of these matrices is the next section; here they are just numbers.) A line of many elements is the product of their matrices, written from right to left in the order the beam meets them.

The transpose. \(M^T\) swaps rows and columns: \((M^T)_{ij} = M_{ji}\). The transpose of a column is a row, \(X^T = (x, x')\). Writing out the components shows

\[ (AB)^T = B^TA^T, \]

the order reversing. A matrix equal to its own transpose is symmetric. One symmetric matrix appears constantly in section 7: the column times its own transpose,

\[ XX^T = \begin{pmatrix} x^2 & xx' \\ x'x & x'^2 \end{pmatrix}, \]

whose average over all the particles of a beam is the beam matrix.

The determinant and areas. The determinant of a \(2 \times 2\) matrix is

\[ \det M = m_{11}m_{22} - m_{12}m_{21}. \]

Its meaning is geometric. \(M\) sends the unit square, with sides \((1, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\), to the parallelogram whose sides are the columns of \(M\), \((m_{11}, m_{21})\) and \((m_{12}, m_{22})\). The area of a parallelogram with sides \((a, c)\) and \((b, d)\) is \(|ad - bc|\) (the size of the cross product of the two sides). So \(|\det M|\) is the factor by which \(M\) multiplies the area of the unit square, and, by cutting any shape into small squares, of any region. Two consequences:

  • \(\det(AB) = \det A\,\det B\): doing \(A\) multiplies areas by \(\det A\), then \(B\) by \(\det B\). (It can also be checked by multiplying out.)
  • \(\det M^T = \det M\), as the formula shows.

So \(\det(MSM^T) = (\det M)^2\det S\). If \(\det M = 1\), a region of the \((x, x')\) plane keeps its area however \(M\) shears, stretches or turns it. Every drift and every thin lens has determinant 1, and that is the matrix form of the conservation of emittance in section 7.

The inverse. If \(\det M \ne 0\), the matrix that undoes \(M\) is

\[ M^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det M}\begin{pmatrix} m_{22} & -m_{12} \\ -m_{21} & m_{11} \end{pmatrix}, \]

as multiplying out \(M^{-1}M\) confirms. Section 7’s beam ellipse is written with the inverse of the beam matrix.

At work. In the first product above, a particle entering parallel to the axis at 1 mm, \(X = (1\ \mathrm{mm}, 0)\), leaves at \(x = -1.5\) mm with \(x' = -0.5\) mrad: it crossed the axis 2 m after the lens (the focal length) and is moving away from it. Both products have determinant \((-1.5)(1) - (5)(-0.5) = 1\). A drift of 4 m, the same lens, and another 4 m give

\[ \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ -0.5 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \]

whose \(m_{12} = 0\): the final position does not depend on the initial angle, so every ray from one point arrives at one point. This is imaging, with magnification \(m_{11} = -1\), the familiar object and image at twice the focal length.

Exercise O1. Multiply \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) and find the determinant of the result.

Answer

\(\begin{pmatrix} 1 \times 1 + 2 \times (-1) & 1 \times 0 + 2 \times 1 \\ 0 \times 1 + 1 \times (-1) & 0 \times 0 + 1 \times 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), with determinant \((-1)(1) - (2)(-1) = 1\).

Exercise O2. Show that the inverse of the drift matrix \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & L \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) is the drift of length \(-L\), and say why that is obvious.

Answer

\(\det = 1\), so \(M^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -L \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), a drift of \(-L\). Running a particle backwards along a straight line undoes the drift.

Exercise O3. The matrix \(\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}\) doubles \(x\) and halves \(x'\). What does it do to the area of a region, and to a circle?

Answer

\(\det = 1\): areas are unchanged. A circle becomes an ellipse twice as wide and half as tall, of the same area. A beam passing through a waist in its optics is squeezed and stretched in this way without its emittance changing.

Pre-requisite: Linear systems and transfer matrices

Pre-requisite. How beam optics follows one particle, and then a whole beam, through drifts, lenses and bending magnets with matrices: the state \((x, x')\), the transfer matrices of a drift and a thin lens, the matrix of a lens followed by a drift used in section 7’s quadrupole scan, how the beam matrix is carried, \(\Sigma_1 = M\Sigma_0M^T\), and how a momentum offset is carried by a third column, the dispersion, so that \(x_f = Mx_0 + D\delta\), as in section 8’s spectrometer. Chapter 16 uses the same matrices for whole rings. Taught from Lee, chapter 2, §I.4 (particle motion in dipole and quadrupole magnets), §II.1 (transfer matrices, including the thin lens and the sector dipole, eqs. 2.28 and 2.29), §II.5 (the σ-matrix, its transport by \(M\sigma M^T\), and the quadrupole-tuning method of emittance measurement) and §IV.1 (the dispersion function and the three-by-three matrix of a sector dipole, eq. 2.154); it uses the matrix rules of the section above.

The state of a particle. Measure a particle’s position \(x\) (m) from a reference path, and its angle \(x' = dx/ds\) (rad) to that path, where \(s\) is the distance along it. Angles are small, so \(x' \approx p_x/p\), the transverse momentum over the total. Inside magnets designed for linear optics the force on a particle is proportional to its offset (a quadrupole’s field grows linearly from its centre), and in a drift there is no force at all. So every element turns \((x, x')\) into a linear combination of \((x, x')\): a \(2 \times 2\) transfer matrix.

A drift. Over a field-free length \(L\) the angle stays and the position grows by \(L\) times the angle:

\[ x_1 = x_0 + Lx'_0, \quad x'_1 = x'_0, \qquad M_{drift} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & L \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

A thin lens. A lens short enough that the position does not change inside it gives an angle kick proportional to the position. For focal length \(f\) the kick is \(-x/f\): a ray entering parallel at \(x\) is turned towards the axis and crosses it a distance \(f\) later. Writing \(K = -1/f\) (m\(^{-1}\)), as section 7 does,

\[ x_1 = x_0, \quad x'_1 = x'_0 + Kx_0, \qquad M_{lens} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ K & 1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

A quadrupole of length \(l_q\) and field gradient \(G\) (T/m) is, when thin, such a lens with \(1/f = Gl_q/(B\rho)\), where \(B\rho = p/q\) is the beam’s magnetic rigidity (Lee, §I.4 and eq. 2.28), focusing in one plane and defocusing in the other, so \(K\) has opposite signs in \(x\) and \(y\). Changing its current changes \(K\). Both matrices have determinant 1.

A lens followed by a drift. The beam meets the lens first, so its matrix stands on the right:

\[ M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & L \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ K & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + LK & L \\ K & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \]

the matrix of section 7. A particle arrives at \(x_1 = (1 + LK)x_0 + Lx'_0\).

Carrying a whole beam. Describe the beam by its beam matrix, the average over all particles of \(XX^T\) (positions and angles measured from the beam’s centre):

\[ \Sigma = \langle XX^T\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_{11} & \sigma_{12} \\ \sigma_{12} & \sigma_{22} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \langle x^2\rangle & \langle xx'\rangle \\ \langle xx'\rangle & \langle x'^2\rangle \end{pmatrix}. \]

Every particle obeys \(X_1 = MX_0\) with the same \(M\), so \(X_1X_1^T = MX_0X_0^TM^T\) (using \((MX_0)^T = X_0^TM^T\)), and averaging over the particles,

\[ \Sigma_1 = M\Sigma_0M^T . \]

This is Lee’s eq. (2.59). The top-left element is the squared beam size downstream. With the lens-and-drift matrix, multiplying out,

\[ \sigma_{11}(K) = (1 + LK)^2\sigma_{11} + 2(1 + LK)L\,\sigma_{12} + L^2\sigma_{22}, \]

and expanding \((1 + LK)^2 = 1 + 2LK + L^2K^2\) and collecting powers of \(K\) gives section 7’s parabola,

\[ \sigma_{11}(K) = L^2\sigma_{11}K^2 + 2\left(L\sigma_{11} + L^2\sigma_{12}\right)K + \left(\sigma_{11} + 2L\sigma_{12} + L^2\sigma_{22}\right). \]

At work: section 7’s example. Take at the quadrupole \(\sigma_{11} = 2 \times 10^{-5}\) m\(^2\) (rms size 4.47 mm), \(\sigma_{12} = 2 \times 10^{-6}\) m rad and \(\sigma_{22} = 4 \times 10^{-7}\) rad\(^2\) (rms angle 0.63 mrad), with \(L = 5\) m. These are section 7’s \(\varepsilon = 2\) µm, \(\beta_x = 10\) m, \(\alpha_x = -1\) (the next section explains the translation). The rms size on the screen is 7.07 mm at \(K = -0.6\) m\(^{-1}\), 2.24 mm at \(K = -0.3\) m\(^{-1}\), 7.07 mm at \(K = 0\) and 13.6 mm at \(K = +0.3\) m\(^{-1}\). The parabola has its minimum, the waist on the screen, at \(K = -0.3\) m\(^{-1}\), a focusing lens of focal length 3.3 m. At every setting \(\sqrt{\det\Sigma_1} = 2\) µm, the same as at the quadrupole.

Momentum and dispersion. A particle with momentum \(p(1 + \delta)\) is bent less by a magnet than one with the reference momentum \(p\): its radius in a field \(B\) is \(\rho(1 + \delta)\). To first order its path differs by an amount proportional to \(\delta\), which adds a third column to the matrix:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} x_f \\ x'_f \\ \delta \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} M_{11} & M_{12} & D \\ M_{21} & M_{22} & D' \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x_0 \\ x'_0 \\ \delta \end{pmatrix}, \qquad x_f = M_{11}x_0 + M_{12}x'_0 + D\delta . \]

\(D\) (m) is the dispersion: how far a particle with \(\delta = 1\) would be moved, to first order. Drifts and lenses have \(D = 0\) but carry an existing offset onwards; bends create it.

The 180° spectrometer. Section 8’s uniform field bends particles through half a circle. Three facts from the geometry of circles give its matrix.

  • A particle starting parallel but displaced by \(x_0\) outwards follows a circle of the same radius shifted by \(x_0\), and arrives displaced by \(x_0\) on the far side of the centre, which is inwards there: \(M_{11} = -1\).
  • A particle starting at the reference point at a small angle \(\alpha\) arrives at \(2\rho\cos\alpha \approx 2\rho(1 - \alpha^2/2)\) from the start: to first order no change, \(M_{12} = 0\). The 180° magnet focuses.
  • A particle with momentum offset \(\delta\) follows a circle of diameter \(2\rho(1 + \delta)\): it arrives \(2\rho\delta\) further out, \(D = 2\rho\).

So \(x_f = -x_0 + 2\rho\,\delta\), which is section 8’s \(\Delta x = 2\rho\delta\). The general matrix of a uniform-field sector magnet of bending angle \(\theta\) has first row \((\cos\theta,\ \rho\sin\theta,\ \rho(1 - \cos\theta))\), which at \(\theta = \pi\) gives exactly these three numbers (Lee, eq. 2.154). A source slit of width \(w\) is imaged with width \(|M_{11}|w\), and two momenta are separated when \(D\delta\) exceeds it:

\[ \delta_{min} = \frac{|M_{11}|\,w}{D}. \]

For section 8’s 2 MeV protons with \(\rho = 0.5\) m and \(w = 1\) mm this is \(10^{-3}\), before the second-order aberration is added.

Pre-requisite (continued): Phase space and Liouville’s theorem

Pre-requisite. This continues Phase space and Liouville’s theorem in chapter 7, which gave six-dimensional phase space, Liouville’s theorem, and the conservation of area in each plane when the planes are uncoupled. It adds the working form used to measure beams: the plane of position and angle \((x, x')\), the rms beam ellipse, the rms emittance \(\varepsilon = \sqrt{\det\Sigma}\) as the ellipse’s area over \(\pi\), the Twiss names for the ellipse’s shape, the proof that linear optics keeps \(\varepsilon\) fixed, and the normalised emittance that stays fixed through acceleration. Section 7 rests on all of it. Taught from Lee, chapter 2, §II.5 (the emittance of a beam, eq. 2.57, and the σ-matrix, eqs. 2.59 and 2.60) and §II.5 E (adiabatic damping and the normalised emittance), and Penrose, The Road to Reality, §13.10 (symplectic groups, of which the area-keeping \(2 \times 2\) matrices are the simplest); it uses the two sections above.

From momentum to angle. Liouville’s theorem is about the plane \((x, p_x)\). Instruments measure angles, \(x' = p_x/p\). At fixed total momentum \(p\) the two planes differ only by the scale factor \(p\) on one axis, so an area in \((x, x')\) is the area in \((x, p_x)\) divided by \(p\), and is conserved too. When the beam is accelerated, \(p_x\) is untouched by a purely longitudinal push while \(p\) grows, so the angles shrink and the area in \((x, x')\) falls as \(1/p\), that is as \(1/(\beta\gamma)\).

The rms emittance. A real beam has no sharp edge, so measure its spread by moments, as Lee does. Relative to the beam’s centre, form \(\langle x^2\rangle\), \(\langle x'^2\rangle\) and \(\langle xx'\rangle\), the beam matrix \(\Sigma\) of the previous section. Write \(\sigma_x = \sqrt{\langle x^2\rangle}\), \(\sigma_{x'} = \sqrt{\langle x'^2\rangle}\), and \(r = \langle xx'\rangle/(\sigma_x\sigma_{x'})\) for the correlation between position and angle. The rms emittance is

\[ \varepsilon = \sqrt{\det\Sigma} = \sqrt{\langle x^2\rangle\langle x'^2\rangle - \langle xx'\rangle^2} = \sigma_x\sigma_{x'}\sqrt{1 - r^2} \]

(m rad, often quoted in mm mrad, which is the same as µm). A beam whose angle is strongly tied to its position, \(r\) close to \(\pm1\), has a small emittance even if it is wide and divergent: it is converging to, or diverging from, a small waist.

The rms ellipse. The curve

\[ X^T\Sigma^{-1}X = 1, \quad\text{that is}\quad \frac{\sigma_{22}x^2 - 2\sigma_{12}xx' + \sigma_{11}x'^2}{\varepsilon^2} = 1 \]

(using the inverse from Matrices), is an ellipse that has the beam’s own size, divergence and tilt: it reaches \(x = \pm\sigma_x\) and \(x' = \pm\sigma_{x'}\). Its area is \(\pi\varepsilon\). To see this, take an upright ellipse \(x^2/a_1^2 + x'^2/a_2^2 = 1\): its area is \(\pi a_1a_2\), and its matrix is \(\Sigma = \mathrm{diag}(a_1^2, a_2^2)\), with \(\sqrt{\det\Sigma} = a_1a_2\). Any tilted ellipse comes from an upright one by a rotation and a rescaling of determinant 1, which change neither the area nor \(\det\Sigma\). So the rms emittance is the area of the rms ellipse divided by \(\pi\), as section 7 says.

The Twiss names. Since \(\det\Sigma = \varepsilon^2\), the matrix can be written as \(\varepsilon\) times a matrix of determinant 1:

\[ \Sigma = \varepsilon\begin{pmatrix} \beta_x & -\alpha_x \\ -\alpha_x & \gamma_x \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \beta_x\gamma_x - \alpha_x^2 = 1, \]

and the ellipse becomes \(\gamma_xx^2 + 2\alpha_xxx' + \beta_xx'^2 = \varepsilon\). The beam size is \(\sigma_x = \sqrt{\varepsilon\beta_x}\); \(\alpha_x\) measures the tilt, negative for a beam whose angle grows with its position (diverging), positive for one converging. These are the Twiss (Courant-Snyder) functions of section 7. Here they are only names for the ellipse’s shape at one place; chapter 16 shows they are properties of the optics, which every particle’s motion follows.

Why linear optics keeps the emittance. The beam matrix is carried by \(\Sigma_1 = M\Sigma_0M^T\). Taking determinants,

\[ \det\Sigma_1 = \det M\,\det\Sigma_0\,\det M^T = (\det M)^2\det\Sigma_0 . \]

Drifts, thin lenses, quadrupoles and dipoles all have \(\det M = 1\) (the area-keeping property that Penrose’s symplectic matrices share), so \(\varepsilon_1 = \varepsilon_0\). The ellipse is sheared, stretched and turned, but its area stays. This is Liouville’s theorem in the form section 7 uses. It fails when the forces are not linear in the offset (lens aberrations and space charge distort the ellipse, and the rms area grows though the true phase-space area does not), when particles scatter in matter (a foil or gas adds random angles), and when they radiate. Those are the cases chapter 7 listed.

The normalised emittance. Since \(x'\) shrinks as \(1/(\beta\gamma)\) under acceleration, the quantity conserved through acceleration is

\[ \varepsilon_n = \beta\gamma\,\varepsilon, \]

with \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) the relativistic factors (unsubscripted; the subscripted \(\beta_x\), \(\gamma_x\) are Twiss functions). Lee calls the shrinking of \(\varepsilon\) adiabatic damping.

At work. A beam with \(\sigma_x = 2\) mm, \(\sigma_{x'} = 1\) mrad and \(r = 0.6\) has \(\varepsilon = 2 \times 1 \times 0.8 = 1.6\) mm mrad, and \(\beta_x = \sigma_x^2/\varepsilon = 2.5\) m, \(\alpha_x = -r\sigma_x\sigma_{x'}/\varepsilon = -0.75\), \(\gamma_x = \sigma_{x'}^2/\varepsilon = 0.625\) m\(^{-1}\); check: \(2.5 \times 0.625 - 0.75^2 = 1\). Section 7’s example beam (\(\varepsilon = 2\) µm, \(\beta_x = 10\) m, \(\alpha_x = -1\), so \(\gamma_x = (1 + \alpha_x^2)/\beta_x = 0.2\) m\(^{-1}\)) has \(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \times 10^{-5} & 2 \times 10^{-6} \\ 2 \times 10^{-6} & 4 \times 10^{-7} \end{pmatrix}\), the matrix used in the previous section; after a 5 m drift it has \(\beta_x = 25\) m and \(\alpha_x = -2\), with \(\varepsilon\) still 2 µm. A proton accelerated from 50 keV (\(\beta\gamma = 0.0103\)) to 1 GeV (\(\beta\gamma = 1.81\)) has its emittance reduced 175 times at constant \(\varepsilon_n\), and its size, for the same optics, by \(\sqrt{175} = 13\): the shrinking beam that residual-gas monitors show during acceleration (section 6).

Pre-requisite: Least-squares fitting

Pre-requisite. How to fit a model to noisy measurements: the method of least squares and why it is the right choice when the errors are Gaussian; how to fit a parabola, a model that is linear in its coefficients, by solving three linear equations; the uncertainties of the fitted coefficients; and why the measurements must span the minimum of the parabola. Section 7’s quadrupole scan is such a fit; later chapters fit orbits and responses in the same way. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 5, §110 and §111 (the Gaussian distribution for one and for several variables), and Lee, chapter 2, §II.5 C (the quadrupole-tuning method: a parabola fitted to the measured \(\sigma_{11}\)). The least-squares method itself is standard numerical practice, which none of these books develops at length; the numbers below come from simulations done for this section.

The problem. At settings \(K_1, K_2, \dots, K_n\) a quantity \(y_i\) is measured, each with a random error of rms \(s_i\), independent of the others. A model \(f(K; p_0, p_1, p_2)\) with unknown coefficients should describe the data; in section 7, \(y_i\) is the squared beam size and \(f = p_0 + p_1K + p_2K^2\). Which coefficients describe the data best?

Why squares. If each error is Gaussian (F6, section 5; chapter 2; Landau and Lifshitz, §110), the probability density of getting the data, for given coefficients, is the product

\[ \prod_i\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,s_i}\exp\left[-\frac{(y_i - f(K_i))^2}{2s_i^2}\right]. \]

The coefficients that make the observed data most probable are those that maximise this product, which means minimising the sum in the exponent:

\[ \chi^2 = \sum_i\frac{\left(y_i - f(K_i)\right)^2}{s_i^2} = \sum_iw_i\left(y_i - f(K_i)\right)^2, \qquad w_i = \frac{1}{s_i^2}. \]

Each point is weighted by the inverse of its variance: precise points count more.

The normal equations. When the model is linear in its coefficients, \(f = \sum_kp_kg_k(K)\) with known functions \(g_0 = 1\), \(g_1 = K\), \(g_2 = K^2\), setting each partial derivative \(\partial\chi^2/\partial p_j\) to zero (a minimum is where the slope in every direction is zero) gives

\[ \sum_k\left[\sum_iw_ig_j(K_i)\,g_k(K_i)\right]p_k = \sum_iw_ig_j(K_i)\,y_i, \qquad j = 0, 1, 2: \]

three linear equations in three unknowns, the normal equations. In matrix form, with \(A\) the table of \(g_k(K_i)\) and \(W\) the diagonal matrix of weights, \((A^TWA)\,p = A^TWy\). For a straight line (\(g_0 = 1\), \(g_1 = K\)) they can be solved by hand; for the parabola a computer solves them in an instant.

Uncertainties. The fitted coefficients are linear combinations of the data, so their errors follow from the data’s errors by the propagation rules of chapter 1. The result is compact: their variances and covariances are the elements of \((A^TWA)^{-1}\). The off-diagonal elements matter: two coefficients can each be uncertain in a correlated way, so that an error in one is largely compensated by the other along the data, but not outside it.

The quadrupole scan. Fit \(\sigma_{11}(K) = p_0 + p_1K + p_2K^2\), then convert to section 7’s form \(a(K - b)^2 + c\):

\[ a = p_2, \qquad b = -\frac{p_1}{2p_2}, \qquad c = p_0 - \frac{p_1^2}{4p_2}, \qquad \varepsilon = \frac{\sqrt{ac}}{L^2}. \]

Here \(a\) is the curvature and \(c\) the height of the minimum. Both must be well determined, and \(c\) is a small number found from three large ones.

Why the scan must span the minimum. If all the settings lie on one side of the minimum, the measured points lie on a gently curving rising line. The curvature \(p_2\) then has to be inferred from a slight bend, and it trades off against the slope \(p_1\): a little more curvature with a little less slope fits the data almost as well. The minimum height \(c\) is then an extrapolation of the parabola to a point outside the data, and the small errors in \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) are multiplied on the way. Simulating section 7’s example (the beam above: \(\varepsilon = 2\) µm, waist at \(K = -0.3\) m\(^{-1}\), \(L = 5\) m), with 15 settings and a random 3 per cent error on each squared size, repeated 20 000 times:

Settings (\(K\) in m\(^{-1}\)) Fit Spread of fitted \(\varepsilon\) Fits with no answer
\(-0.6\) to \(0\), spanning the waist weighted 0.8 per cent none
\(-0.9\) to \(+0.3\), spanning the waist unweighted 6 per cent none
\(0\) to \(+0.6\), one side only weighted 52 per cent 32 per cent (\(ac < 0\))

In the one-sided scan the curvature is known only to 8 per cent instead of 1.3, and in a third of the trials the fitted minimum falls below zero, so that \(\sqrt{ac}\) does not exist. The middle line shows a second lesson: the large sizes far from the waist have large absolute errors, and an unweighted fit lets them dominate; weighting by \(1/s_i^2\) restores the information near the waist. Section 7’s single trial returning 2.05 µm is typical of an unweighted fit.

Three points or many. Three settings determine a parabola exactly but leave nothing to check it with. More points average down the noise, roughly as one over the square root of their number, and the scatter of the points about the fitted curve (the residuals) shows whether the model fits at all: residuals much larger than the \(s_i\) mean the model is wrong, for example because the beam is not Gaussian or the lens is not thin.

7. Emittance

The rms emittance in one plane is \(\varepsilon = \sqrt{\langle x^2\rangle\langle x'^2\rangle - \langle x x'\rangle^2}\), the area of the beam’s rms ellipse in position and angle divided by \(\pi\) (chapter 7 gives the definition and the conventions in use); the Courant-Snyder functions that describe it are introduced in chapter 16. A profile alone gives it only if the optics are known:

\[ \sigma_x^2 = \varepsilon\,\beta_x + \left(D_x\,\delta\right)^2. \]

In a ring, where \(\beta_x\) and \(D_x\) are known, one profile gives \(\varepsilon\). In a line, where they are not, the angle must be measured too.

Slit and grid. A slit 0.1 to 0.5 mm wide selects a thin slice at position \(x\); the slice drifts 10 cm to 1 m to a grid that measures its angles. The slit is stepped across the beam, and the whole distribution in \((x, x')\) is built up. The angular resolution is

\[ \Delta x' = \frac{d_{slit} + 2 r_w}{d}, \]

with \(d\) the drift (m) and \(r_w\) the grid wire radius. A 0.1 mm slit and wires 0.1 mm in diameter at 0.5 m give 0.4 mrad. The method works only where the beam stops in the slit plate, below a range of about a centimetre, so near ion sources and at low-energy linear accelerators.

Pepper-pot. A plate with a grid of holes, a screen behind it, one camera picture: both planes at once, in one pulse. The GSI device has 15 by 15 holes of 0.1 mm at 3 mm pitch and a screen 25 cm behind; the angular resolution is about 0.4 mrad, and spots from neighbouring holes overlap beyond \(\pm 6\) mrad. Before each measurement a laser through the holes calibrates the camera.

The quadrupole scan. Measure the size at one place while changing the strength of a quadrupole upstream. The beam matrix \(\Sigma = \varepsilon\begin{pmatrix}\beta_x & -\alpha_x \\ -\alpha_x & \gamma_x\end{pmatrix}\) at the quadrupole is carried to the screen by \(\Sigma_1 = M\Sigma_0 M^T\), with \(M\) the transfer matrix. For a thin lens of strength \(K = -1/f\) (m\(^{-1}\); \(f\) its focal length, and \(K\) here is not the constant of the Bethe formula) followed by a drift \(L\) (m),

\[ M = \begin{pmatrix}1 + LK & L\\ K & 1\end{pmatrix}, \]

and the measured squared size is a parabola in \(K\):

\[ \sigma_{11}(K) = L^2\sigma_{11}\,K^2 + 2\left(L\sigma_{11} + L^2\sigma_{12}\right)K + \sigma_{11} + 2L\sigma_{12} + L^2\sigma_{22}, \]

where \(\sigma_{ij}\) without argument are the elements at the quadrupole. Fit \(\sigma_{11}(K) = a(K - b)^2 + c\) and compare coefficients (expanding, \(a(K - b)^2 + c = aK^2 - 2abK + ab^2 + c\)): \(a = L^2\sigma_{11}\), \(-ab = L\sigma_{11} + L^2\sigma_{12}\), \(ab^2 + c = \sigma_{11} + 2L\sigma_{12} + L^2\sigma_{22}\). These are three equations for the three unknowns \(\sigma_{11}\), \(\sigma_{12}\), \(\sigma_{22}\): the first gives \(\sigma_{11}\), the second then \(\sigma_{12}\), the third \(\sigma_{22}\). Putting them into the determinant, everything but one term cancels (the algebra is written out in Exercise 7 at the end of the chapter): \(\det\Sigma_0 = \sigma_{11}\sigma_{22} - \sigma_{12}^2 = ac/L^4\), so

\[ \varepsilon = \frac{\sqrt{ac}}{L^2}. \]

A test with assumed numbers (\(\varepsilon = 2\) µm, \(\beta_x = 10\) m, \(\alpha_x = -1\) at the quadrupole, \(L = 5\) m, 15 settings, 3 per cent random error on each squared size) returns 2.05 µm. The scan must pass through the waist, the minimum of the parabola, or \(a\) and \(c\) are poorly determined.

8. Energy

The spectrometer. A magnet sorts particles by rigidity \(B\rho = p/q\) (chapter 6 uses one to hold a tandem’s voltage). The simplest to analyse is the 180° spectrometer: a uniform field bends particles from a source through half a circle onto a detector. A particle of momentum \(p\) arrives at distance \(2\rho\) from the source, so a momentum change \(\delta = \Delta p/p\) moves it by

\[ \Delta x = 2\rho\,\delta. \]

A particle leaving at angle \(\alpha\) to the central direction arrives at \(2\rho\cos\alpha \approx 2\rho(1 - \alpha^2/2)\): to first order in \(\alpha\) all directions meet again, which is why the 180° geometry focuses, and the second-order miss is \(\rho\alpha^2\). With a source of width \(w\), two momenta are resolved when their separation exceeds the image width:

\[ \delta_{min} = \frac{w + \rho\,\alpha^2}{2\rho}. \]

For 2 MeV protons (\(B\rho = 0.2045\) T m) in a field of 0.409 T (\(\rho = 0.5\) m), a 1 mm source and a 10 mrad half-angle, the aberration is 0.05 mm and \(\delta_{min} = 1.05 \times 10^{-3}\). Kinetic energy and momentum are related by \(\Delta W/W = (1 + 1/\gamma)\,\delta\). (From \(E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4\), \(E\,dE = c^2p\,dp\); and \(dW = dE\), so \(dW/W = (p^2c^2/EW)\,dp/p\). With \(p^2c^2 = (\gamma^2 - 1)m^2c^4 = (\gamma - 1)(\gamma + 1)m^2c^4\), \(E = \gamma mc^2\) and \(W = (\gamma - 1)mc^2\), the factor is \((\gamma + 1)/\gamma = 1 + 1/\gamma\): 2 for slow particles, as for \(W = p^2/2m\), and 1 for fast ones, as for \(W \approx pc\).) So \(\Delta W/W = 2.1 \times 10^{-3}\), or 4.2 keV. In general, for any magnet system that images a slit of width \(w\) onto another with magnification \(M\) and dispersion \(D_x\) (m), the final position is \(x_f = M x_0 + D_x\delta\), and the resolution is \(\delta_{min} = M w/D_x\). A beam line’s dispersion at a screen gives the energy spread from the width of the spot, by the second term of \(\sigma_x^2 = \varepsilon\beta_x + (D_x\delta)^2\).

Time of flight. Two pick-ups a distance \(L\) apart time the bunch: \(\beta = L/(ct)\). Differentiating \(W = (\gamma - 1) m c^2\) gives \(dW = mc^2\,d\gamma\). With \(\gamma = (1 - \beta^2)^{-1/2}\), the chain rule gives \(d\gamma = -\tfrac12(1 - \beta^2)^{-3/2}(-2\beta)\,d\beta = \gamma^3\beta\,d\beta\); and \(\beta = L/(ct)\) gives \(d\beta/\beta = -dt/t\) (a longer time means a slower particle). Dividing \(dW\) by \(W\), and dropping the sign,

\[ \frac{\Delta W}{W} = \frac{\gamma^3\beta^2}{\gamma - 1}\,\frac{\Delta t}{t} = \gamma(\gamma + 1)\,\frac{\Delta t}{t}, \]

using \(\beta^2\gamma^2 = (\gamma - 1)(\gamma + 1)\). For slow particles it is \(2\,\Delta t/t\); for fast ones the method loses sensitivity as \(\gamma^2\). At the GSI linear accelerator two pick-ups 1.629 m apart, with three bunches between them at 36.136 MHz (27.673 ns spacing) and an oscilloscope reading of 15.82 ns, give a flight time of 98.84 ns, \(\beta = 0.05498\) and \(W = 1.411\) MeV per atomic mass unit. With 1 mm on the distance and 0.1 ns on the time, added in quadrature (F6, section 8), \(\Delta W/W = 0.24\) per cent. (The GSI notes quote 1.407 ± 0.003 MeV/u, close to this; the last digit depends on the mass per nucleon used.) For a 1 GeV proton over 10 m (38.1 ns), 10 ps of timing gives \(\Delta W/W = 0.17\) per cent.

Pre-requisite: Fourier series and transforms

Pre-requisite. How a signal is split into frequencies: the Fourier series of a repeating signal, the Fourier transform of any signal, the transforms of an oscillation that lasts a finite time (a peak at its frequency, as wide as one over the duration), of a decaying oscillation and of a Gaussian pulse, and Parseval’s theorem, which shares a signal’s energy among its frequencies. Section 9 finds the tune as the peak of a transform. The same ideas were used in advance for the bandwidths of section 4 and the radiation spectrum of section 6, and almost every later chapter uses them. Taught from Penrose, The Road to Reality, §9.1 (Fourier series) and §9.4 (the Fourier transform), Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 2, §49 (spectral resolution), and Lee, Appendix B, §I (Fourier transforms of beam signals).

A repeating signal: the Fourier series. (F5, section 16 gave the idea; here it is made exact.) In a ring, a pick-up sees the same bunch pass every revolution period \(T_0\); the beam current at that point repeats. Any reasonable function with period \(T_0\) can be written as a sum of oscillations at the revolution frequency \(\omega_0 = 2\pi/T_0\) and its harmonics:

\[ f(t) = a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[a_n\cos(n\omega_0t) + b_n\sin(n\omega_0t)\right]. \]

The coefficients follow from one fact: over a whole period, \(\int\cos(n\omega_0t)\cos(m\omega_0t)\,dt\) is zero unless \(n = m\), when it is \(T_0/2\), and the same holds for sines, while every product of a sine with a cosine integrates to zero. Multiply both sides by \(\cos(m\omega_0t)\) and integrate over a period: every term but one vanishes, and

\[ a_m = \frac{2}{T_0}\int_0^{T_0}f(t)\cos(m\omega_0t)\,dt, \qquad b_m = \frac{2}{T_0}\int_0^{T_0}f(t)\sin(m\omega_0t)\,dt, \]

\[ a_0 = \frac{1}{T_0}\int_0^{T_0}f\,dt . \]

At work. A single short bunch of charge \(q\) circulating gives \(a_0 = q/T_0\), the mean current, and for a bunch much shorter than \(T_0\) every harmonic has \(a_n \approx 2q/T_0\): a comb of equal lines at every multiple of the revolution frequency. For a Gaussian bunch of rms duration \(\sigma_t\) the lines fall off as \(2I_{avg}\,e^{-(n\omega_0\sigma_t)^2/2}\) (Chao and Tigner, §7.4.2), for the reason shown below.

Any signal: the Fourier transform. Let the period grow without limit: the harmonics crowd together into a continuum. Define

\[ F(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)\,e^{i\omega t}\,dt, \qquad f(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(\omega)\,e^{-i\omega t}\,d\omega . \]

\(F(\omega)\) is a complex number at each frequency: its size says how much of that frequency the signal contains, its angle the phase. Books differ in the sign of the exponent and where the \(2\pi\) goes (Lee uses \(e^{-j\omega t}\)); only consistency matters, and this chapter’s continuous transforms use the form above. For a real signal \(F(-\omega)\) is the complex conjugate of \(F(\omega)\), so the negative frequencies carry nothing new.

An oscillation recorded for a finite time. Let \(f = \cos\omega_1t\) for a record of duration \(t_{rec}\) centred on \(t = 0\), and zero outside. Writing the cosine as \(\tfrac12(e^{i\omega_1t} + e^{-i\omega_1t})\) and integrating each exponential over the record,

\[ F(\omega) = \frac{\sin\left[(\omega - \omega_1)t_{rec}/2\right]}{\omega - \omega_1} + \frac{\sin\left[(\omega + \omega_1)t_{rec}/2\right]}{\omega + \omega_1}. \]

Near \(\omega_1\) the first term dominates. It peaks at \(\omega = \omega_1\) with height \(t_{rec}/2\), and first falls to zero at \(\omega - \omega_1 = \pm2\pi/t_{rec}\), that is at \(f_1 \pm 1/t_{rec}\) in ordinary frequency. The peak marks the frequency, and its width is set by the length of the record: a longer record gives a sharper peak. That is the whole basis of section 9: an oscillation recorded for longer has its frequency fixed more finely. A 1 ms record of a 300 kHz oscillation gives a peak with zeros at 299 and 301 kHz.

A decaying oscillation. A signal \(e^{-t/\tau}\cos\omega_1t\) for \(t > 0\) (a ringing cavity, or a kicked beam whose coherent motion dies away) has, near \(\omega_1\),

\[ F(\omega) \approx \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{1}{1/\tau - i(\omega - \omega_1)}, \qquad |F|^2 \propto \frac{1}{(\omega - \omega_1)^2 + 1/\tau^2}, \]

a peak (a Lorentzian) of full width \(2/\tau\) at half height. The decay time plays the part the record length played before. A cavity position monitor ringing at 6.5 GHz with a loaded quality factor of 1000 has a field decay time \(\tau = 2Q_L/\omega = 49\) ns and a line width \(f/Q_L = 6.5\) MHz.

A Gaussian pulse. Let \(f = e^{-t^2/(2\sigma_t^2)}\). Complete the square in the exponent:

\[ -\frac{t^2}{2\sigma_t^2} + i\omega t = -\frac{(t - i\omega\sigma_t^2)^2}{2\sigma_t^2} - \frac{\omega^2\sigma_t^2}{2}, \]

and the remaining integral is the Gaussian integral, \(\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_t\) (the shift of the variable by an imaginary constant does not change it). So

\[ F(\omega) = \sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_t\,e^{-\omega^2\sigma_t^2/2}: \]

a Gaussian in frequency, with rms width \(1/\sigma_t\). A short bunch has a wide spectrum. The spectrum’s power falls to half where \(\omega^2\sigma_t^2 = \ln2\), at \(f = \sqrt{\ln2}/(2\pi\sigma_t) = 0.13/\sigma_t\): 130 MHz for a 1 ns bunch, 4.0 GHz for a bunch 1 cm long (\(\sigma_t = 33\) ps). These are the bandwidths quoted in the transfer-function section.

Parseval’s theorem. The energy in a signal can be counted in time or in frequency:

\[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)^2\,dt = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|F(\omega)|^2\,d\omega = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{\infty}|F(\omega)|^2\,d\omega . \]

Proof: replace one of the two factors \(f(t)\) by its inverse transform and change the order of integration:

\[ \int f(t)\,f(t)\,dt = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int F(\omega)\left[\int f(t)\,e^{-i\omega t}\,dt\right]d\omega = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int F(\omega)\,F(\omega)^{\ast}\,d\omega, \]

since for a real \(f\) the bracket is the complex conjugate of \(F(\omega)\). The last form in the theorem uses \(|F(-\omega)| = |F(\omega)|\). A numerical check with a Gaussian pulse gives both sides as 1.77245. Section 6’s radiation spectrum is this theorem applied to the radiated field.

Filters in this language. The output of a linear system is the convolution of its input with its impulse response (transfer functions). Transforming a convolution gives a product: the output’s transform is \(H(\omega)\) times the input’s, and \(H(\omega)\) is the transform of the impulse response. (The circuit sections write phasors as \(e^{j\omega t}\); with this section’s \(e^{i\omega t}\) in the forward transform, their \(j\) becomes \(-i\). For the low-pass, \(\int_0^\infty(1/RC)\,e^{-t/RC}e^{i\omega t}\,dt = 1/(1 - i\omega RC)\), the same \(H\).) That is why an instrument’s frequency response and its pulse response are two descriptions of one thing.

Answer

At \(f_1 \pm 1/t_{rec} = 270 \pm 0.5\) kHz. With 20 ms they close in to \(\pm 0.05\) kHz: ten times the record, ten times finer.

Exercise S2. A proton bunch at nearly the speed of light has an rms length of 10 cm. Up to what frequency does its spectrum stay above half power?

Answer

\(\sigma_t = 0.1/3.00 \times 10^8 = 0.334\) ns, so \(f = 0.1325/\sigma_t = 397\) MHz.

Answer

\(2e^{-(n\omega_0\sigma_t)^2/2}\) with \(n\omega_0\sigma_t = 2\pi \times 10^8 \times 10^{-9} = 0.628\): \(2e^{-0.197} = 1.64\) times the mean current. The harmonics stay near twice the mean until \(n\omega_0\sigma_t\) approaches 1, here around the 160th harmonic.

Pre-requisite: Sampling, discrete Fourier transform, FFT, aliasing

Pre-requisite. What changes when a signal is known only at separate instants, as a ring’s beam position is known only once per turn at each monitor: the discrete Fourier transform and the fast algorithm that computes it; the frequency resolution \(1/N\) of \(N\) samples, and the \(\pm1/(2N)\) to which the highest bin locates a peak; and aliasing, which hides the integer part of the tune and folds the rest into the range 0 to ½. Section 9 measures the tune in exactly this way. Taught from Lee, Appendix B, §I.1 (the Nyquist sampling theorem: in tune units the critical tune is ½, and higher frequencies fold back) and §I.2 (the discrete Fourier transform, and the tune resolution \(\Delta q = 1/N\), with his example of 385 turns), and Penrose, The Road to Reality, §9.2 (functions on a circle); it builds on the previous section.

Samples once per turn. A position monitor in a ring sees the bunch once per revolution period \(T_0\). After a kick the bunch oscillates across the orbit, making \(Q\) oscillations per turn; at one monitor the readings are

\[ x_n = A\cos(2\pi Qn + \phi_0), \qquad n = 0, 1, \dots, N - 1, \]

because each turn advances the oscillation’s phase by \(2\pi Q\). These \(N\) numbers are all the data there is.

Aliasing. Two facts about integers make the integer part of \(Q\) invisible. For any integer \(m\), \(\cos(2\pi(Q + m)n + \phi_0) = \cos(2\pi Qn + 2\pi mn + \phi_0) = \cos(2\pi Qn + \phi_0)\), since \(mn\) is an integer: tunes that differ by a whole number give identical samples. And \(\cos(2\pi(m - Q)n - \phi_0) = \cos(2\pi Qn + \phi_0)\), since cosine is even: the tunes \(q\) and \(1 - q\) also give identical samples (with the phase reversed). So a single monitor sampled once per turn can tell only the fractional tune, folded into \(0 \le q \le \tfrac12\). The samples for \(Q = 6.2734\), \(0.2734\), \(0.7266\) and \(5.7266\) are the same: 1, \(-0.1465\), \(-0.9571\), 0.4269, 0.8320, and so on. This is the sampling theorem in tune units (Lee, eq. B.1): sampled at one rate, a signal can be resolved only up to half that rate; anything faster is folded back, or aliased, into the range below. Whether the true fractional tune is \(q\) or \(1 - q\) is settled by other means: a second monitor a known phase advance away, or a small change of a quadrupole and the direction in which the peak moves.

The discrete Fourier transform. The natural transform of \(N\) samples is

\[ X_k = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x_n\,e^{-2\pi ikn/N}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, N - 1, \]

with the inverse \(x_n = (1/N)\sum_kX_k\,e^{2\pi ikn/N}\). It is the continuous transform of the previous section evaluated at the frequencies \(k/N\) (in units of the revolution frequency) and with the integral replaced by a sum over the samples (Lee, eq. B.4, with his sign in the exponent; as the previous section said, the sign is a convention, and it does not change \(|X_k|\)). For real data \(X_{N-k}\) is the complex conjugate of \(X_k\), so only \(k\) from 0 to \(N/2\) carry information: the same folding, seen from the other side.

Resolution. For \(x_n = \cos(2\pi qn)\) the sum behaves like the finite-record transform of the previous section, with the record \(N\) turns long. \(|X_k|\) peaks at the bin \(k\) closest to \(qN\), with a width of about one bin. The bins are \(1/N\) apart in tune (Lee, eq. B.5: \(\Delta q = 1/N\)). Taking the tune as the centre of the highest bin can be wrong by at most half a bin:

\[ |q_{measured} - q| \le \frac{1}{2N}, \]

section 9’s rule: \(10^{-3}\) needs 500 turns. Interpolating between bins, by fitting the known shape of the peak, does much better when the signal is clean. If the coherent oscillation dies away after a few hundred turns (decoherence), recording longer adds only noise: the effective \(N\) is the number of turns the oscillation lasts.

At work. A simulated record of \(N = 500\) turns with \(Q = 6.2734\), amplitude 1 and random noise of rms 0.3 per reading: the highest bin is \(k = 137\), giving \(q = 0.274\), wrong by \(6 \times 10^{-4}\), inside the half-bin limit of \(10^{-3}\). Multiplying the samples by a smooth window (which tames the side lobes of the peak) and interpolating between the three highest bins gives 0.273403, wrong by \(3 \times 10^{-6}\). Lee’s example of 385 turns has a bin width of \(1/385 = 0.0026\), which he quotes as a resolution of about 0.003.

The fast Fourier transform. Computed directly, the \(N\) values \(X_k\) each need \(N\) multiplications: \(N^2\) in all. The fast Fourier transform (published by Cooley and Tukey in 1965) splits the sum into its even and odd samples, each half a transform of length \(N/2\), and repeats the split until single samples remain, needing about \(N\log_2N\) operations. For \(N = 1024\) that is about \(10^4\) instead of \(10^6\); for 4096, \(5 \times 10^4\) instead of \(1.7 \times 10^7\). It is what lets a tune be read many times a second from turn-by-turn data.

Exercise T1. How many turns of coherent oscillation are needed to fix the fractional tune to \(10^{-4}\) from the highest bin alone?

Answer

\(1/(2N) = 10^{-4}\) gives \(N = 5000\) turns. In a ring with a 1 µs revolution period that is 5 ms of oscillation, longer than a kicked beam usually stays coherent; hence interpolation, or continuous gentle excitation.

Exercise T2. One monitor gives a fractional tune of 0.31. What true tunes are consistent with it, and how would a second monitor help?

Answer

\(Q = m + 0.31\) or \(Q = m + 0.69\) for any integer \(m\). The integer comes from the machine’s design or an orbit measurement. A second monitor sees the same oscillation shifted in phase by the betatron phase advance between the two, which the optics give. With \(1 - q\) in place of \(q\) the shift would appear with the opposite sign, so the measured shift picks the right one.

Exercise T3. A tune peak sits exactly halfway between two bins of a 1000-turn record. What is the error of the highest-bin estimate?

Answer

Half a bin: \(1/(2 \times 1000) = 5 \times 10^{-4}\), the worst case. On average, over all positions of the true tune, the error is a quarter of a bin.

9. Tune, in outline

In a ring, particles oscillate across the orbit as they go round; the number of oscillations per turn is the tune \(Q_x\) or \(Q_y\) (chapter 16), and its fractional part decides which errors resonate (chapter 18). To measure it, kick the beam once with a fast magnet, record its position on one monitor turn by turn, and take the Fourier transform: the peak is at the fractional tune. With \(N\) turns the peak is located to about \(\Delta Q_x = 1/(2N)\), so \(10^{-3}\) needs 500 turns of coherent oscillation (Forck, section 5.9). The oscillation often decoheres sooner, because particles have slightly different tunes, and the kick enlarges the beam. Gentler methods drive the beam with a swept or noise excitation and measure its response.

Pre-requisite (continued): Nuclear reactions and Q value

Pre-requisite. This continues Nuclear reactions and Q value in chapter 1, which gave reaction notation, Q values from mass excesses, decay and half-life (with more in chapter 5). It adds three things that loss monitors and shielding depend on: how neutrons are slowed by elastic collisions (moderation) and then captured in boron-10 or helium-3, giving charged particles a counter can see; photonuclear reactions, whose threshold is the neutron separation energy, and the giant dipole resonance; and how activity builds up while a beam runs and decays after it stops. Sections 10 and 13 use all three. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 1, §17 (elastic collisions: the energy given to a struck particle), and Livingston and Blewett, §14-4 (photoneutron thresholds, table 14-6), §14-6 (neutron shielding and the moderation of neutrons), §14-7 (induced radioactivity) and §14-9 (photoneutrons and the giant resonance).

Slowing neutrons down. A neutron has no charge, so it loses no energy to electrons (section 2) and travels until it hits a nucleus. Below a few MeV the commonest event is elastic scattering. Treat the neutron as mass 1 and the nucleus as mass \(A\), at rest. In the centre-of-mass frame an elastic collision only turns the velocities, through an angle \(\chi\); transforming back (Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 1, §17) the neutron’s laboratory energy after the collision is

\[ W' = W\,\frac{A^2 + 2A\cos\chi + 1}{(A + 1)^2}, \]

which runs from \(W\) for a grazing collision (\(\chi = 0\)) down to \(W\left[(A - 1)/(A + 1)\right]^2\) for a head-on one. The largest fraction it can lose in one collision is \(4A/(A + 1)^2\). Averaging over directions (scattering of slow neutrons is about equally likely in every direction in the centre-of-mass frame), the mean drop in \(\ln W\) per collision is

\[ \xi = 1 + \frac{(A - 1)^2}{2A}\ln\frac{A - 1}{A + 1} \]

(and \(\xi = 1\) for hydrogen). Since each collision subtracts on average \(\xi\) from \(\ln W\), the number of collisions to go from \(W_1\) to \(W_2\) is \(\ln(W_1/W_2)/\xi\).

Nucleus Largest loss per collision \(\xi\) Collisions from 2 MeV to 0.025 eV
Hydrogen (\(A = 1\)) all of it 1 18
Carbon (12) 28 per cent 0.158 115
Oxygen (16) 22 per cent 0.120 152
Iron (56) 6.9 per cent 0.035 520
Lead (208) 1.9 per cent 0.0096 1900

A neutron loses energy best to something of its own mass, as a billiard ball does. That is why water, polyethylene and concrete (rich in hydrogen) moderate neutrons, and why iron, which stops fast neutrons well by other reactions, lets slow ones through: the iron shields of section 13 are finished with concrete for this reason, as Livingston and Blewett (§14-6) explain. A neutron that has come into equilibrium with its surroundings at room temperature has about 0.025 eV and moves at 2200 m/s: it is thermal.

Capturing them. Slow neutrons are captured readily, because a slow neutron spends longer near each nucleus: many capture cross-sections grow as \(1/v\). Two captures make charged particles that a gas counter (section 11) can see.

  • \(^{10}\)B(n,α)\(^7\)Li. From the mass excesses (in keV: n 8071.3, \(^{10}\)B 12050.6, \(^4\)He 2424.9, \(^7\)Li 14907.1), \(Q = 2.790\) MeV. In 94 per cent of captures the lithium is left in its excited state at 0.48 MeV, and the products share 2.31 MeV: by momentum balance the alpha takes \(2.31 \times 7.016/(4.003 + 7.016) = 1.47\) MeV and the lithium 0.84 MeV. The thermal cross-section is 3840 b.
  • \(^3\)He(n,p)\(^3\)H. \(Q = 0.764\) MeV, shared as 0.573 MeV to the proton and 0.191 MeV to the triton. The thermal cross-section is 5330 b.

With the \(1/v\) law, boron-10’s cross-section is 611 b at 1 eV and only 0.6 b at 1 MeV: a fast neutron crosses a counter unseen. So loss monitors and survey meters surround the counter with polyethylene, which turns fast neutrons into thermal ones first. In boron trifluoride enriched to 96 per cent in boron-10 at one atmosphere, the mean free path of a thermal neutron is \(1/(n\sigma) = 11\) cm (F6, section 14, with \(\sigma\) in barns, 1 b \(= 10^{-28}\) m\(^2\)); in helium-3 at four atmospheres it is 1.9 cm. A tube a few centimetres across and a few tens of centimetres long therefore catches a good fraction of the thermal neutrons that enter it.

Photonuclear reactions. A photon absorbed by a nucleus can knock out a neutron if it brings at least the neutron’s separation energy, the Q value of the reverse of capture:

\[ S_n = \left[M(A - 1) + m_n - M(A)\right]c^2 . \]

From the mass excesses: 2.22 MeV for the deuteron (the binding energy of the deuteron), 1.66 MeV for beryllium-9, 18.72 MeV for carbon-12, 15.66 MeV for oxygen-16, 10.86 MeV for copper-63 and 7.37 MeV for lead-208. Livingston and Blewett’s table 14-6 lists 18.7 MeV for carbon, 15.6 for oxygen and 10.9 for copper-63, and they give about 7 MeV for the heaviest elements; a few heavy isotopes with a loosely bound last neutron (lead-207, for one) start lower. Above threshold the cross-section rises to a broad maximum, the giant dipole resonance, at 15 to 20 MeV in most elements (Livingston and Blewett, §14-9, who take 14 MeV for lead; modern data put lead’s at about 13.5 MeV), a few MeV wide. In it the photon’s electric field drives all the protons one way and, by recoil, the neutrons the other: the two fluids oscillate against each other, a driven oscillator with a natural frequency in the MeV range and a low quality factor. An electron beam’s bremsstrahlung has plenty of photons at these energies, which is why section 13’s electron machines make neutrons once their energy passes the threshold of the materials the beam strikes, about 7 to 10 MeV in heavy metals.

Activation. A beam that makes radioactive nuclei at a steady rate \(R\) (per second), each decaying with constant \(\lambda = \ln2/T_{1/2}\), builds up a number \(N^{\ast}\) obeying a first-order differential equation (gain \(R\), loss \(\lambda N^{\ast}\); F7, section 13 gave the decay law)

\[ \frac{dN^{\ast}}{dt} = R - \lambda N^{\ast} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N^{\ast}(t) = \frac{R}{\lambda}\left(1 - e^{-\lambda t}\right), \]

so the activity \(\lambda N^{\ast}\) during an irradiation of length \(t_{irr}\) is

\[ \mathcal{A} = R\left(1 - e^{-\lambda t_{irr}}\right), \]

rising towards the production rate itself (saturation) after a few half-lives, and after the beam stops falling as \(e^{-\lambda t_c}\) with the cooling time \(t_c\). A short-lived nuclide saturates quickly and dies quickly; a long-lived one builds slowly and stays. At work: sodium-24 (\(T_{1/2} = 15\) h) made at \(10^6\) per second for an 8-hour shift reaches \(3.1 \times 10^5\) Bq, 31 per cent of saturation, and two days later is down to \(3.4 \times 10^4\) Bq. Cobalt-60 (\(T_{1/2} = 5.27\) years) reaches only 12 per cent of its saturation activity in a year of running, but will still be there years later. Hence section 13’s rule of thumb: an hour after a run the dose comes mostly from nuclides with half-lives of hours, a month later from those with half-lives of months.

\(W' = W[(A - 1)/(A + 1)]^2 = 2 \times (11/13)^2 = 1.43\) MeV. Even the best collision with carbon removes only 28 per cent.

Exercise U2. Check the Q value of \(^3\)He(n,p)\(^3\)H from the mass excesses n 8071.3, \(^3\)He 14931.2, \(^1\)H 7289.0 and \(^3\)H 14949.8 keV.

Exercise U3. Sodium-22 (\(T_{1/2} = 2.6\) years) is made in a concrete shield at a steady rate. What fraction of its saturation activity has it reached after 10 years of running?

10. Beam loss monitors

A lost particle hits the pipe and makes a shower outside it. Monitors placed outside the pipe count that shower and show where and when beam is being lost. They protect the machine as much as they measure it: a loss monitor that reads too high stops the beam. The common types (Forck, chapter 8):

  • Ionisation chambers, sealed tubes of argon or nitrogen with about 100 cm\(^3\) of gas. They are radiation hard, slow (the ions take about 100 µs to drift) and read the dose directly. A dose rate \(\dot D\) (Gy/s) in a gas mass \(m\) (kg) with energy \(W_i\) per ion pair gives a current \(I = \dot D\,m\,e/W_i\): for 100 cm\(^3\) of argon at one atmosphere and 20 °C (\(m = 0.166\) g, \(W_i = 26\) eV), 1 Gy/s gives 6.4 µA and 1 mGy/s 6.4 nA.
  • Plastic scintillators with photomultipliers count individual particles up to about \(10^7\) per second, but darken: at about \(10^4\) Gy their light transmission falls to \(1/e\).
  • Neutron counters, boron trifluoride or helium-3 tubes in polyethylene, which slows the neutrons to be captured.
  • Silicon PIN diodes in coincidence, which count charged particles and ignore the soft photons of synchrotron light.
  • Optical fibres, which darken where dosed and are read like a telecommunication fibre by timing reflected laser pulses.

The physics of the shower is the subject of section 13: about 10 fast neutrons for each 1 GeV proton stopped in copper or iron.

Pre-requisite (continued): Counting statistics (Poisson)

Pre-requisite. This continues Counting statistics (Poisson) in chapter 1, which gave the Poisson rule that \(N\) counts carry an uncertainty \(\sqrt{N}\). It adds the time between random counts, and from it dead time: why a counter that is blind for a time \(\tau\) after each count loses counts at high rates, and how to correct for it. It also applies the same statistics to the finite number of particles in a beam. Section 11 uses the dead-time correction for the Geiger counter; the close of the chapter and chapter 24 use the beam’s statistical noise. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 5, §114 (Poisson’s formula for the number of particles found in a small part of a large system).

The gaps between random counts. Counts that arrive independently at a mean rate \(n\) (per second) follow Poisson’s formula (F6, section 6): the probability of exactly \(k\) counts in a time \(t\) is \((nt)^ke^{-nt}/k!\). For \(k = 0\) this is \(e^{-nt}\), the probability that a time \(t\) passes with no count. So the gap from one count to the next exceeds \(t\) with probability \(e^{-nt}\): gaps are exponentially distributed, and short gaps are the commonest. The chance that the next count comes within a short time \(\tau\) is \(1 - e^{-n\tau} \approx n\tau\). Random counts bunch up far more than evenly spaced ones would, which is why a counter’s dead time costs counts long before the mean gap, \(1/n\), approaches \(\tau\).

Dead time that waits (non-paralysable). After each recorded count the counter is dead for \(\tau\), and counts arriving then are lost without effect. If \(m\) counts per second are recorded, the counter is dead for a fraction \(m\tau\) of each second, so it loses \(nm\tau\) of the \(n\) that arrive:

\[ n - m = nm\tau \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n = \frac{m}{1 - m\tau}. \]

As \(n\) grows without limit, \(m\) approaches \(1/\tau\): the counter is always dead and counts as fast as it can recover.

Dead time that restarts (paralysable). In some counters every arrival, recorded or not, restarts the dead time. A count is recorded only if the gap before it exceeds \(\tau\), which has probability \(e^{-n\tau}\), so \(m = ne^{-n\tau}\). This has a maximum, \(1/(e\tau)\), at \(n = 1/\tau\), and falls at higher rates: a very hot source can read low. Two different true rates give the same reading, one on each side of the maximum, and only a change of source distance or shielding tells which.

At work. For a Geiger counter with \(\tau = 100\) µs (section 11), a reading of 1000 per second corresponds to 1111 true counts per second (non-paralysable) or 1118 (paralysable); a reading of 5000 per second to 10 000 (non-paralysable), while a paralysable counter with this \(\tau\) can never read more than 3679 per second. A scintillation counter with \(\tau = 100\) ns loses 10 per cent at \(10^6\) counts per second; to keep losses to 1 per cent it must stay below \(10^5\). Pile-up is the companion effect in counters that measure pulse heights: two particles within the resolving time add into one pulse of the wrong size.

A beam is a sample too. A bunch of \(N\) particles, scattered at random with rms size \(\sigma\), has a centre that is itself uncertain by \(\sigma/\sqrt{N}\), the standard error of a mean. For \(10^{10}\) particles and \(\sigma = 1\) mm that is 10 nm; for \(10^8\) particles and \(\sigma = 2\) mm, 0.2 µm. A position monitor with fine enough resolution sees this centre jump from turn to turn as the particles reshuffle; this is the statistical noise of a finite beam that the end of the chapter turns into stochastic cooling.

Exercise V1. A Geiger tube with a dead time of 80 µs records 2000 counts per second. Estimate the true rate.

Answer

\(n = m/(1 - m\tau) = 2000/(1 - 0.16) = 2381\) per second: 16 per cent of the counts were lost.

Exercise V2. What is the largest true rate a counter with \(\tau = 100\) ns can take with no more than 1 per cent loss?

Answer

The fractional loss is about \(m\tau\); \(m\tau = 0.01\) gives \(10^5\) counts per second.

Exercise V3. A bunch of \(10^8\) protons has rms size 2 mm. By how much does its centre fluctuate from turn to turn if the particles are fully reshuffled each turn?

Answer

\(\sigma/\sqrt{N} = 2\ \mathrm{mm}/10^4 = 0.2\) µm.

Pre-requisite (continued): Diodes, rectifiers and capacitor networks

Pre-requisite. This continues Diodes, rectifiers and capacitor networks in chapter 5, which treated the diode as a one-way valve. It adds what is inside a silicon diode: electrons and holes, doping, the p-n junction and its depleted layer, how a reverse voltage widens that layer, and the electron-hole pairs a charged particle frees in it. Section 11 uses this to explain the silicon detector as a solid ionisation chamber, and section 10’s PIN-diode loss monitors are the same device. The user’s books do not treat semiconductors; this section builds on Poisson’s equation and on the Fermi-Dirac picture of electrons in solids from chapter 2, and takes its silicon numbers from the Particle Data Group review cited in section 11 (section 35.8).

Electrons and holes. In a crystal the energies an electron may have form bands separated by gaps. In a metal the highest occupied band is partly full, and electrons move freely. In silicon the lower band (the valence band) is exactly full and the next (the conduction band) is empty at absolute zero, with a gap \(E_g = 1.12\) eV between them. A full band carries no current: for every electron moving one way another moves the other way. At room temperature a few electrons are shaken across the gap, with a probability that goes as the Boltzmann factor \(e^{-E_g/2k_BT}\) (the factor 2 because the Fermi level sits in the middle of the gap). Each leaves behind an empty state in the valence band, which the neighbouring electrons can shuffle into; the vacancy moves through the crystal like a positive charge and is called a hole. Pure silicon at room temperature has about \(10^{16}\) free electrons and as many holes per cubic metre (\(10^{10}\) per cm\(^3\)).

That is far too many for a detector. A wafer 1 cm\(^2\) by 300 µm holds \(3 \times 10^8\) free electrons and as many holes, while a particle crossing it frees about 23 000 (section 11): the signal would be lost in the current the free carriers carry under any applied voltage.

Doping. Replace a few silicon atoms (four outer electrons) with phosphorus (five): the fifth electron is barely bound and joins the conduction band, so the crystal has free electrons and is called n-type. Boron (three outer electrons) instead takes an electron from the valence band, leaving a hole: p-type. The dopant atoms stay fixed in the lattice, charged once they have given up or taken an electron. Detector silicon is very pure, lightly n-doped, with \(N_d \approx 10^{18}\) m\(^{-3}\) (\(10^{12}\) cm\(^{-3}\)), about one dopant atom in \(5 \times 10^{10}\) silicon atoms.

The p-n junction. Put a thin, heavily doped p layer on n-type silicon. Electrons from the n side diffuse into the p side and fill holes there; holes diffuse the other way. Near the boundary the free carriers disappear, leaving the fixed charged dopant atoms: positive on the n side, negative on the p side. Their field pushes back against further diffusion, and an equilibrium is reached with a layer empty of free carriers, the depleted layer, and a built-in potential of about 0.6 V across it. This is the junction of chapter 5’s silicon rectifier.

Reverse bias widens the layer. Make the n side positive. Electrons are pulled away from the junction and the depleted layer grows. On the lightly doped n side, the depleted region of width \(W\) holds a fixed charge density \(eN_d\), and the potential obeys Poisson’s equation in one dimension, \(d^2V/dx^2 = -eN_d/\varepsilon\), with \(\varepsilon = 11.7\,\varepsilon_0\) for silicon. Integrating once, the field falls linearly from its largest value at the junction to zero at the edge of the layer; integrating again, the voltage across the layer is

\[ V = \frac{eN_dW^2}{2\varepsilon} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad W = \sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon V}{eN_d}} . \]

The whole thickness \(d\) is depleted at

\[ V_{fd} = \frac{eN_dd^2}{2\varepsilon}. \]

For \(N_d = 10^{18}\) m\(^{-3}\) and \(d = 300\) µm, \(V_{fd} = 70\) V; at 100 V the layer would be 360 µm deep, so the wafer is fully depleted with room to spare. A fully depleted diode is a slab of solid with almost no free carriers and a field across it: an ionisation chamber filled with silicon instead of gas, 2330 kg/m\(^3\) against argon’s 1.7.

Electron-hole pairs. A charged particle crossing the depleted layer loses energy to its electrons (section 2), lifting many across the gap. On average 3.65 eV of lost energy makes one electron-hole pair, about three times the gap, because much of the energy goes into lattice vibrations rather than pairs; argon needs 26 eV per ion pair. The field sweeps electrons to the n side and holes to the p side within about 10 ns, and the moving charges induce a current pulse in the electrodes. A minimum-ionising particle frees about 23 000 pairs (3.7 fC) most probably in 300 µm, though its mean loss of 116 keV would make 31 800: the mean is pulled up by rare hard collisions (section 11). A 1 cm\(^2\) detector of that thickness has a capacitance \(\varepsilon A/d = 35\) pF, so 3.7 fC would make only 0.1 mV on it; detectors are read with charge-sensitive amplifiers, close to the silicon.

Leakage and cooling. Even depleted, the layer generates a few pairs thermally, and their drift is a steady leakage current. The generation goes roughly as the intrinsic carrier density, proportional to \(T^{3/2}e^{-E_g/2k_BT}\): from 20 °C to 27 °C it rises by 74 per cent, doubling about every 9 K near room temperature, and cooling to 0 °C cuts it to 18 per cent. Radiation damage displaces silicon atoms and creates states inside the gap, which make thermal generation much easier; a detector’s leakage grows with the dose it has taken, which is why trackers near a collision point are cooled.

Exercise W1. What voltage fully depletes a 500 µm detector with \(N_d = 5 \times 10^{17}\) m\(^{-3}\)?

Answer

\(V_{fd} = eN_dd^2/(2\varepsilon) = 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \times 5 \times 10^{17} \times (5 \times 10^{-4})^2/(2 \times 11.7 \times 8.854 \times 10^{-12}) = 97\) V.

Exercise W2. A 5.5 MeV alpha stops in silicon. How many pairs does it free, and what charge is that?

Answer

\(5.5 \times 10^6/3.65 = 1.5 \times 10^6\) pairs, \(1.5 \times 10^6 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19} = 0.24\) pC, as section 11 says (241 fC).

Exercise W3. Why is a silicon detector reverse biased rather than forward biased?

Answer

Forward bias collapses the depleted layer and lets a large current of free carriers flow, in which 23 000 extra electrons would be invisible. Reverse bias empties the layer of free carriers and leaves only the small leakage current, so the pairs a particle makes are the signal.

11. Detecting particles

Ionisation in a gas. A charged particle crossing a gas leaves electron-ion pairs, one for every \(W_i\) of energy lost: about 26 eV in argon, 34 eV in carbon dioxide, 37 eV in hydrogen (PDG, table 35.5). A 5.5 MeV alpha stopping in argon makes \(2.1 \times 10^5\) pairs, 34 fC; a minimum-ionising particle crossing 1 cm of argon makes about 97 pairs, \(1.6 \times 10^{-17}\) C. What the detector does with them depends on the field.

Consider a wire of radius \(a_w\) at voltage \(V\) inside a cylinder of radius \(b\). By Gauss’s law a charge \(\lambda\) per metre on the wire makes \(\mathcal{E} = \lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0r)\), and integrating from \(a_w\) to \(b\) gives \(V = (\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0)\ln(b/a_w)\). Eliminating \(\lambda\), the field at radius \(r\) is

\[ \mathcal{E}(r) = \frac{V}{r\ln(b/a_w)}. \]

For \(a_w = 12.5\) µm, \(b = 5\) mm and \(V = 2\) kV (assumed round values), \(\mathcal{E} = 267\) kV/cm at the wire’s surface and falls to 10 kV/cm, roughly where electrons begin to ionise further on their way, at 0.33 mm. Raise the voltage and the detector passes through three regimes:

  • Ionisation chamber. The field only collects the charge, without multiplying it; at too low a field ions and electrons recombine. Output proportional to energy deposited, no gain. The Curies used ionisation chambers with electrometers to measure activity from the late 1890s; today they measure dose.
  • Proportional counter. Electrons drifting into the last fraction of a millimetre around the wire gain enough energy between collisions to ionise, and each makes an avalanche. The gain, about \(10^4\), is fixed for a given voltage, so the pulse is still proportional to the energy deposited. The 97 electrons of a minimum-ionising track become about 160 fC, easily amplified. Georges Charpak at CERN made in 1968 a chamber of many parallel wires, each its own proportional counter, the multiwire proportional chamber, which read positions electronically at high rates (Nobel Prize 1992).
  • Geiger-Müller counter. At still higher voltage, ultraviolet photons from the avalanche start avalanches along the whole wire, and every particle gives the same large pulse, whatever its energy. The discharge is stopped by a quenching gas, organic vapour at first, halogens since Liebson’s tube of 1947. Halogen-quenched tubes run at 400 to 600 V. After each pulse the tube is dead for 50 to 100 µs, so rates above about 1000 counts per second are lost. For a dead time \(\tau\) the true rate \(n\) follows from the measured rate \(m\) by \(n = m/(1 - m\tau)\): with \(\tau = 100\) µs, 1000 counts per second measured means 1111 true.

The cloud chamber. C. T. R. Wilson made tracks visible in 1911 and described his expansion chamber in 1912. A quick expansion cools moist gas and leaves it supersaturated; droplets form on the ions along each track and are photographed in side light (chapter 1). Wilson shared the Nobel Prize in 1927; Anderson found the positron in a cloud chamber in 1932. Langsdorf’s diffusion chamber of 1936 is continuously sensitive, with alcohol vapour over a plate colder than \(-26\) °C.

Scintillators and photomultipliers. A scintillator converts part of the energy deposited into a flash of light; a photomultiplier converts the light into electrons at a photocathode (the photoelectric effect) and multiplies them by secondary emission at about ten dynodes (electrodes at rising voltages, each struck by the electrons from the one before and giving off several electrons for each that strikes it), about five electrons per stage, for gains of \(10^5\) to \(10^7\) and a time resolution of about 1 ns. Plastic scintillators are cheap, fast (nanoseconds) and can be made in any shape; inorganic crystals such as thallium-doped sodium iodide (Hofstadter filed a patent in 1948) give more light, hence better energy resolution, and are denser. A scintillation counter with a photomultiplier counts up to about \(10^7\) particles per second, about seven million times the 90 a minute (1.5 a second) of an observer at the microscope. Light output falls below proportionality at high ionisation density (Birks’ law).

Silicon. A reverse-biased silicon diode is a solid ionisation chamber: the depleted layer has almost no free carriers, and a particle crossing it frees electron-hole pairs, on average one for 3.65 eV at room temperature, about seven times less than argon needs (26 eV), in a medium a thousand times denser. A minimum-ionising particle loses on average 388 eV/µm, 116 keV in 300 µm, but the most probable loss is lower, because the mean is pulled up by rare hard collisions: about 23,000 electrons, 3.7 fC (PDG, section 35.8). A 5.5 MeV alpha stopping in silicon (range 28 µm) frees \(1.5 \times 10^6\) pairs, 241 fC. Silicon detectors made by the planar process of the integrated-circuit industry, divided into strips or pixels tens of micrometres apart, are now the trackers nearest the collision point in particle physics. They are damaged by radiation and must often be cooled.

12. Radiation protection: the quantities

  • Absorbed dose \(D\), energy deposited per unit mass, in gray: 1 Gy = 1 J/kg (named in 1975 by the 15th CGPM, the General Conference on Weights and Measures, which governs the SI).
  • Equivalent dose \(H_T\), the absorbed dose in an organ weighted by a radiation weighting factor \(w_R\), in sievert (named in 1979 by the 16th CGPM): \(w_R = 1\) for photons, electrons and muons, 2 for protons and charged pions, 20 for alphas and heavy ions, and between about 2.5 and 21 for neutrons depending on energy (ICRP, as tabulated by the PDG).
  • Effective dose \(E\), the sum over organs of \(H_T\) weighted by tissue factors (the share of the whole body’s risk of harm that each organ carries, adding to 1), in sievert: the quantity that limits are written in. Survey meters and badges measure operational stand-ins for it, the ambient and personal dose equivalents.

Natural background averages 2.4 mSv a year worldwide, about 0.27 µSv per hour. The ICRP (International Commission on Radiological Protection) recommends for radiation workers 20 mSv a year averaged over five years, with no more than 50 mSv in any one year; 1 mSv a year for the public. The European Union and Switzerland set 20 mSv a year, the United States 50 mSv; many laboratories set lower limits. Spread over 2000 working hours, 20 mSv is 10 µSv an hour.

13. Where the dose comes from, and the shield

Electrons: bremsstrahlung. An electron beam stopped in matter makes a forward cone of bremsstrahlung. Swanson’s rules of thumb (IAEA Technical Reports Series 188, 1979) for the absorbed dose rate at 1 m from a thick high-Z target (one of high atomic number \(Z\), such as tungsten or lead), per kilowatt of beam:

  • at 0°, \(20\,W_0^2\) Gy/h below 20 MeV and \(300\,W_0\) Gy/h above, with \(W_0\) the beam energy in MeV;
  • at 90°, about 50 Gy/h above 100 MeV, independent of energy.

A 100 MeV, 1 kW electron beam on a dump makes 30,000 Gy/h forward at 1 m. Above a threshold of 4 to 6 MeV in heavy nuclei and 10 to 19 MeV in light ones (2.23 MeV for deuterium), the photons also knock neutrons out of nuclei, mostly through the giant dipole resonance up to about 30 MeV (PDG, section 37.4), and above the resonance the unshielded neutron dose equivalent at 1 m is about 20 Sv/h per kilowatt (TRS 188 gives it in old units as “twice the power in watts” in rem per hour).

Protons and ions: neutrons. A proton above about 1 GeV stopped in a thick target almost surely makes a nuclear reaction; in copper or iron about 10 fast neutrons per 1 GeV proton, the yield growing roughly with energy (Forck, section 8.1). Deuterons give neutrons copiously even at a few MeV, because the deuteron is so loosely bound. Neutrons carry the dose through thick shielding: outside more than about a metre of concrete, neutrons dominate at electron and hadron machines above a few hundred MeV (PDG, section 37.4).

Activation. Nuclear reactions leave radioactive nuclei in magnets, pipes, collimators, cooling water, air and concrete. In metals the nuclides of concern include \(^{22}\)Na, \(^{54}\)Mn, \(^{56}\)Co, \(^{57}\)Co, \(^{58}\)Co and \(^{60}\)Co; in concrete \(^{24}\)Na at short decay times and \(^{22}\)Na later, with \(^{60}\)Co, \(^{152}\)Eu and \(^{134}\)Cs from traces of cobalt, europium and caesium (PDG, section 37.6). The dose rate at a given time after the beam stops is dominated by nuclides with half-lives of about that time. Activation is why a component is surveyed before it is touched, and why its dose rate falls fast in the first hours and slowly afterwards.

Shielding with tenth-value layers. In thick shields the dose falls exponentially, \(H = H_0\,e^{-d/\lambda} = H_0\,10^{-d/\mathrm{TVL}}\), with attenuation length \(\lambda\) and tenth-value layer \(\mathrm{TVL} = \lambda\ln 10\): since \(10 = e^{\ln10}\), \(10^{-d/\mathrm{TVL}} = e^{-d\ln10/\mathrm{TVL}}\), and this equals \(e^{-d/\lambda}\) when \(\mathrm{TVL} = \lambda\ln10 = 2.303\lambda\). Each TVL cuts the dose by ten. Thicknesses are tabulated as mass per area (g/cm\(^2\)), because what stops radiation is the amount of matter crossed; dividing by the density gives a length: 115 g/cm\(^2\) over 2.35 g/cm\(^3\) is 48.9 cm. In ordinary concrete of density 2.35 g/cm\(^3\):

Radiation Value Thickness of concrete
Bremsstrahlung from electrons above 100 MeV TVL about 115 g/cm\(^2\) 0.49 m
Giant-resonance neutrons (Am-Be spectrum) TVL 96 g/cm\(^2\) 0.41 m
Neutrons above about 200 MeV \(\lambda\) about 117 g/cm\(^2\) \(\lambda\) = 0.50 m, TVL = 1.15 m

(TRS 188, tables XLI and XLII; PDG, section 37.4.) Livingston and Blewett (chapter 14, section 14-4, p. 524), from measurements at the National Bureau of Standards, give one terminal coefficient of 0.0218 cm\(^2\)/g for bremsstrahlung from electrons of 20 to 100 MeV, a TVL of 106 g/cm\(^2\), inside the experimental range of 101 to 122 that TRS 188 lists. Three metres of concrete reduce high-energy neutrons by a factor of \(2.4 \times 10^{-3}\). To bring the 30,000 Gy/h forward of the 100 MeV, 1 kW dump down to 10 µSv/h takes 9.5 tenth-value layers of 0.49 m, 4.6 m of concrete; this is why dumps are put underground or inside iron and concrete blocks. Water and polyethylene, rich in hydrogen, slow neutrons well; iron stops high-energy neutrons better per metre but lets low-energy ones through, so iron shields are finished with concrete.

Interlocks. The shield is complemented by the rule that nobody is inside it when beam can be produced. The principles in TRS 188 (section 6.4): an enclosure with interlocked doors; passive barriers before active devices; fail-safe design, so that any failure turns the beam off; redundancy, typically two switches in series; flashing warning lights that operate when and only when radiation is produced; an audible warning with a time delay before start-up; emergency-off switches inside, which must be reset where they were pressed before the machine can restart; any bypass authorised, logged and posted; all of it tested at intervals of no more than six months; and, where beam can be sent to more than one area, redundant beam stoppers and locked-off magnets upstream of an occupied area.

How it was researched and built

Counting, 1903 to 1932. The spinthariscope (1903) made single particles visible and set the method of nuclear physics for twenty-five years. Bragg and Kleeman’s work (1904 and 1905) measured how alphas lose energy and range along their path. Rutherford and Geiger’s electrical counter (1908) used gas multiplication near a wire to count alphas one by one, and with it they measured the number of alphas a gram of radium emits. The Bothe-Geiger coincidence experiment of 1924 and 1925 recorded the pulses of two counters photographically, through string electrometers, to about 1 ms, the start of coincidence counting. The Geiger-Müller tube (Kiel, 1928) counted any ionising particle, but its pulses still had to be recorded. Wynn-Williams’ thyratron scale of two (Cavendish, 1932) divided the rate so that a mechanical register could keep up. Through these years the cloud chamber (1911) showed what a counter could not: the event itself, its geometry and, in a magnetic field, its momentum, at one expansion every several seconds or more.

The theory, 1930 to 1933. Bohr’s classical stopping theory (1913) had the right form; its logarithm, with the classical cut-off \(b_{min} \approx z e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\gamma m_e v^2)\), is the right one only where that exceeds the quantum cut-off \(\hbar/(2\gamma m_e v)\), that is where \(2z\alpha_f/\beta > 1\) (\(\alpha_f\) the fine-structure constant): for slow, highly charged ions. Bethe’s quantum calculation (1930, relativistic 1932) gave the formula used ever since, with one material constant, the mean excitation energy. Bloch (1933) estimated it as about \(10\,Z\) eV for an atom of atomic number \(Z\) and added a low-energy correction. Tabulated stopping powers and ranges (today the NIST PSTAR and ASTAR tables, ICRU Report 49, and SRIM for ions) are this theory fitted to measurements.

Light into electrons, 1934 to 1948. The photomultiplier came out of television research. Iams and Salzberg at RCA built a single-stage tube in 1934 (gain about 8); Kubetsky in the Soviet Union built a multi-dynode tube in 1934 with gains above a thousand; Zworykin, Morton and Malter at RCA published the first full analysis of a multi-dynode tube in 1936. In 1944 Samuel Curran, working at Berkeley, put the new RCA photomultiplier behind a scintillator and made the first electronic scintillation counter. Hofstadter’s sodium iodide crystal followed in 1948. Within a few years the eye at the microscope had been replaced.

The beam’s own instruments, from the 1950s. As synchrotrons and colliders appeared, beams had to be measured without being stopped. The current transformer and the electrostatic pick-up, both applications of the image current, became standard. The DC transformer made it possible to read a stored beam’s current continuously; Unser’s paper of 1981 describes its high-resolution form. Transition radiation, predicted in 1945 and seen in 1959, was turned into a profile monitor for electron beams in the 1970s (Wartski and colleagues, 1975). Charpak’s multiwire chamber (1968) gave profile monitors for weak extracted beams as well as detectors for experiments. Wire scanners, residual-gas monitors, synchrotron-light monitors and the many kinds of loss monitor were developed laboratory by laboratory. Their reports fill the Beam Instrumentation Workshop (BIW) and DIPAC (Diagnostics and Instrumentation for Particle Accelerators) conference series. The units of dose were settled in the SI with the gray (1975) and the sievert (1979).

What a laboratory measures with today. In a modern machine nearly every element of the list above is present, often in hundreds. A transfer line has transformers for current, screens and wire grids for profiles, position monitors after each quadrupole pair, and a loss monitor near each collimator. A ring has a DC transformer, a wall-current monitor, position monitors read turn by turn, a tune system, profile monitors of the non-intercepting kinds and a loss monitor system tied to the machine protection. The design of an instrument starts from the beam: its species and energy (which decide whether a cup, a screen or a wire can survive and whether a pick-up sees a thin disc of field), its charge per bunch and bunch length (which set signal and bandwidth), its size (which sets resolution) and its power (which decides whether anything may touch it at all). Electrodes and pick-ups are designed with electromagnetic field codes, heating of intercepting devices with finite-element thermal codes, the response of detectors and loss monitors with shower codes such as GEANT and FLUKA, and the optics with programs such as MAD. Electronics have moved from analogue sum-and-difference hybrids to fast digitisers and digital receivers, which reduce each bunch to a position in software. Radiation shielding is designed with the tenth-value layers above for a first estimate and with shower codes for the final design. The whole practice of commissioning with these instruments is chapter 29.

At the bench

This section collects what an experimenter sees and does, from the GSI instruments described in Forck’s lecture notes, the IAEA safety report TRS 188, and the PDG reviews.

Reading a Faraday cup. The cup is driven into the beam by a pneumatic or motor actuator through a bellows; a lamp or limit switch shows its position. The current goes to a picoammeter or a current-to-voltage amplifier with switchable feedback resistors, so that 1 µA reads as 1 V on a range of 1 MΩ, 1 nA as 1 V on 1 GΩ. Typical readings: tens of picoamperes for a weak radioactive-ion beam, microamperes from a small accelerator, milliamperes from a high-current linear accelerator. The steps: insert the cup; with no beam, read the offset and leakage (a damp feedthrough or a dirty insulator shows as a current with no beam); switch on the suppressor and raise it until the reading stops changing, a few hundred volts; then read. A reading that rises with suppression voltage switched off is secondary electrons escaping; a reading that changes with the position of a magnet far upstream may be stray electrons from the pipe reaching the cup. A cooled cup’s reading includes a small current through the cooling water.

Reading a transformer. On the oscilloscope the transformer shows the beam pulse: a flat top for a linear accelerator’s macro-pulse, with the slow rise of space-charge compensation after the source; a staircase during multi-turn injection into a ring; a ramp and decay through a synchrotron cycle. A test winding through the ring carries a known calibration pulse. Signatures: a sagging flat top on a long pulse is droop; a reading that changes when a turbo pump is switched on is pick-up of vibration or stray field; a large reading with the beam steered onto the pipe nearby is secondary electrons flowing through the ring. A DC transformer is zeroed when the ring is empty, after the magnets ramp down; between zeros it drifts by some microamperes a day.

Reading position monitors. The display is an orbit: a reading in millimetres at each monitor round the ring or along the line. The raw signals are millivolts to volts per bunch at the electrodes. Calibration has three parts: the electronic gain of each channel (switched or injected test signals), the geometric sensitivity (computed, or mapped on a bench with a stretched wire), and the offset between the monitor’s electrical centre and the quadrupole’s magnetic centre, which is found with the beam itself by changing the quadrupole’s strength and finding the position at which the orbit does not move (chapter 18). Signatures: one monitor that disagrees with its neighbours in a way the optics cannot produce is a bad cable, a swapped pair of electrodes or a failed channel; readings that drift with the hall’s temperature are electronics.

Screens. The camera picture shows a spot, its centre and width read off a projection. Before the beam, the screen is lit by a lamp to check focus and the calibration marks; with beam, the camera’s gain and exposure and a set of neutral filters are adjusted so that the brightest pixel is not saturated, since a saturated image is wider than the beam. A slow screen such as Chromolux smears the image of a short pulse and keeps glowing; a YAG screen shows a beam of only a million ions. Screens age: their efficiency falls with dose, and a spot that is permanently darker, or one that keeps glowing where the beam has been, shows damage. An intense beam destroys a screen or a foil outright. In the GSI example, a tantalum foil 1 µm thick in a 10 mA beam of argon ions at 11.4 MeV per nucleon, in 1 ms pulses every 20 ms, reached about 2000 °C during each pulse and fell to about 200 °C between them (Forck, figure 2.25); the peak, not the average, decides whether it survives. For high-power beams screens are replaced by optical transition radiation from thin foils, by residual-gas monitors, or used only with a reduced, short pulse. Plastic scintillators lose transparency at about \(10^4\) Gy, and secondary-emission foils at the CERN SPS lost half their yield after \(10^{18}\) protons per square centimetre at 450 GeV.

Wire scans and emittance scans. A wire scan takes seconds to minutes and assumes the beam stays the same throughout; a profile that is ragged or asymmetric from scan to scan is a beam that is changing, not a profile. A wire that breaks shows as a signal that stops partway through a scan; a wire that sags from heating shows as a profile that shifts. A slit-and-grid emittance scan takes minutes per plane; a pepper-pot gives one picture per pulse, after a laser calibration of the camera.

The dark-adapted eye. Before electronic counting, and still with any weak screen, the observer prepared the eye. Geiger and Marsden sat in the dark about 30 minutes before counting; a counting spell lasted about a minute before the observer needed relief; observers took turns (APS News, June 2012; chapter 1). A weak spot on a phosphor screen in a darkened control room is still found the same way.

A survey meter in a radiation area. Near a machine the reading on a survey meter tells three things. With beam off, it reads the natural background: the world average of 2.4 mSv a year is 0.27 µSv per hour, and the range of 1 to 13 mSv a year quoted by the PDG is about 0.1 to 1.5 µSv per hour. Near a component after a run, the reading rises as the meter approaches, falls by the inverse square with distance, and falls with time as short-lived activity decays: the first measurement fixes how long work may take there. A Geiger-Müller survey meter reads photons and electrons and saturates at high rates because of its dead time; an ionisation-chamber meter reads dose rate over a wider range; neutrons need a meter with a moderator, a polyethylene sphere around a thermal-neutron counter. Signs on the area state the highest dose rate within. A personal dosimeter is worn throughout and read against the limit.

Search and secure. Before beam is switched on, the enclosure is cleared. The outline below is general, not any one laboratory’s rule, and the details differ from place to place: the operator in charge takes the access keys back so that no one is inside with a key; trained searchers walk the enclosure in a fixed order, looking into every corner and behind every shielding block, and press search buttons placed along the route, so that the system knows the route has been completed; the doors are closed behind them and interlocked. An audible warning sounds, warning lights flash, and after a time delay that allows anyone left inside to press an emergency-off switch, the beam is permitted. Opening any door, or pressing any emergency switch, removes the permit; the search must then be repeated, and the trip reset first where it occurred and then at the control console (TRS 188, section 6.4).

Timescales. A transformer or wall-current monitor reads in nanoseconds; a position monitor bunch by bunch or, averaged, in milliseconds; a residual-gas monitor at camera frame rates, about 100 pictures a second; a wire scan in seconds; a slit emittance scan in minutes; activation decays over hours to years.

Where it stops

Every instrument meets one of three limits. An instrument that touches the beam changes it and is destroyed by it: cups stop working above about 100 MeV per nucleon, screens and wires melt or darken, and above a few kilowatts in a small spot nothing may touch the beam at all. An instrument that does not touch it reads a small signal against noise: the transformer’s microampere, the residual-gas monitor’s picoamperes, the button’s millivolts from a weak bunch. And imaging is limited by optics: a synchrotron-light monitor is limited by diffraction to about 100 µm and more, which is coarse against the micrometre beams of modern light sources and linear colliders. Counters have their own limit, dead time and pile-up, about a thousand counts per second for a Geiger tube and \(10^7\) for a scintillator.

Two ideas carry past these limits. The first is to measure the beam through its own dynamics instead of its picture: the tune, the response to a kick, the orbit change when a quadrupole moves, from which the optics and emittance follow (chapter 16, chapter 18); and to image with the beam’s own radiation at short wavelengths or with interference (chapter 20). The second is that the pick-up, which reads the beam, can be turned round to act on it. A pick-up sees not only the beam’s centre but the statistical noise of its finite number of particles. Amplified and fed to a kicker further round the ring, that signal can reduce the beam’s spread: stochastic cooling, which made the antiproton collider possible (chapter 24). The same image currents that make the signals act back on the beam and limit its intensity (chapter 22).

The beams these instruments first measured, from the multiplier and the electrostatic generator, were limited in energy by the voltage one machine could hold. The way past that limit, giving a particle the same modest voltage many times, begins in chapter 9.

Exercises

1. A Faraday cup reads 2.0 µA of 10 MeV protons. How many protons per second arrive, what power does the cup absorb, and how deep do they go in copper?

Answer

\(\dot N = I/e = 2.0 \times 10^{-6}/1.602 \times 10^{-19} = 1.25 \times 10^{13}\) per second. Power \(= I \times W/e = 2.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 10 \times 10^{6} = 20\) W. The PSTAR range at 10 MeV is 0.2199 g/cm\(^2\); divided by 8.96 g/cm\(^3\) it is 0.245 mm. The heat is all in the front quarter-millimetre.

2. The cup has no suppression and each proton releases on average 2 secondary electrons, all of which escape. What does it read? A permanent-magnet field of 10 mT is added: what is the gyroradius of a 10 eV electron, and why does it suppress?

Answer

Each proton brings \(+e\) and each escaping electron takes away \(-e\), so the cup gains \(3e\) per proton and reads 6.0 µA, three times the truth. With \(r_c = \sqrt{2 m_e W}/(eB)\), \(W = 10\) eV and \(B = 0.01\) T: \(r_c = 1.07\) mm. Electrons spiral round field lines a millimetre across and, with the field across the cup, cannot travel the centimetres to its mouth.

3. For the GSI passive transformer (\(\mu_r = 10^5\), \(r_i = 70\) mm, \(r_o = 90\) mm, \(l = 16\) mm, \(N = 10\), \(R = 50\) Ω) find \(L\), the droop time constant and the sensitivity. What is the thermal-noise floor over 300 MHz at 300 K, in volts and in equivalent beam current at 4 V/A?

Answer

\(L = (\mu_0\mu_r/2\pi)\,l N^2\ln(r_o/r_i) = 2 \times 10^{-2} \times 0.016 \times 100 \times \ln(9/7) = 8.04\) mH. \(\tau_{droop} = L/R = 0.161\) ms (quoted 0.2 ms). \(R/N = 5\) V/A (quoted 4 V/A). \(\sqrt{4 k_B T R\,\Delta f} = \sqrt{4 \times 1.381 \times 10^{-23} \times 300 \times 50 \times 3 \times 10^8} = 15.8\) µV, which at 4 V/A is 3.9 µA. The quoted resolution of 40 µA rms is ten times worse: the amplifier dominates.

4. Derive the small-displacement sensitivity \(S = 4\sin(\alpha/2)/(\alpha a)\) of a pair of electrodes from the image-current density, and evaluate it for \(a = 25\) mm and \(\alpha = 30^\circ\). A beam gives \(\Delta U/\Sigma U = 0.10\): where is it?

Answer

To first order, \(j_{im} = (I/2\pi a)[1 + (2r/a)\cos(\phi - \theta)]\). Integrating \(a\,j_{im}\) over \(\phi\) from \(-\alpha/2\) to \(\alpha/2\): \(I_R = (I/2\pi)[\alpha + (2r/a)\cos\theta \cdot 2\sin(\alpha/2)]\), and \(I_L\) the same with a minus sign. The ratio is \((2r\cos\theta/a)\cdot 2\sin(\alpha/2)/\alpha\). With \(\alpha = 0.5236\) rad: \(S = 4 \times 0.2588/(0.5236 \times 25\ \mathrm{mm}) = 0.0791\) per mm, 7.91 per cent per millimetre. \(x = 0.10/0.0791 = 1.26\) mm; at this offset the exact integral gives 0.0997 instead of 0.1000, a difference of 0.3 per cent.

5. A 180° spectrometer analyses 2 MeV protons with a field giving \(\rho = 0.5\) m. The source is 1 mm wide and the accepted half-angle 10 mrad. Find the field, the smallest resolvable momentum and energy differences.

Answer

\(pc = \sqrt{W(W + 2 m_p c^2)} = \sqrt{2 \times 1878.5} = 61.30\) MeV, \(B\rho = 0.2045\) T m, so \(B = 0.409\) T. The aberration is \(\rho\alpha^2 = 0.5 \times 10^{-4}\) m \(= 0.05\) mm, so \(\delta_{min} = (1 + 0.05)\ \mathrm{mm}/(2 \times 500\ \mathrm{mm}) = 1.05 \times 10^{-3}\). With \(\gamma = 1.00213\), \(\Delta W/W = (1 + 1/\gamma)\delta = 2.10 \times 10^{-3}\), 4.2 keV. The source width, not the aberration, dominates; narrowing the source buys resolution at the cost of current.

6. Repeat the GSI time-of-flight measurement: pick-ups 1.629 m apart, three bunches between them at 36.136 MHz, oscilloscope reading 15.82 ns. Find \(\beta\), the energy per nucleon, and the uncertainty for 1 mm and 0.1 ns.

Answer

\(T = 1/f = 27.673\) ns, \(t = 3T + 15.82 = 98.84\) ns, \(\beta = L/(ct) = 0.05498\), \(\gamma - 1 = 1.515 \times 10^{-3}\), \(W = 1.411\) MeV per atomic mass unit (931.494 MeV). \(\Delta\beta/\beta = \sqrt{(1/1629)^2 + (0.1/98.84)^2} = 1.18 \times 10^{-3}\), and \(\Delta W/W = \gamma(\gamma + 1)\Delta\beta/\beta = 2.37 \times 10^{-3}\), 3.3 keV per nucleon.

7. Show that in a quadrupole scan with a thin lens and drift \(L\), fitting \(\sigma_{11}(K) = a(K - b)^2 + c\) gives \(\varepsilon = \sqrt{ac}/L^2\).

Answer

From the coefficients, \(\sigma_{11} = a/L^2\) and \(\sigma_{12} = -(a/L^2)(1/L + b)\). Then \(ab^2 + c = \sigma_{11} + 2L\sigma_{12} + L^2\sigma_{22}\) gives \(L^2\sigma_{22} = ab^2 + c - a/L^2 + 2(a/L^2)(1 + bL)\), so \(\sigma_{22} = [a(1 + bL)^2 + cL^2]/L^4\). Then \(\sigma_{11}\sigma_{22} - \sigma_{12}^2 = [a^2(1 + bL)^2 + acL^2 - a^2(1 + bL)^2]/L^6 = ac/L^4\), and \(\varepsilon = \sqrt{\det\Sigma} = \sqrt{ac}/L^2\). Numerically, an assumed beam of 2 µm through \(L = 5\) m with 3 per cent noise on 15 points returns 2.05 µm.

8. Evaluate the Bethe formula for a 150 MeV proton in water (\(Z/A = 0.5551\), \(I_e = 75\) eV) without the density term, and compare with PSTAR. How far does the proton go, and by what factor has its stopping power risen when it is down to 1 MeV?

Answer

\(\gamma = 1.1599\), \(\beta^2 = 0.2567\), \(\beta^2\gamma^2 = 0.3453\), \(W_{max} = 0.3524\) MeV. The bracket is \(\frac{1}{2}\ln[2 \times 0.511 \times 0.3453 \times 0.3524/(75 \times 10^{-6})^2] - 0.2567 = 8.20\), and \(0.307075 \times 0.5551/0.2567 \times 8.20 = 5.445\) MeV cm\(^2\)/g; PSTAR gives 5.443. The CSDA range is 15.77 cm. At 1 MeV the stopping power is 260.8 MeV cm\(^2\)/g, 48 times the entrance value: this is the Bragg peak.

9. A 100 MeV electron beam of 1 kW is stopped in a thick dump. Using Swanson’s rule, estimate the forward dose rate at 1 m and the thickness of ordinary concrete (TVL 115 g/cm\(^2\), density 2.35 g/cm\(^3\)) needed to bring it to 10 µSv/h. Compare with the neutron attenuation length of 117 g/cm\(^2\) for a proton machine.

Answer

\(300\,W_0 = 30{,}000\) Gy/h at 1 m. The factor to remove is \(3 \times 10^4/10^{-5} = 3 \times 10^9\), \(\log_{10} = 9.48\) tenth-value layers of \(115/2.35 = 0.489\) m: 4.6 m of concrete, ignoring the inverse square, which in practice helps. For high-energy neutrons the TVL is \(117 \ln 10/2.35 = 1.15\) m, more than twice as thick: proton machines need deeper shields per decade.

10. Show that backward optical transition radiation from a perfect conductor gives about \((2\alpha_f/\pi)[\ln(2\gamma) - 1/2]\ln(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)\) photons per particle, with \(\alpha_f\) the fine-structure constant, and evaluate it for 1 GeV electrons between 400 and 700 nm.

Answer

Write \(e^2/(4\pi^3\varepsilon_0 c) = \alpha_f\hbar/\pi^2\). Then \(d^2N/(d\omega\,d\Omega) = (\alpha_f/\pi^2\omega)\,\beta^2\sin^2\theta/(1 - \beta^2\cos^2\theta)^2\). The solid-angle integral over the hemisphere is \(2\pi\int_0^{\pi/2} \beta^2\sin^3\theta\,d\theta/(1 - \beta^2\cos^2\theta)^2 \approx 2\pi[\ln(2\gamma) - 1/2]\) for \(\gamma \gg 1\), and \(\int d\omega/\omega = \ln(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)\). So \(N = (2\alpha_f/\pi)[\ln(2\gamma) - 1/2]\ln(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)\). For \(\gamma = 1957\): \(\ln(2\gamma) - 1/2 = 7.77\), \(\ln(7/4) = 0.560\), \(N = 0.00465 \times 7.77 \times 0.560 = 0.0202\): one photon per 49 electrons, matching the numerical integral.
