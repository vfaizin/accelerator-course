15 The synchrotron

1944 to 1960: Oliphant’s memorandum, Veksler and McMillan, the converted betatron at Woolwich, the General Electric 70 MeV ring, the Cosmotron, the Bevatron and the Dubna synchrophasotron; past the wall of the solid magnet, whose iron grows as the cube of the momentum, by a ring of fixed radius whose field and frequency rise with the particle.

In one page

The wall. A cyclotron or synchrocyclotron bends its particles in one solid magnet, and the orbit grows with the momentum, so the pole must be as wide as the last orbit. The iron grows as the cube of the radius. A betatron keeps a fixed orbit but needs a core whose flux grows as the square of the radius, and electrons in it radiate. By 1950 both had reached a few hundred MeV (chapter 11, chapter 12).

The idea. Keep the orbit at one radius \(\rho\). Raise the magnetic field in step with the momentum, so the magnet is only a ring. Give the energy from a radio-frequency gap or cavity at one or a few places, and raise its frequency in step with the speed. Phase stability, found by Veksler in 1944 and McMillan in 1945, holds the particles in step with the wave if the field and frequency are only nearly right.

The equations to remember. For a particle of charge \(q\) (C) and momentum \(p\) (kg m/s) on a fixed bending radius \(\rho\) (m) in a ring of circumference \(C\) (m):

\[ B(t) = \frac{p(t)}{q\rho}, \qquad f_{RF} = h f_0 = \frac{h\beta c}{C} \]

the field (T) follows the momentum; the RF frequency (Hz) is a whole number \(h\) of revolution frequencies.

\[ q\hat{V}\sin\phi_s = q\,C\rho\,\frac{dB}{dt} \]

the energy gain per turn (J) that the rising field demands, which fixes the synchronous phase \(\phi_s\) for a voltage amplitude \(\hat{V}\) (V).

\[ Q_x = \sqrt{1-n}, \quad Q_y = \sqrt{n}, \quad \alpha_c = \frac{1}{1-n} = \frac{1}{Q_x^2} \]

the tunes (betatron oscillations per turn) and momentum compaction (relative change of path length per relative change of momentum) of a weak-focusing ring with field index \(0 < n < 1\) and no straight sections; all three are defined and derived in sections 5 and 6.

\[ \eta = \alpha_c - \frac{1}{\gamma^2}, \qquad \gamma_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_c}} = Q_x < 1 \]

the phase-slip factor and the transition gamma: in a weak-focusing ring \(\gamma_t\) is below 1, so every particle is above transition and none ever crosses it.

Numbers to know. Goward and Barnes turned a 4 MeV betatron into an 8 MeV synchrotron at Woolwich in August 1946. The General Electric 70 MeV synchrotron, with a glass vacuum chamber, showed synchrotron light on 24 April 1947. The Cosmotron passed 1 GeV in 1952 and reached 3.3 GeV in 1953. The Bevatron reached 6.2 GeV in 1954, above the 5.63 GeV threshold for making antiprotons, which were found in October 1955. Its 15.24 m orbit needed 0.030 T at its 10 MeV injection and 1.55 T at the top, the revolution frequency rose from 0.36 to 2.5 MHz, and the protons gained about 1.5 keV a turn for 1.85 s, about four million turns. Its magnet weighed about 10,000 tons, the Dubna synchrophasotron’s (10 GeV, 1957) 36,000 tonnes: the mass grows roughly as \(p^3\), and that is the wall that strong focusing removed.

The wall

In the years just after the Second World War there were three kinds of circular accelerator, and each was near its end.

The cyclotron of chapter 10 keeps a fixed frequency and a fixed field, and its orbit spirals out as the particle gains momentum. Its revolution angular frequency is \(qB/(\gamma m)\), so it falls out of step once \(\gamma\) departs from 1 by more than about the inverse of the number of turns: a few tens of MeV for protons. The synchrocyclotron of chapter 12 lowers its frequency as the particle’s mass grows and so removes the relativistic limit, but it keeps the spiral orbit. Its magnet must fill the whole circle of the last orbit. Lawrence’s 184-inch magnet, completed as a synchrocyclotron in November 1946, later gave protons of about 340 to 350 MeV (340 MeV in his Nobel lecture of 1951; later accounts give 350 MeV). For 6 GeV at 1.5 T the last orbit would have a radius of 15 m, and the pole a face of 730 m\(^2\). At a fixed field the radius grows in proportion to the momentum, and the iron in proportion to its cube.

The betatron of chapter 11 keeps the orbit at one radius, but it gives the energy through the flux in a central core. The flux needed grows as the square of the radius times the field. A betatron on the Bevatron’s 15.24 m orbit would need a swing of 2260 Wb, a core of about 750 m\(^2\) swung from \(-1.5\) T to \(+1.5\) T on every cycle, more than the 730 m\(^2\) enclosed by the orbit itself. And an electron on a 1 m orbit at 300 MeV radiates 721 eV a turn, a loss growing as the fourth power of the energy. Kerst’s 300 MeV betatron at Illinois (1950) was the end of that line.

What was wanted was the betatron’s fixed orbit without its core, and the cyclotron’s resonant gap without its solid pole.

Oliphant, 1943. Mark Oliphant, the Australian head of physics at Birmingham, spent the war years partly in the United States on the electromagnetic separation of uranium. In September 1943, by E. J. N. Wilson’s account, he wrote a classified memorandum to the British authorities proposing that “particles should be constrained to move in a circle of constant radius” (Oliphant, quoted by Wilson), in an annular magnet whose field rises as the particles gain energy from an alternating voltage on hollow electrodes. He knew, he wrote later, what would happen to particles that led or lagged in phase, but he did not make the full analysis, and the memorandum was not published.

Veksler, 1944. Vladimir Veksler, at the Lebedev Physical Institute in Moscow, published two notes in Doklady Akademii Nauk SSSR in 1944. The first showed that resonant acceleration of relativistic particles was possible in a constant magnetic field, by “automatic phasing” (the idea behind the microtron). The second, received on 8 July 1944, extended it to a magnetic field rising in time. It says, in Russian, that in such an accelerator the magnetic field has the task of guiding the orbits, while the acceleration is done by an alternating electric field; that, unlike the betatron, the radiation from the electrons need not spoil the acceleration, because the phasing compensates for it; and that the magnet can be a narrow ring, which is extremely advantageous. It states two conditions: the field may vary only a little over the radius, and the voltage induced per turn by the changing field must be small compared with the RF voltage. An English version appeared in the Journal of Physics of the USSR in 1945.

McMillan, 1945. Edwin McMillan, at Los Alamos and planning his return to Berkeley, saw the same principle in June 1945. His letter, “The synchrotron, a proposed high energy particle accelerator”, appeared in Physical Review in September 1945. It coined both the words “synchrotron” and “phase stability”, and it worked out the equations of the phase oscillation. Only after publication did he learn of Veksler’s notes, which had not reached the United States in wartime. He acknowledged Veksler’s priority; both agreed that the ideas were independent; and they shared the Atoms for Peace Award in 1963 (Jackson and Panofsky’s memoir of McMillan).

The principle was now clear: a ring magnet whose field follows the momentum, a gap whose frequency follows the speed, and phase stability to make up for imperfect programming. What remained was to show that it worked, then to build it for protons at energies no cyclotron could reach, and to find out what such a ring costs.

The physics

1. Three programmes on one ring

The physical picture is a racetrack of magnets around an evacuated pipe. The magnets bend the particle on arcs of fixed radius \(\rho\). Between arcs there may be straight sections, free of field, for the injector (the smaller accelerator that supplies the particles), the accelerating gap and the targets. Three things are programmed in time: the field in the magnets, the frequency of the voltage on the gap, and its amplitude. If the field is too strong for the particle’s momentum, the particle curves inward and hits the inner wall; too weak, and it hits the outer wall. If the frequency is wrong, the particle arrives at the gap at the wrong moment and is decelerated. The orbit is held near the middle of the pipe only if all three move together. The next three sections derive what each must do.

Coordinates: \(s\) is the distance along the reference orbit, \(x\) the horizontal displacement, positive outward, and \(y\) the vertical. \(C\) is the circumference of the reference orbit and \(R = C/2\pi\) its average radius. In a ring without straight sections \(R = \rho\); with straight sections \(R > \rho\).

A synchrotron ring drawn to the Bevatron’s proportions: four bending magnets, each a quarter circle of radius \(\rho = 15.24\) m, joined by four field-free straights of about 6.1 m, for a circumference near 120 m. The injector feeds one straight, the accelerating gap sits in another, the target in a third. The local axes: \(s\) along the orbit, \(x\) outward, away from the ring’s centre, \(y\) up, out of the page. With \((x, y, s)\) right-handed and the field along \(+y\), a positive particle is bent inward only if it runs clockwise as seen from above, as drawn.

Pre-requisite (continued): Magnetic circuits and iron

Pre-requisite. Chapter 8 taught iron as a magnetic material: the field \(H\) and the relative permeability \(\mu_r\), the magnetic circuit of a magnet with a gap, domains, saturation near 2 T and, in passing, hysteresis. This section adds what a ring magnet, cycled from almost nothing to full field, does with them: why its field stops following its current at high field, why a field of fixed size stays in the gap when the current is zero (remanence) and how big it is, and why a ferrite, a magnetic insulator, can be tuned by a bias current. The next section uses the first three to explain why a ring is programmed by its measured field, and section 10 uses remanence for the choice of injection field; section 13 uses the ferrite. Taught from Livingston and Blewett, §8-2 (the magnetic circuit and the permeability of iron), §§8-9 and 12-4 (remanent fields), §13-5 (the Cosmotron’s magnet and supply) and §§13-7 and 13-8 (ferrite); Chao and others, Handbook, §7.2.2 (the remanent field in a gap).

The magnet with a gap, recalled. Chapter 8 wrote Ampère’s law round a magnet of \(N\) turns carrying \(I\) (A), with a path \(l\) (m) in iron and a gap \(g\) (m) where the beam runs, and the same flux density \(B\) in both:

\[ NI = H_{iron}\,l + H_{gap}\,g, \qquad B = \frac{\mu_0 NI}{g + l/\mu_r} \approx \frac{\mu_0 NI}{g}. \]

While \(l/\mu_r\) is small against \(g\), the gap sets the field and the iron only carries the flux round; the field is proportional to the current, and the inductance \(L = N\Phi/I\) (H), with \(\Phi\) the flux through one turn, is a constant. A synchrotron magnet leaves that region at both ends of its cycle.

At work. The Cosmotron’s gap was 9 in, 0.229 m. For 1.4 T the gap alone needs \(NI = Bg/\mu_0 = 2.55\times10^5\) ampere-turns. Scale-model measurements gave \(3.36\times10^5\) (Livingston and Blewett, §13-5), 32 per cent more. The excess drives the iron, which at 1.4 T is approaching saturation, and the flux that leaks outside the gap.

Saturation: the field stops following the current. As \(B\) in the iron approaches 2 T, nearly all the domains are already aligned, and more current adds little magnetisation. The relative permeability falls, from thousands to hundreds and below. With a 3 m iron path behind a 0.30 m gap, \(l/\mu_r\) is 1 per cent of \(g\) at \(\mu_r = 1000\), 10 per cent at 100 and half of it at 20. So the field per ampere falls at the top of the cycle, and the inductance with it. Iron’s permeability also changes at the bottom: it is modest at the weakest fields, climbs to a maximum at moderate fields, and falls in saturation (Livingston and Blewett, §8-2). The Cosmotron’s models showed exactly this in the inductance of the whole magnet: 0.47 H at the start, 0.80 H near 0.1 T, and 0.25 H at the top (§13-5). A field proportional to the current is therefore an approximation that fails at both ends of the cycle.

Hysteresis and remanence. Chapter 8 noted that the magnetisation of iron depends on where it has been. Drive the iron up to a high field and bring \(H\) back to zero: the domains do not all return, and a closed ring of iron keeps a flux density \(B_r\), the remanence, which can be of order 1 T. To bring \(B\) to zero, \(H\) must be driven backwards to \(-H_c\), where \(H_c\) (A/m) is the coercive field; it is small for the soft steels used in magnets, of order 100 A/m or less. A full cycle traces a closed loop in the \(B\)–\(H\) plane, the hysteresis loop, and the area inside it is the energy turned into heat per cubic metre per cycle.

Now put the gap back. With the current off, Ampère’s law round the circuit reads \(H_{iron}l + (B/\mu_0)g = 0\), so

\[ B = -\frac{\mu_0 l}{g}\,H_{iron}. \]

The iron must sit on its hysteresis loop and on this straight line at once. The line is very shallow: \(\mu_0 l/g\) is of order \(10^{-5}\) T per A/m. It meets the falling branch of the loop at a slightly negative \(H\), close to \(-H_c\), where the loop drops steeply through zero. Putting \(H_{iron} \approx -H_c\) into the line, the two minus signs cancel, and the gap holds

\[ B_{rem} \approx \mu_0 H_c\,\frac{l}{g}, \]

the coercive field times the ratio of the length of iron to the gap, which is how Halbach and Schlueter state it (Chao and others, §7.2.2). This field is a fixed number of tesla, not a fraction of the excitation, so it matters most at the lowest field, which is at injection. It depends on the shape of the previous cycle (Livingston and Blewett, §8-9), on how each block of iron was handled, and even on the magnetised steel in the building’s foundations (§12-4), so it differs from magnet to magnet round the ring.

At work. The Cosmotron’s remanent field was about 25 G, 2.5 mT (Livingston and Blewett, §13-5). Its 4 MeV protons, on a 30 ft (9.14 m) radius, have \(B\rho = 0.289\) T m and need an injection field of 31.6 mT. The remanent field was 8 per cent of it. In ampere-turns it corresponds to \(H_c l = B_{rem}g/\mu_0 = 455\) A. Livingston and Blewett report that designers try to keep the variation of the remanent field round the ring below a part in a thousand at injection, and correct what remains with DC currents in extra coils (§12-4).

Ferrite. A ferrite is an oxide of iron combined with other metals, manganese and zinc in the Cosmotron’s, ground, pressed and fired like a ceramic. It is magnetic, but its conductivity is about \(10^{-7}\) of a metal’s (Livingston and Blewett, §13-7), so a changing field drives almost no eddy current in it even at megahertz. Its permeability is lower than iron’s, about 1000 at low frequency and 500 at 5 MHz for a typical ferrite of the Cosmotron’s kind, and it saturates far lower: 0.2 to 0.3 T for the ferrites of the 1950s.

Its use in an accelerator rests on the incremental permeability. Put a steady bias field \(H_b\) on the ferrite from a DC winding, and a small RF field on top of it from a second winding. The RF flux does not see \(B/H\) but the slope of the \(B\)–\(H\) curve at the bias point:

\[ \mu_{inc} = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\left.\frac{dB}{dH}\right|_{H_b}. \]

At zero bias the slope is the full permeability. As the bias drives the material towards saturation the curve flattens and \(\mu_{inc}\) falls. A coil wound on the ferrite has an inductance proportional to \(\mu_{inc}\), so a resonant circuit, \(\omega = 1/\sqrt{LC}\) with \(C\) fixed, tuned by the bias current changes its frequency as

\[ \frac{f_2}{f_1} = \sqrt{\frac{\mu_{inc,1}}{\mu_{inc,2}}}, \]

when the ferrite carries all of the coil’s flux.

At work. The Cosmotron’s master oscillator was tuned by a small ferrite toroid. Its permeability fell from about 1000 to under 10 as the DC magnetising field rose from zero to 20 ampere-turns per centimetre, 2000 A/m: a factor of 100 in inductance and 10 in frequency (Livingston and Blewett, §13-8). The two windings were arranged so that the DC winding coupled as little as possible to the RF one. The ring needed a factor of 10.6: 4 MeV protons go round its 69.6 m at 0.396 MHz, and 3 GeV protons at 4.18 MHz.

Exercise A1. The Bevatron’s gap was 1 ft (0.305 m) high, and its supply gave at most 8400 A. What is the least number of turns that could give 1.549 T in the gap?

Answer

The gap alone needs \(NI = Bg/\mu_0 = 1.549 \times 0.3048/(4\pi\times10^{-7}) = 3.76\times10^5\) ampere-turns. At 8400 A that is \(N = 44.7\), so at least 45 turns. More are needed in practice, for the iron near saturation and for the flux that leaks round the gap, as the Cosmotron’s 32 per cent excess shows.

Exercise A2. At the Cosmotron’s peak the winding of \(N = 48\) turns carried 7000 A, and the total flux, stray field included, was 88 Wb (Livingston and Blewett, §13-5). Find the flux linkage \(\Lambda = N\Phi\) and the ratio \(\Lambda/I\). A linear inductor would store \(\tfrac12\Lambda I\); the measured stored energy was \(1.2\times10^7\) J. Why less?

Answer

\(\Lambda = 48 \times 88 = 4224\) Wb, and \(\Lambda/I = 0.603\) H. A linear inductor would store \(\tfrac12 \times 4224 \times 7000 = 1.48\times10^7\) J. The energy put in is \(\int I\,d\Lambda\), the work done by the supply against the induced voltage \(d\Lambda/dt\). If \(\Lambda\) were proportional to \(I\) this would be \(\tfrac12\Lambda I\). In saturation the last of the flux is added only at the highest currents, so a plot of \(I\) against \(\Lambda\) curves upward and lies below the straight line from the origin to the peak; the area under it is smaller. The measured \(1.2\times10^7\) J is 81 per cent of the linear value.

Exercise A3. A magnet has 2 m of iron, a gap of 0.1 m and steel with \(H_c = 80\) A/m. Estimate its remanent field. If a ring of such magnets injects 10 MeV protons on a 15.24 m radius, what fraction of the injection field is that? If the remanent field differs by 10 per cent from one magnet to the next, how big a relative field error results, and how does it compare with the \(10^{-3}\) the orbit tolerates?

Answer

\(B_{rem} \approx \mu_0 H_c l/g = 4\pi\times10^{-7} \times 80 \times 2/0.1 = 2.0\) mT. The injection field is \(B\rho/\rho = 0.458/15.24 = 30.1\) mT, so the remanent field is 6.7 per cent of it. A 10 per cent spread of that is a relative field error of \(6.7\times10^{-3}\), nearly seven times the tolerance. This is why a ring is injected at as high a field as the injector allows (section 10), and why remanent fields were measured and corrected.

Exercise A4. The Bevatron’s revolution frequency rose by a factor of 6.84. By what factor must the incremental permeability of a ferrite-tuned inductor fall, at fixed capacitance? Could a toroid like the Cosmotron’s do it?

Answer

\(f \propto \mu_{inc}^{-1/2}\), so \(\mu_{inc}\) must fall by \(6.84^2 = 47\). The Cosmotron’s toroid fell by a factor of 100 between zero bias and 2000 A/m, so it has range to spare; the bias current only has to follow the field along the right curve.

2. The field follows the momentum

A particle of charge \(q\) and momentum \(p\) moving perpendicular to a uniform field \(B\) moves on a circle of radius \(\rho = p/(qB)\) (Landau and Lifshitz, volume 2, section 21). The reason, recalled from F4, section 10 and F2, section 4: the force \(q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\) is always at right angles to the velocity, so it turns the momentum without changing its size, and a momentum \(p\) turned at the angular rate \(v/\rho\) needs a force \(pv/\rho\); setting \(qvB = pv/\rho\) gives \(\rho = p/(qB)\). This holds at any speed, with \(p = \gamma m v\) (chapter 1). To keep \(\rho\) fixed as \(p\) grows, the field must grow in proportion:

\[ B(t) = \frac{p(t)}{q\rho}, \qquad B\rho = \frac{p}{q}. \]

The product \(B\rho\) (T m) is the magnetic rigidity: how hard the particle is to bend. For a singly charged particle, \(B\rho\,[\mathrm{T\,m}] = 3.3356\,p\,[\mathrm{GeV}/c]\). The number comes from the units. A momentum of 1 GeV/\(c\) (F7, section 3) is \(10^9\,e\) joules divided by \(c\), that is \(10^9 e/c\) kg m/s, and dividing by the charge \(e\) leaves \(B\rho = 10^9/c = 10^9/(2.998\times10^8) = 3.3356\) T m.

Worked numbers (Bevatron). The Bevatron’s magnets bend the protons on a radius of 600 inches, 15.24 m (Crebbin and Lothrop, 1969). The protons came in at about 10 MeV, from a 500 keV Cockcroft-Walton generator and a linear accelerator that added 9.5 MeV (Lawrence and His Laboratory, chapter 6), and left at 6.2 GeV. The momentum follows from the kinetic energy \(W\) through \(E = W + mc^2\) and \(pc = \sqrt{E^2 - (mc^2)^2}\), with \(mc^2 = 0.9383\) GeV for the proton (chapter 1). At 10 MeV, \(p = 0.1374\) GeV/\(c\), \(B\rho = 0.458\) T m and \(B = 0.0301\) T. At 6.2 GeV, \(p = 7.076\) GeV/\(c\), \(B\rho = 23.60\) T m and \(B = 1.549\) T. The field must rise by a factor of 52.

Worked numbers (an electron ring). McMillan’s 300 MeV electron synchrotron at Berkeley had a radius of 1 m (Jackson and Panofsky). At 300 MeV an electron has \(p = 300.5\) MeV/\(c\) and needs \(B = 1.002\) T on that radius.

The relation is exact for the reference particle. Two approximations hide in using it to set a real magnet. The field must be the same in every magnet to a part in a thousand or better, or the closed orbit wanders (section 8). And the field in the iron gap is not simply proportional to the current: at low field the remanent magnetism of the iron and the eddy currents of section 12 add to it, and at high field the iron saturates. A ring is therefore programmed by the measured field, not by the current (At the bench).

3. The frequency follows the speed

A particle of speed \(v = \beta c\) goes round in the revolution period \(T_0 = C/(\beta c)\) (s; the subscript keeps it apart from the temperature \(T\)), so its revolution frequency is

\[ f_0 = \frac{\beta c}{C}, \qquad \omega_0 = 2\pi f_0. \]

The gap voltage must have the same phase each time the particle crosses, so its frequency is a whole multiple of the revolution frequency:

\[ f_{RF} = h f_0 = \frac{h\beta c}{C}, \]

where \(h\) is the harmonic number. A bunch is a short cluster of particles riding together at one phase of the wave. In one turn the RF goes through \(h\) cycles, so there are \(h\) places round the ring where a particle sees the right phase, and \(h\) is also the largest number of bunches the ring can hold. Because the field and the speed are tied through the momentum, the frequency can be written as a function of the field. Divide \(p = \gamma mv\) by \(E = \gamma mc^2\): \(pc/E = v/c = \beta\). With \(E = \sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4}\) this is \(\beta = p/\sqrt{p^2 + m^2c^2}\), and putting in \(p = qB\rho\) and cancelling \(q\rho\) from top and bottom,

\[ f_{RF}(B) = \frac{hc}{C}\,\frac{B}{\sqrt{B^2 + B_m^2}}, \qquad B_m = \frac{mc}{q\rho}. \]

\(B_m\) is the field at which the particle’s momentum equals \(mc\), that is \(\beta\gamma = 1\). Below \(B_m\) the frequency rises almost in proportion to the field; above it, it creeps towards its limit \(hc/C\) (figure 15.2).

Figure 15.2. The RF frequency the Bevatron needs at each value of its field, from the formula above with \(h = 1\), \(C = 120\) m and \(B_m = 0.205\) T. The steep part lies below \(B_m\), where the protons are still far from the speed of light: a third of the sweep is done by 100 MeV, at 6 per cent of the top field. Above \(B_m\) the frequency creeps towards \(c/C = 2.50\) MHz.

Worked numbers (Bevatron). For protons \(mc/e = 3.1297\) T m, so on \(\rho = 15.24\) m, \(B_m = 0.205\) T. The ring was four arcs and four straight sections, with a circumference of about 120 m (400 ft). With \(h = 1\), the frequency at 10 MeV is 0.362 MHz and at 6.2 GeV it is 2.48 MHz, a ratio of 6.84, the ratio of the speeds. At 100 MeV the field is only 0.097 T, but the frequency is already 1.07 MHz: a third of the whole frequency sweep is used up in the first 6 per cent of the field. If the field rose at its average rate of 0.82 T/s, that would take 82 ms. In 1971 the Bevatron’s RF ran from 500 kHz to 2.51 MHz at the first harmonic (Evans and Lothrop); the higher starting frequency implies that by then the protons were injected at about 19 to 20 MeV.

Worked numbers (Dubna). The synchrophasotron’s circumference was 208 m. Protons injected at about 9 MeV have \(\beta = 0.1375\) and \(f_0 = 0.198\) MHz; at 10 GeV \(\beta = 0.9963\) and \(f_0 = 1.436\) MHz. The range 0.2 to 1.44 MHz is the one Baldin and colleagues give for the ring’s RF in 1971, in a paper on accelerating deuterons, at half the protons’ speed, on the second harmonic.

Electrons need almost no sweep. An electron is relativistic at a few MeV. In Goward and Barnes’ machine at Woolwich the electrons went from 4 to 8 MeV, and \(\beta\) changed from 0.99356 to 0.99820, a change of 0.47 per cent. In McMillan’s 1 m ring, a full circle (\(C = 2\pi\) m) with an H-frame magnet and a toroidal chamber of eight 45° sectors of fused silica (Livingston and Blewett, sections 12-4 and 12-8), \(f_0\) is 46.7 MHz at 2 MeV and 47.7 MHz at 300 MeV. So an electron synchrotron can run its cavity at a fixed frequency, once the electrons have been brought to a few MeV, and the early machines did this by starting them as betatrons. The orbit then shifts slightly outward as \(\beta\) rises; the 2 per cent change in McMillan’s ring would move the orbit by 2 per cent of the radius if nothing else changed. Proton machines must sweep, and that is the hard part (section 13).

4. The energy gain per turn and the synchronous phase

With \(\rho\) fixed, \(p = qB\rho\) gives

\[ \frac{dp}{dt} = q\rho\,\frac{dB}{dt}. \]

The rate of change of energy is \(dE/dt = v\,dp/dt\), since \(dE = v\,dp\) for a particle (Landau and Lifshitz, volume 2, section 9). To see why, take a small change of \(E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4\) (F1, section 5): \(2E\,dE = 2pc^2\,dp\), so \(dE = (pc^2/E)\,dp\), and \(pc^2/E = \gamma mvc^2/(\gamma mc^2) = v\). It is the work-energy rule of F3, section 4 in relativistic form: a force \(F = dp/dt\) acting on a particle moving at \(v\) delivers power \(Fv\). In one turn, which lasts \(C/v\), the particle must therefore gain

\[ \Delta E_{turn} = v\,\frac{dp}{dt}\cdot\frac{C}{v} = q\,C\rho\,\frac{dB}{dt}. \]

The speed has cancelled. The energy gain per turn depends only on the geometry and the rate of rise of the field, not on the particle’s energy or mass. The kinetic energy \(W\) changes by the same amount as \(E\).

The gap supplies \(\Delta W = q\hat{V}\sin\phi\), where \(\hat{V}\) is the voltage amplitude summed over all gaps in a turn and \(\phi\) the RF phase at the crossing, increasing for later arrival. The synchronous particle, the one that stays exactly on the reference orbit, must cross at the phase \(\phi_s\) that gives exactly what the field demands:

\[ \hat{V}\sin\phi_s = C\rho\,\frac{dB}{dt}. \]

A comparison with the betatron. In a ring without straight sections, \(C = 2\pi\rho\) and the requirement is \(\hat{V}\sin\phi_s = 2\pi\rho^2\,dB/dt\). A betatron of radius \(\rho\) needs the flux through its orbit to change at \(d\Phi/dt = 2\pi\rho^2\,dB/dt\) by the two-to-one rule (the average field inside the orbit must rise twice as fast as the field at it, so \(d\Phi/dt = \pi\rho^2 \cdot 2\,dB/dt\)). The volts per turn are the same, as they must be: both machines supply the same momentum to the same orbit in the same time. The synchrotron gets them from a resonant gap in a few metres of the ring, instead of from a core filling the whole circle.

Worked numbers (Bevatron). From 10 MeV to 6.2 GeV in 1.85 s on \(\rho = 15.24\) m, the average rate of rise is \(dB/dt = 0.82\) T/s, and with \(C = 120\) m the energy gain per turn is \(120 \times 15.24 \times 0.82 = 1.50\) keV. As a check, Lawrence and His Laboratory gives 4,000,000 turns in 1.85 s; \((6.2\ \text{GeV} - 10\ \text{MeV})/4\times10^6 = 1.55\) keV a turn, and the path is \(4\times10^6 \times 120\ \text{m} = 480{,}000\) km, the “300,000 miles” of that account. The Bevatron’s two-gap drift tube in its north straight section gave at most about 5 keV a turn (Crebbin and Lothrop). So \(\sin\phi_s = 1.50/5 = 0.30\). Two phases in each cycle have this sine, \(17.5^\circ\) on the rising side of the wave and \(180^\circ - 17.5^\circ = 162.5^\circ\) on the falling side. As section 7 shows, the Bevatron was above transition, and then only the falling side is stable (below, and figure 15.3), so \(\phi_s = 162.5^\circ\).

Electrons radiate. An electron must also make up the energy it radiates. The loss per turn, quoted here and derived in chapter 20 (Landau and Lifshitz, volume 2, section 74), is

\[ U_0 = \frac{e^2\beta^3\gamma^4}{3\varepsilon_0\rho}, \]

or \(U_0\,[\mathrm{keV}] = 88.46\,E^4[\mathrm{GeV}]/\rho[\mathrm{m}]\) for \(\beta \approx 1\). In McMillan’s ring at 300 MeV it is 721 eV a turn. The gap must then supply \(\hat{V}\sin\phi_s = C\rho\,\dot{B} + U_0/e\). Unlike the betatron’s induced voltage, the cavity voltage can be raised to pay it. This is the advantage Veksler named in 1944.

Phase stability, in outline. Why does the bunch stay near \(\phi_s\)? Suppose a particle has a little more energy than the synchronous one. Whether it then arrives earlier or later depends on two effects that oppose each other: it is faster, and it travels a longer path (section 6). Section 7 shows that in a weak-focusing ring the longer path wins at every energy: the particle with more energy arrives later, at a larger \(\phi\). To be stable it must then gain less, so \(\Delta W\) must fall as \(\phi\) rises past \(\phi_s\): \(\cos\phi_s < 0\), or \(\pi/2 < \phi_s < \pi\), the falling side of the sine (figure 15.3). McMillan’s own description, in the memoir by Jackson and Panofsky, says the same: for rings with fields uniform in azimuth, stability lies on the decreasing part of the RF wave. The particles oscillate about \(\phi_s\) in energy and phase. The theory of these synchrotron oscillations is in chapter 12 and in depth in chapter 19. Chapter 12, section 2, derived the small-amplitude result needed here: the phase obeys the simple-harmonic equation of F3, section 9, turn by turn, and the number of these oscillations per turn, the synchrotron tune, is

\[ Q_s = \frac{\Omega_s}{\omega_0} = \sqrt{\frac{h\,|\eta|\,q\hat{V}\,|\cos\phi_s|}{2\pi\beta^2 E}}, \]

with \(q\hat{V}\) and \(E\), the total energy, both in joules, \(\eta\) the phase-slip factor of section 7, \(\omega_0 = 2\pi f_0\) the revolution angular frequency and \(\Omega_s\) (rad/s) the angular frequency of the synchrotron oscillation; the synchrotron frequency in hertz is \(f_s = \Omega_s/2\pi = Q_sf_0\). Linear-accelerator texts write the gain as \(qE_0T_tL\cos\phi\) with a different zero of phase; the physics is the same (chapter 14).

Figure 15.3. Phase stability above transition. The energy gain per crossing is \(q\hat{V}\sin\phi\), and the rising field demands \(0.30\,q\hat{V}\) (the Bevatron’s 1.5 keV from a 5 kV gap), met at \(17.5^\circ\) and at \(162.5^\circ\). Above transition a particle with extra energy takes longer to go round. Near \(162.5^\circ\) an early particle gains more, so it comes round later next time; a late one gains less and comes round sooner. Both drift back towards \(\phi_s\) (red arrows). Near \(17.5^\circ\) the same reasoning pushes them away.

Worked numbers (Bevatron, illustrative). With \(h = 1\), \(\hat{V} = 5\) kV, \(\sin\phi_s = 0.30\), and an assumed momentum compaction \(\alpha_c = 2\) (section 6), \(Q_s = 0.0062\) at injection, a synchrotron frequency of 2.3 kHz; at 1 GeV, 2.1 kHz; at 6.2 GeV, 1.1 kHz. The Bevatron’s phase loop, which damped these oscillations, had its highest gain in the 1 to 2 kHz range (Evans and Lothrop, 1971): the same numbers seen from the control room.

The synchronous phase is not fixed through the cycle. A motor-generator supply (an alternator driven by a flywheel, section 11) drives the current along a curve, not a straight line, so \(\dot{B}\) is small at injection, largest in the middle and small again near the top. Either the voltage is programmed to follow \(\dot{B}\), or \(\phi_s\) moves through the cycle.

5. Weak focusing in a ring

A particle that starts a little off the reference orbit, or at a small angle to it, must be brought back. In the early synchrotrons this was done exactly as in the betatron (chapter 11): by letting the field fall slightly with radius. Near the orbit write

\[ B_y(x) = B_0\left(1 - n\,\frac{x}{\rho}\right), \qquad n = -\frac{\rho}{B}\frac{\partial B}{\partial r}, \]

with \(n\) the field index, a pure number: the relative fall of the field per relative step outward. (The \(\partial\) is a partial derivative, F1, section 9: the slope with \(r\) at fixed height and azimuth.) The median plane is the horizontal plane half-way between the poles, \(y = 0\). The field lines then bow outward, so off the median plane there is a radial field \(B_x = -n B_0 y/\rho\), which pushes a positive particle back towards the plane (figure 15.4). The radial field follows from the curl. The gap holds no current, so \(\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = 0\) there (F2, section 11; Ampère’s law with no current, F4, section 12). Its component along \(s\) says \(\partial B_x/\partial y = \partial B_y/\partial x\), and from the first equation \(\partial B_y/\partial x = -nB_0/\rho\). On the median plane \(B_x = 0\) by symmetry, so integrating up from \(y = 0\) gives \(B_x = -nB_0y/\rho\).

Figure 15.4. The gap of a weak-focusing magnet in cross-section, the beam coming out of the page. The poles open outward, so the field weakens with \(x\) (\(n > 0\)) and its lines bow outward. On the median plane the field is vertical and bends the particle inward, round the ring. Above the plane the lines lean inward (\(B_x < 0\)) and the force \(q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\) (F2, section 3) points down; below, they lean outward and it points up. Slope and bowing exaggerated.

The equations of motion for small \(x\) and \(y\), derived in chapter 11, section 4 and in Humphries, section 7.3, are (a prime is a derivative with respect to \(s\), so \(x' = dx/ds\) is the angle of the path to the reference orbit)

\[ \frac{d^2x}{ds^2} + \frac{1-n}{\rho^2}\,x = 0, \qquad \frac{d^2y}{ds^2} + \frac{n}{\rho^2}\,y = 0. \]

The horizontal restoring term has two parts: \(1/\rho^2\), from the geometry of circles (an orbit displaced outward in a uniform field is another circle that crosses the reference twice a turn, figure 15.5), and \(-n/\rho^2\), from the weaker field outside. Both motions are stable only if \(0 < n < 1\). Each equation has the form \(u'' + Ku = 0\) of simple harmonic motion (F3, section 9), with \(s\) in place of time: for \(K > 0\) the solution is \(u = A\cos(\sqrt{K}s + \varphi)\), which repeats when \(\sqrt{K}s\) grows by \(2\pi\); for \(K < 0\) it grows exponentially. The solutions are oscillations, the betatron oscillations, with wavelengths \(2\pi\rho/\sqrt{1-n}\) and \(2\pi\rho/\sqrt{n}\). Their number per turn of length \(2\pi\rho\), the tunes, are

\[ Q_x = \sqrt{1-n}, \qquad Q_y = \sqrt{n}, \qquad Q_x^2 + Q_y^2 = 1. \]

Both tunes are below 1: a weakly focused particle makes less than one oscillation per turn. That is what “weak” means. The wavelength of the oscillation is longer than the ring.

Figure 15.5. Why the horizontal tune is 1 in a uniform field (\(n = 0\)). A particle of the right momentum started a distance \(a\) outside the reference orbit moves on a circle of the same radius \(\rho\), centred at \(O'\), a distance \(a\) from \(O\). Its displacement is \(x \approx a\cos\theta\): outside on one side, inside on the other, crossing the reference twice. One full oscillation per turn. A field that weakens outward (\(n > 0\)) bends the outer part less, which lengthens the oscillation, and \(Q_x = \sqrt{1-n}\) falls below 1. Shift exaggerated.

Straight sections. A racetrack ring has drifts between the arcs, where the particle runs straight and is not focused. The equations above hold in the arcs; across the straights \(x\) and \(x'\) change as in empty space. Lengthening the straights raises the tunes and makes the betatron amplitude vary around the ring. The exact values come from multiplying the transfer matrices of the arcs and drifts (taught in chapter 8: each element is a 2 by 2 matrix that takes \((x, x')\) at its entrance to \((x, x')\) at its exit), the method of chapter 16; for \(n = 0.6\), \(\rho = 15.24\) m and four straights of 6.1 m (the Bevatron’s proportions) it gives \(Q_x \approx 0.71\) and \(Q_y \approx 0.87\), against 0.63 and 0.77 without straights. Both the Cosmotron and the Bevatron were built with \(n = 0.6\) at the injection field, the pole faces sloped to give it; with straight sections the stable range narrows from 0.5 to 1.0 to about 0.5 to 0.8 (Livingston and Blewett, section 13-3, page 448). For the Cosmotron, with 9.15 m arcs and four 10 ft (3 m) straights, the same authors give the radial oscillation frequency as 0.70 of the revolution frequency (section 13-6), close to this model. For the Bevatron, Crebbin and Lothrop drove the radial betatron oscillation at about 0.6 of the revolution frequency, below the model’s 0.71; the simple model leaves out the fringe fields and the change of \(n\) with field, and the difference is not resolved here.

Worked numbers. With \(n = 0.6\): \(Q_x = 0.632\), \(Q_y = 0.775\). On \(\rho = 15.24\) m the horizontal wavelength is \(2\pi\rho/Q_x = 151\) m, longer than the Bevatron’s 120 m circumference.

6. Off-momentum orbits and momentum compaction

A particle whose momentum differs from the reference by \(\delta = \Delta p/p\) has its own closed orbit, displaced to where the field bends it on a circle. Take the field \(B_y(r) = B_0(\rho/r)^n\), the one whose index is \(n\) at every radius (its logarithm is \(\ln B_y = \text{const} - n\ln r\), so \(-(r/B_y)\,dB_y/dr = n\) exactly; near \(r = \rho\) it is the linear field of section 5). In it a particle of momentum \(p(1+\delta)\) circles at the radius \(r\) where \(p(1+\delta) = qB_y(r)\,r\):

\[ 1 + \delta = \frac{B_0\rho^n r^{1-n}}{B_0\rho} = \left(\frac{r}{\rho}\right)^{1-n}. \]

For small \(\delta\), write \(r = \rho + x\) and use the first term of the Taylor series (F1, section 8), \((1 + x/\rho)^{1-n} \approx 1 + (1-n)\,x/\rho\). Then \(\delta = (1-n)\,x/\rho\), and

\[ x_{co} = \frac{\rho\,\delta}{1-n} = D_x\,\delta, \qquad D_x = \frac{\rho}{1-n}. \]

\(D_x\) (m) is the dispersion, the displacement of the closed orbit per unit relative momentum deviation. With \(n = 0.6\) on 15.24 m, \(D_x = 38\) m: a proton 1 per cent high in momentum circles 38 cm outside the reference. This is why weak-focusing rings needed wide gaps.

The longer orbit has a longer circumference. In a ring without straights, \(C = 2\pi r\), so

\[ \frac{\Delta C}{C} = \frac{x_{co}}{\rho} = \frac{\delta}{1-n}. \]

The momentum compaction \(\alpha_c\) is defined as the relative change in path length per relative change in momentum:

\[ \alpha_c = \frac{\Delta C/C}{\delta} = \frac{1}{1-n} = \frac{1}{Q_x^2}. \]

For a weak-focusing ring \(\alpha_c > 1\): the path length grows faster than the momentum. In a uniform field (\(n = 0\)) \(\alpha_c = 1\), the cyclotron’s case, where the orbit radius is proportional to the momentum.

With straight sections. The path length changes only where the orbit bends. On an arc of radius \(\rho\) a short step \(ds\) of the reference subtends the angle \(ds/\rho\), and an orbit a distance \(x\) further out covers that angle on the radius \(\rho + x\), a path \((1 + x/\rho)\,ds\), longer by \(x\,ds/\rho\). On a straight, parallel paths have the same length. Adding up round the ring with \(x = D_x\delta\), \(\Delta C = \delta\oint D_x/\rho\,ds\), so in general \(\alpha_c = (1/C)\oint D_x/\rho\,ds\) (the integral taken over the bends only, where \(1/\rho \neq 0\)). If \(D_x\) is about \(\rho/(1-n)\) in the arcs, the arcs make up a fraction \(2\pi\rho/C = \rho/R\) of the circumference and

\[ \alpha_c \approx \frac{\rho/R}{1-n}. \]

For the Bevatron, \(\rho/R = 15.24/19.1 = 0.80\), and with \(n = 0.6\), \(\alpha_c \approx 2.0\). This is the value used for illustration in section 4.

7. The phase-slip factor and transition

The time for one turn is \(T_0 = C/v\). Take logarithms, \(\ln T_0 = \ln C - \ln v\), and a small change of each: since \(d(\ln f) = df/f\) (the logarithmic derivative of F1, section 5), relative changes add and subtract as the logarithms do. For a small momentum deviation,

\[ \frac{\Delta T_0}{T_0} = \frac{\Delta C}{C} - \frac{\Delta v}{v}. \]

The first term is \(\alpha_c\delta\). For the second, \(p = m c\,\beta\gamma\) with \(\beta\gamma = \beta(1-\beta^2)^{-1/2}\). By the product and chain rules, \(d(\beta\gamma)/d\beta = (1-\beta^2)^{-1/2} + \beta^2(1-\beta^2)^{-3/2} = (1-\beta^2)^{-3/2} = \gamma^3\), so \(d(\beta\gamma) = \gamma^3 d\beta\). Dividing by \(\beta\gamma\), \(dp/p = \gamma^2\,d\beta/\beta\) and \(\Delta v/v = \delta/\gamma^2\). Then

\[ \frac{\Delta T_0}{T_0} = \eta\,\delta, \qquad \eta = \alpha_c - \frac{1}{\gamma^2}. \]

The phase-slip factor \(\eta\) says whether a particle with extra momentum comes round sooner (\(\eta < 0\)) or later (\(\eta > 0\)). At low energy the speed term dominates and, in a ring with \(\alpha_c < 1\), \(\eta\) is negative: the ring is below transition. At high energy the speed hardly changes and the path term wins: \(\eta > 0\), above transition. The two balance at the transition gamma

\[ \gamma_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_c}}. \]

Chapter 12 found the same factor for a cyclotron’s field (section 1); here it holds for any ring. At transition the phase focusing of section 4 vanishes and changes sign, so the stable phase must be switched from \(\phi_s\) to \(\pi - \phi_s\) as the beam passes through (chapter 19).

Weak focusing never crosses transition. With \(\alpha_c = 1/(1-n)\),

\[ \gamma_t = \sqrt{1-n} = Q_x < 1. \]

Since every particle has \(\gamma \geq 1\), a weak-focusing ring is above transition from the moment of injection, and \(\eta = \alpha_c - 1/\gamma^2 > 0\) throughout. Humphries (section 15.6) derives the same result: stable betatron motion limits \(\gamma_t\) to between 0 and 1. With \(n = 0.6\), \(\alpha_c = 2.5\) and \(\gamma_t = 0.632\); at 10 MeV injection \(\eta = 1.52\) and at 6.2 GeV \(\eta = 2.48\). The stable phase is on the falling side of the wave from start to finish, which is why the Bevatron’s \(\phi_s\) in section 4 was 162.5°, not 17.5°.

This is a quiet virtue of weak focusing. Strong-focusing rings (chapter 16) have \(Q_x\) of several or many, so \(\alpha_c \approx 1/Q_x^2\) is small and \(\gamma_t \approx Q_x\) lies inside the energy range. Their protons must cross transition, as the CERN Proton Synchrotron’s first beams showed in 1959 (Where it stops).

Pre-requisite (continued): Laplace transform, feedback and stability

Pre-requisite. Chapter 6 taught the language of a regulating loop: the Laplace transform, the transfer function, the loop gain that divides slow errors, and the stability margin that a delay eats into. This section applies it to a ring’s RF in two ways. First, an open-loop programme corrected by a closed loop that measures the beam’s radius. Second, a loop that does not hold a quantity at a set point but damps an oscillation, and so needs the right gain and phase over the band of frequencies where the oscillation lives. The next section gives the tolerance that makes the first necessary; section 4 met the synchrotron oscillation that the second damps. Taught from Chao and others, Handbook, §7.3.1.1 (the differentiator, the phase-lock loop with radial correction, and the beam’s response to the RF); Lee, chapter 3, §II.6 (the phase detector and phase feedback) and §IV.3 (the radial loop); Livingston and Blewett, §13-8 (frequency control in the Cosmotron); Landau and Lifshitz, volume 1, §25 (the damped oscillator).

In this section \(s\) is the Laplace variable of chapter 6, not the distance along the orbit.

Programme plus correction. The RF frequency is first set by a programme: a function of the measured magnetic field, made by a circuit that knows the formula of section 3. Call its error \(\epsilon = \Delta f_{prog}/f\). Section 8 shows that a frequency error moves the beam radially by

\[ \Delta x = -k\,\frac{\Delta f}{f}, \qquad k = \frac{D_x}{\eta}, \]

with \(k\) about 25 m at the Bevatron’s injection and 15 m at the top. Now add a pick-up that measures \(\Delta x\) and a loop that adds a correction \(\Delta f_{corr}/f = G\,\Delta x\), with \(G\) (1/m) the loop’s gain. For slow changes,

\[ \Delta x = -k\left(\epsilon + G\,\Delta x\right) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta x = -\frac{k\,\epsilon}{1 + kG}. \]

The loop gain is \(G_0 = kG\), dimensionless (chapter 6 called it \(L_0\); here \(L\) is an inductance). As in chapter 6 the closed loop divides the open-loop error by \(1 + G_0\). The programme only has to be good enough to bring the beam into the range of the pick-up; the loop does the rest.

At work. A programme error of \(10^{-3}\) at the Bevatron’s injection would put the beam 25 mm off. With \(G_0 = 100\) it is held to 0.25 mm. The gain this needs is \(G = 100/25 = 4\) per metre: each millimetre of error moves the frequency by 0.4 per cent. Livingston and Blewett (§13-8) put the Cosmotron’s tolerance at about 0.2 per cent in frequency, about the error that would move the orbit by an inch at injection. The Cosmotron met it with a programme alone: a network of 28 biased diode circuits shaped the ferrite’s bias current to follow the calculated frequency curve within 0.1 per cent over the whole 1 s rise, while the field rose more than fortyfold (§13-8). A tolerance of a few millimetres, ten times tighter, is what needs the loop.

The beam is itself an oscillator. The loop cannot be made as fast as one likes. A step in the RF frequency does not move the beam at once. The bunch must slip in phase, gain or lose energy, and move to its new orbit, and it overshoots and oscillates about the new equilibrium at the synchrotron frequency \(\Omega_s\). In transforms, the bunch’s response to the RF is

\[ R_b(s) = \frac{\Omega_s^2}{s^2 + \Omega_s^2}, \]

which has no damping at all (Chao and others, §7.3.1.1). A radial loop that tried to act faster than \(\Omega_s\) would be driving an undamped resonance. So the radial loop is kept slow, below the synchrotron frequency, and a second loop damps the oscillation itself.

Damping an oscillation with a loop. Let \(\xi(t)\) be the oscillating quantity, here the bunch’s phase error \(\phi - \phi_s\). Without feedback,

\[ \ddot{\xi} + \Omega_s^2\,\xi = 0. \]

A detector measures \(\xi\), a filter with transfer function \(K(s)\) (in s\(^{-2}\), so that the product is an acceleration of \(\xi\)) shapes it, and the result is applied back as a shift of the RF phase that pushes on \(\xi\). In transforms, with no initial values,

\[ s^2\Xi + \Omega_s^2\,\Xi = -K(s)\,\Xi \quad\Rightarrow\quad s^2 + \Omega_s^2 + K(s) = 0. \]

The roots \(s\) of this equation give the motion \(e^{st}\). Without feedback they are \(s = \pm i\Omega_s\). With a weak loop, look for the root near \(+i\Omega_s\): write \(s = i\Omega_s + \lambda\) with \(\lambda\) small. Then \(s^2 = -\Omega_s^2 + 2i\Omega_s\lambda + \lambda^2\); the \(-\Omega_s^2\) cancels the \(+\Omega_s^2\), and \(\lambda^2\), the product of two small quantities, is dropped. Evaluate \(K\) at \(i\Omega_s\), writing the complex number (F5, section 1) \(K(i\Omega_s) = K_r + iK_i\):

\[ 2i\Omega_s\lambda + K_r + iK_i = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \lambda = -\frac{K_i}{2\Omega_s} + i\,\frac{K_r}{2\Omega_s}. \]

Since \(e^{st} = e^{i\Omega_st}e^{\lambda t}\), the real part of \(\lambda\) sets growth or decay and its imaginary part shifts the frequency. This is the rule of the whole subject. The part of the feedback in phase with \(\xi\), \(K_r\), changes the frequency and does nothing to the amplitude. Only the part a quarter period ahead of \(\xi\), \(K_i\), acts like friction: the amplitude decays as \(e^{-at}\) with

\[ a = \frac{K_i}{2\Omega_s}. \]

The simplest such filter is a differentiator, \(K(s) = 2as\), which feeds back \(2a\dot{\xi}\) and turns the equation into the damped oscillator \(\ddot{\xi} + 2a\dot{\xi} + \Omega_s^2\xi = 0\) of Landau and Lifshitz, §25. Physically, \(\dot{\xi}\) is proportional to the bunch’s energy error, so the loop takes energy away from a bunch that has too much and gives it to one that has too little. If \(K_i\) is negative, the loop pumps the oscillation up instead.

Why the band matters. A real filter has delays and corners. A corner frequency is where a filter’s response bends: below it a stage passes the signal unchanged, above it the stage cuts the amplitude and adds a lag growing to \(90^\circ\); a stage with corner \(f_1\) multiplies the response by \(1/(1 + if/f_1)\), a lag of \(\arctan(f/f_1)\). A delay \(t_d\) multiplies \(K\) by \(e^{-st_d}\), which at \(s = i\Omega_s\) rotates it by the angle \(\Omega_s t_d\). The differentiator’s damping becomes \(a\cos(\Omega_s t_d)\), which vanishes when \(\Omega_s t_d = \pi/2\), that is, when the delay reaches a quarter of the synchrotron period, and turns to anti-damping beyond. The differentiator must also not respond to noise at the revolution frequency and its harmonics. The design rule in Chao and others (§7.3.1.1) is therefore a differentiator whose corners sit well above \(\Omega_s\) and well below the revolution frequency \(\omega_0\). In a proton ring \(\Omega_s\) also moves through the cycle: the Bevatron’s synchrotron frequency in section 4 falls from about 2.3 kHz at injection to 1.1 kHz at the top. The loop must keep both its gain and its quarter-period lead over that whole band. That is the meaning of the Bevatron’s phase loop having its highest gain in the 1 to 2 kHz range.

At work (an illustrative design). A differentiator with corners at 20 kHz and 50 kHz, both far below the Bevatron’s revolution frequency of 0.36 to 2.5 MHz, keeps a phase lead of \(85.6^\circ\) at 1.1 kHz and \(80.8^\circ\) at 2.3 kHz instead of \(90^\circ\). Since only \(K_i\) damps, the lost lead reduces the damping by \(\cos(90^\circ - \text{lead})\), to 99.7 and 98.7 per cent of the ideal; the corners also lower the gain slightly, by \(1/\sqrt{(1 + (f/f_1)^2)(1 + (f/f_2)^2)}\), and together the damping is 99.5 and 98.0 per cent of the ideal. The corners cost almost nothing. A delay of a hundred microseconds would leave \(\cos(\Omega_st_d)\) of it: 77 per cent at 1.1 kHz but only 13 per cent at 2.3 kHz.

Answer

Open loop, \(\Delta x = 15.3 \times 2\times10^{-3} = 30.6\) mm. Closed, \(30.6/(1 + G_0) \leq 1\) mm needs \(G_0 \geq 29.6\), so a loop gain of about 30.

Exercise B2. A synchrotron oscillation at 2 kHz is to be damped with an amplitude time constant of 1 ms by a differentiator. What coefficient \(2a\) is needed, and what loop delay would cancel the damping altogether?

Answer

\(a = 1/(1\ \text{ms}) = 1000\) s\(^{-1}\), so \(K(s) = 2as\) with \(2a = 2000\) s\(^{-1}\); then \(K_i = 2a\Omega_s = 2.5\times10^7\) s\(^{-2}\) at \(\Omega_s = 2\pi \times 2000 = 1.26\times10^4\) rad/s. The damping \(a\cos(\Omega_s t_d)\) vanishes at \(t_d = 1/(4f_s) = 125\) μs. A numerical solution of \(s^2 + \Omega_s^2 + 2as\,e^{-st_d} = 0\) agrees: the decay rate is 1000 s\(^{-1}\) with no delay, 750 s\(^{-1}\) at 62.5 μs, and the root crosses to growth near 116 μs, a little before 125 μs, because with \(a/\Omega_s = 0.08\) the first-order rule is only approximate.

Exercise B3. A loop feeds back \(K = 0.2\,\Omega_s^2\), a real constant, with no phase shift. What does it do to the oscillation?

Answer

\(K_i = 0\), so no damping. The equation becomes \(\ddot{\xi} + 1.2\,\Omega_s^2\xi = 0\), an oscillation at \(\sqrt{1.2} = 1.095\) times the old frequency; the first-order rule gives \(1 + K_r/(2\Omega_s^2) = 1.1\). A loop that feeds back the phase error without a lead only stiffens the oscillation.

8. How closely the frequency must follow the field

Suppose the field is right but the RF frequency is off by a fraction \(\Delta f/f\). The bunch must follow the RF, so its revolution frequency changes by the same fraction. A particle can change its revolution frequency only by changing its momentum: from section 7, \(\Delta f_0/f_0 = -\Delta T_0/T_0 = -\eta\,\delta\) (a frequency is one over a period, so their relative changes are equal and opposite). The beam’s momentum, and with it the beam’s radius, therefore shift:

\[ \delta = -\frac{1}{\eta}\frac{\Delta f}{f}, \qquad \Delta x = D_x\,\delta = -\frac{D_x}{\eta}\,\frac{\Delta f}{f}. \]

In a weak-focusing ring both \(D_x\) and \(\eta\) are positive, so raising the frequency moves the beam inward, onto a shorter path. This gives the operator a way to steer the beam radially, and the designer a tolerance.

Worked numbers (Bevatron-like, \(n = 0.6\)). \(D_x = 38.1\) m. At 10 MeV, \(\eta = 1.52\), so \(\Delta x = -25.0\ \mathrm{m} \times \Delta f/f\): a 5 mm shift for \(\Delta f/f = 2.0\times10^{-4}\). At 6.2 GeV, \(\Delta x = -15.3\ \mathrm{m}\times\Delta f/f\), and 5 mm needs \(3.3\times10^{-4}\). The frequency must follow a field that rises by a factor of 52 to a few parts in \(10^4\) at every moment. No open-loop programme of the 1950s could do that unaided. The answer, in every proton synchrotron, was feedback: pick-up electrodes measured the beam’s radial position, and an error signal corrected the frequency (At the bench).

The field has a similar tolerance. If the field in every magnet were too high by \(\Delta B/B\) at fixed momentum, the particles would behave as if their momentum were low by that fraction, and would move inward by \(D_x\,\Delta B/B\): 38 mm for \(10^{-3}\) in the example. An error in one magnet only also kicks the orbit, and since \(Q_x < 1\) that kick makes a distortion of the closed orbit around the whole ring of a size set by the radius (chapter 18).

9. The wall of the weak-focusing ring

Why did weak-focusing rings get so heavy? Every displacement in sections 5 to 8 scales with the radius. The horizontal betatron oscillation has a wavelength of \(2\pi\rho/Q_x\), more than the circumference. An injection error of angle \(\theta\) grows into an oscillation of amplitude about \(\rho\,\theta/Q_x\): the motion is \(x = A\sin(Q_xs/\rho)\), whose slope at the start is \(x' = AQ_x/\rho\), and setting that equal to \(\theta\) gives \(A = \rho\theta/Q_x\). The dispersion is \(\rho/(1-n)\). A field error makes an orbit distortion proportional to \(\rho\). With a fixed technology (fields of about 1.5 T, field errors of about \(10^{-3}\), injection spreads of a fraction of a per cent), the aperture needed grows in proportion to the radius.

The radius in turn grows in proportion to the momentum at a fixed peak field. So the gap’s width and height each grow as \(p\), its cross-section as \(p^2\), and the ring’s length as \(p\). The volume of field, and the iron and copper around it, grow as

\[ M \propto \rho \times (\text{aperture})^2 \propto p^3. \]

Worked numbers. The Cosmotron (3.3 GeV, \(p = 4.13\) GeV/\(c\)) had 288 magnet blocks of about 5.7 tons each, about 1,640 tons (Livingston and Blewett, section 13-4). Scaling as \(p^3\) predicts 8,200 tons for the Bevatron (\(p = 7.08\) GeV/\(c\)), which had about 10,000 tons, and 30,100 tons for the Dubna synchrophasotron (\(p = 10.90\) GeV/\(c\)), which had 36,000 tonnes (figure 15.7). The exponents fitted between pairs of these machines are 3.4 (Cosmotron to Bevatron), 3.0 (Bevatron to Dubna) and 3.2 (Cosmotron to Dubna): the later machines were somewhat heavier than \(p^3\) scaling from the Cosmotron predicts, consistent with their being designed more conservatively.

The aperture itself was the gamble. Brookhaven’s Cosmotron team chose pole faces 36 in (0.91 m) wide with a 9 in (0.23 m) gap, for a vacuum chamber 30 in by 6 in inside (Livingston and Blewett, section 13-3; Wilson gives about 1.2 m by 0.22 m). Berkeley began with a far larger one, as the next section tells. Ten gigaelectronvolts at 36,000 tonnes was close to what a laboratory would build. A 30 GeV weak-focusing ring would by the same scaling need about 23 times the Dubna mass, which nobody would build.

10. Injection and the shrinking beam

The ring cannot accept particles at rest. At injection the field must be large enough to be well defined: above the remanent field of the iron and the distortions of eddy currents, both of which are fixed in absolute terms and so are worst, relatively, at the lowest field. A higher injection energy raises the field at injection, and it also reduces the space-charge forces (the mutual electric repulsion of the beam’s own particles) in an intense beam (chapter 22). So proton synchrotrons are fed by an injector: the Bevatron by a Cockcroft-Walton generator and a linear accelerator at about 10 MeV, the Dubna synchrophasotron by a linear accelerator of about 9 MeV, Nimrod by a 600 keV pre-injector and a 15 MeV linear accelerator (Gray, 1967), the Cosmotron by an electrostatic generator. The Bevatron’s quarter-scale model was fed by a small cyclotron with 1 MeV protons, brought in by magnetic and electrostatic deflectors (Lawrence’s Nobel lecture). The early electron synchrotrons started their electrons as betatrons, with a small flux bar, and switched to the cavity at a few MeV.

The injected pulse is long compared with a turn. While the field is still rising slowly, the injector fills the ring over many turns, each new turn entering beside the last; the beam then fills the horizontal aperture, and only particles that land inside the stable region in phase and energy when the RF is switched on are captured. The rest are lost in the first milliseconds. The techniques of injection and extraction are in chapter 21.

The beam is largest at injection. As the momentum rises, the betatron amplitude shrinks as \(p^{-1/2}\), the adiabatic damping derived for the betatron in chapter 11, section 6 (Landau and Lifshitz, volume 1, section 49): the oscillation’s action, its energy divided by its frequency, stays fixed while the guide field changes slowly, and with it the area the beam occupies in the plane of \(x\) against \(x'\); as \(p\) rises the restoring force stiffens and the amplitude falls. Here \(\sqrt{7.076/0.1374} = 7.18\). From 10 MeV to 6.2 GeV in the Bevatron the amplitude falls by a factor of 7.2, to 0.139 of its value at injection. So the aperture is set by the injected beam, and the sections of the cycle near injection are where beam is lost.

Pre-requisite (continued): Diodes, rectifiers and capacitor networks

Pre-requisite. Chapter 5 taught the diode, a valve that passes current one way only, and the rectifier that turns AC into DC for a capacitor. This section turns to the other kind of load, a large inductance such as a ring magnet. It shows how a polyphase rectifier turns an alternator’s AC into DC for the magnet, how a controlled valve, fired at a chosen moment, sets the average voltage, and how the same valves, fired late, make the voltage negative while the current keeps flowing, so that the magnet’s energy goes back to the alternator. The next section uses all of this for the motor-generator sets and ignitrons of the Bevatron. Taught from Livingston and Blewett, §12-5 (magnet excitation and ignitrons) and §13-5 (the Cosmotron’s pulse power supply); Chao and others, Handbook, §6.11 (power converters for magnets: the four quadrants, thyristor rectifiers, 12-pulse circuits and the return of energy to the mains). The books state the controlled rectifier’s output without deriving it; the derivation here is the standard one.

The load. A ring magnet is an inductance \(L\) (H) with the resistance \(R\) (\(\Omega\)) of its copper. The voltage across it is

\[ V = L\frac{dI}{dt} + RI. \]

Its time constant \(L/R\) is seconds long. The Cosmotron’s 48-turn coil had \(R = 0.114\ \Omega\) (Livingston and Blewett, §13-5, give \(R = 4.95\times10^{-5}N^2\) ohms), so with \(L\) between 0.25 and 0.80 H, \(L/R\) is 2 to 7 s. Against an alternator at 60 Hz, whose period is 17 ms, the current is a smooth quantity: it cannot follow the wiggles of the voltage, only its average. The voltage applied over a cycle of the alternator sets the change of current, \(\Delta I = (\bar{V} - RI)\,\Delta t/L\). So what matters is the average output voltage of the rectifier, and the current, held up by the inductance, flows steadily in one direction.

The polyphase rectifier. An alternator with \(m\) phases gives \(m\) sinusoidal voltages of peak \(\hat{V}\) (V), each shifted by \(2\pi/m\) from the next. Connect each through a valve to the magnet. At any moment the valve on the most positive phase conducts and the others are reverse biased, as for the diode of chapter 5. Each phase takes its turn for a fraction \(1/m\) of a cycle, centred on its crest. Measuring the angle \(\theta = \omega t\) from that crest, the output is \(\hat{V}\cos\theta\) for \(-\pi/m < \theta < \pi/m\), and the pattern repeats \(m\) times a cycle. Its average is

\[ V_{d0} = \frac{m}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi/m}^{\pi/m}\hat{V}\cos\theta\,d\theta = \frac{m}{\pi}\sin\frac{\pi}{m}\,\hat{V}. \]

For \(m = 2\), full-wave rectification of one phase, \(V_{d0} = 2\hat{V}/\pi = 0.637\hat{V}\). For \(m = 6\), \(0.955\hat{V}\); for \(m = 12\), \(0.989\hat{V}\). (For a bridge, \(\hat{V}\) is the peak of the voltage between the lines selected, not of one phase.) More phases give a flatter output: the voltage never falls far from the crest before the next phase takes over.

The controlled valve. Now replace each diode by a valve that does not conduct when its anode goes positive, but only when it is fired by a pulse; once on, it stays on until its current tries to reverse. The mercury-arc ignitron of the 1950s was such a valve: a pool of mercury as cathode, in which a pulse on an igniter electrode strikes an arc. Its modern successor is the thyristor. Fire each valve a delay angle \(\alpha_d\) after the moment at which a diode would have taken over, and take the handover from one valve to the next as instantaneous. The previous valve keeps conducting until then, because its current, held up by the magnet’s inductance, has nowhere else to go. Each phase now supplies the output from \(-\pi/m + \alpha_d\) to \(\pi/m + \alpha_d\), and the average is

\[ \bar{V} = \frac{m}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi/m+\alpha_d}^{\pi/m+\alpha_d}\hat{V}\cos\theta\,d\theta = \frac{m\hat{V}}{2\pi}\left[\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{m}+\alpha_d\right) - \sin\!\left(\alpha_d - \frac{\pi}{m}\right)\right] = V_{d0}\cos\alpha_d, \]

using \(\sin(A+B) - \sin(B-A) = 2\cos B\sin A\). The firing angle is a dial: \(\alpha_d = 0\) gives the full \(V_{d0}\), \(\alpha_d = 90^\circ\) gives zero on average.

Inversion. Past \(90^\circ\) the average voltage is negative. The current cannot reverse, because the valves pass it one way only, and it does not need to: the magnet’s inductance keeps it flowing in the same direction while the negative voltage brings it down. The power delivered to the magnet, \(\bar{V}I\), is now negative. Energy flows out of the magnet’s field, through the valves, into the alternator’s windings, against their voltage, which turns the alternator into a motor. In the language of Chao and others (§6.11), the converter works in two quadrants: current of one sign, voltage of either. The firing angle cannot be pushed all the way to \(180^\circ\): each valve must hand its current to the next while the next one’s voltage is still the higher, which takes time, so a margin is kept short of \(180^\circ\).

Figure 15.8. The output of a controlled rectifier with \(m = 6\) phases (the Cosmotron’s had 12; six show the shape more clearly). Grey: the six phase voltages \(\hat{V}\cos(\omega t - 60^\circ k)\). Red: the voltage on the magnet, each valve conducting for \(60^\circ\) from its firing. Dashed: the average \(V_{d0}\cos\alpha_d\) with \(V_{d0} = 0.955\hat{V}\). At \(\alpha_d = 0\) the output rides the crests; at \(60^\circ\) each valve is fired later and the average halves; at \(120^\circ\) the output is negative throughout and the magnet returns energy, while its current keeps flowing the same way.

This is the motor-generator cycle of the large weak-focusing rings (Livingston and Blewett, §13-5). During the rise the valves rectify and the alternator, driven by its flywheel, slows as it gives up energy. At the top the firing is shifted past \(90^\circ\); the valves invert, the magnet drives the alternator as a motor, and the flywheel speeds up again. Only the losses come from the mains.

At work: the Cosmotron’s cycle. Its alternator was a 12-phase, 60 Hz machine feeding 24 ignitrons. The voltage at the coil started at 5400 V and fell to 3700 V as the flywheel slowed over the 1 s rise; the current reached 7000 A. The ignitrons then reversed it to \(-2800\) V, rising in size to about \(-4300\) V, and the current returned to zero in 1.15 s. The cycle repeated every 5 s (Livingston and Blewett, §13-5). At the top the power into the magnet was \(3700 \times 7000 = 25.9\) MW, the “26,000 kVA” of the design. Reversing the voltage, not the current, was the whole trick.

Exercise C1. The Cosmotron’s current rose roughly linearly to 7000 A in 1 s and fell linearly to zero in 1.15 s, every 5 s, through \(R = 0.114\ \Omega\). Estimate the average power lost in the copper. Livingston and Blewett give about 800 kW.

Answer

For a current rising linearly from 0 to \(I\) in a time \(\tau\), \(\int I(t)^2\,dt = I^2\tau/3\). The rise and fall together give \(\int I^2\,dt = 7000^2 \times 2.15/3 = 3.51\times10^7\) A\(^2\) s, so the energy lost is \(0.114 \times 3.51\times10^7 = 4.0\) MJ a cycle, or 801 kW averaged over 5 s. The agreement with the book’s 800 kW shows that the copper, not the magnet’s stored energy, is what the mains pays for.

Exercise C2. To hold the Cosmotron’s field flat at 7000 A, the supply must give only the voltage across the copper. If the rectifier’s full output at that moment is \(V_{d0} = 4200\) V, at what firing angle must the valves run?

On the flat top \(dI/dt = 0\), so \(V = RI = 0.114 \times 7000 = 798\) V. Then \(\cos\alpha_d = 798/4200 = 0.190\) and \(\alpha_d = 79^\circ\). Close to \(90^\circ\): a flat top needs only a little of the alternator’s voltage.

Exercise C3. The Bevatron’s magnet had \(L \approx 2.2\) H and its supply gave up to 15 kV at 8400 A. Ignoring resistance, how long does the current take to rise to 8400 A at full voltage, and how long to fall to zero again at \(-15\) kV? How much energy goes in and comes back?

Answer

\(dI/dt = V/L = 15000/2.2 = 6.8\) kA/s, so 8400 A takes 1.23 s, and the fall at \(-15\) kV takes the same. The energy is \(\tfrac12 LI^2 = \tfrac12 \times 2.2 \times 8400^2 = 78\) MJ, the figure of section 11. The peak power is \(15\ \text{kV} \times 8400\ \text{A} = 126\) MW, first out of the flywheels and then back into them.

Exercise C4. The output of an \(m\)-pulse rectifier is not flat: its largest ripple component is at \(m\) times the alternator frequency, with amplitude \(2V_{d0}/(m^2 - 1)\) at \(\alpha_d = 0\) (a Fourier coefficient of the repeated cosine arch, which can be checked numerically). 11. The magnet cycle, its energy and its power

The ring magnet is a large inductance. Its stored energy at peak field is \(\frac{1}{2}LI^2 = \int B^2/(2\mu_0)\,dV\), most of it in the gap.

Worked numbers (Bevatron). At 1.549 T the energy density in the gap is \(B^2/(2\mu_0) = 0.954\) MJ/m\(^3\). A gap 1 ft by 4 ft (0.372 m\(^2\)) along 95.8 m of arc holds 35.6 m\(^3\), so the gap alone stores 34 MJ. Evans, Frias and Hordos (1971) give the supply’s maximum as 15,000 V at 8,400 A, with the stored energy in the magnet peaking at 78 MJ; fringe fields and the field in and around the coils account for the difference. The implied inductance is \(2\times78\ \mathrm{MJ}/(8400\ \mathrm{A})^2 = 2.2\) H. At full voltage and current the supply’s output is 126 MW, and the energy goes in over about a second.

Taking 78 MJ from the public supply in a second, and giving it back in the next second, would swing the grid by about 100 MW every few seconds. The solution in every large weak-focusing ring was a motor-generator set with a flywheel. An induction motor of modest power keeps a heavy flywheel turning. On the rising field the flywheel’s kinetic energy drives an alternator, whose output is rectified into the magnet. At the top the rectifiers are switched to invert: the magnet’s energy drives the alternator as a motor and speeds the flywheel up again. Only the losses come from the mains. The Bevatron had two such sets, with flywheels of 65 to 70 tons (the laboratory’s history gives 65; the engineering papers of 1969 and 1971 give 70), driven by two 3,600 horsepower induction motors that limited the average loss to about 6 MW (Evans, Frias and Hordos). Holding the field flat at the top cost about 13 MW in the copper and fixed losses (Salsig and Vogel, 1969). Nimrod’s two 60 MVA motor-alternator-flywheel sets fed mercury-arc converters (Brooks and Reed, 1967), and its load rose from zero to 100,000 horsepower in 0.75 s (Wilson).

The rectifiers were mercury-arc valves. The ignitron, a mercury-pool rectifier fired by a pulse on an igniter electrode, could be timed to the alternator’s waveform, so it could rectify or invert on command. The Bevatron’s eight high-voltage cubicles held ignitrons in pairs carrying about 2,000 A each (Evans, Frias and Hordos); Lawrence’s Nobel lecture of 1951 already shows the ignitrons and switchgear of its motor-generator plant.

Pre-requisite (continued): Fields in conductors, skin depth, surface resistance

Pre-requisite. Chapter 13 gave Ohm’s law in a conductor, \(\mathbf{J} = \sigma\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) with \(\sigma\) the conductivity in S/m, and the currents a radio-frequency field drives on a cavity’s walls, and its section 3 found the skin depth of a copper wall from Maxwell’s equations. This section takes the same law to a slowly changing field in iron. It derives the power a field dissipates per cubic metre, \(\sigma\mathcal{E}^2\); shows that the field obeys a diffusion equation inside a conductor, so that a changing field reaches only a skin depth \(\sqrt{2/(\omega\mu\sigma)}\) into it, with \(\mu\) including iron’s large permeability; and turns that into the rule for laminations. The next section uses all three for the eddy currents in a ring magnet. As in that section, \(\sigma\) here is a conductivity, and \(\delta_s\) is used for the skin depth to keep it apart from the momentum deviation \(\delta\). Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, §4.1.3 (resistance, Ohm’s law and Joule heating) and §8.6.2 (fields in conductors and the skin depth); Livingston and Blewett, §13-4 (the Cosmotron’s laminated magnet); Chao and others, Handbook, §7.2.1 (laminated cores, their thickness set by the ramp rate) and §7.2.3.3 (eddy currents and the skin depth in iron).

The power. An electric field \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) (written \(\mathcal{E}\), as in F4, to keep it apart from the energy \(E\)) pushes on the charges in a conductor. If the charge density of the carriers is \(\rho_q\) (C/m\(^3\)) and they drift at velocity \(\mathbf{v}\), the force on a cubic metre of them is \(\rho_q\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\), and the rate at which the field does work on them is \(\rho_q\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\), since \(\mathbf{J} = \rho_q\mathbf{v}\). The carriers do not speed up: they hand the energy to the lattice in collisions, as heat. With Ohm’s law,

\[ \frac{P}{V} = \mathbf{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = \sigma\mathcal{E}^2 = \frac{J^2}{\sigma}\quad(\text{W/m}^3). \]

Integrated over a wire of length \(l\) and cross-section \(A\) carrying current \(I = JA\), it gives \(I^2 l/(\sigma A) = I^2R\), the Joule heating of a resistor (Tong, §4.1.3).

At work. In the next section the field in a sheet of iron, changing at \(\dot{B}\), induces an electric field \(\mathcal{E} = \dot{B}x\) at a distance \(x\) from the mid-plane. In a Cosmotron plate, 12.7 mm thick, at its highest rate of rise of 1.6 T/s (Livingston and Blewett, §13-4), \(\mathcal{E}\) at the surface is \(1.6 \times 0.00635 = 0.010\) V/m. With \(\sigma = 10^7\) S/m the power density there is \(\sigma\mathcal{E}^2 = 1.0\) kW/m\(^3\); averaged over the thickness it is a third of that, \(\sigma d^2\dot{B}^2/12 = 344\) W/m\(^3\).

When the eddy currents push back. That calculation assumed that the eddy currents are too weak to change the field that drives them. They are not always. Each eddy current makes its own field, opposing the change (Lenz), so the field inside the conductor lags behind the field at its surface. To see how far it gets, combine three laws. Faraday: \(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t\). Ampère, in matter, without the displacement current: \(\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J} = \sigma\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\). And \(\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}\) with \(\mu = \mu_r\mu_0\), taken constant. Dropping the displacement current is safe in a metal: it is smaller than the conduction current by \(\omega\varepsilon_0/\sigma\), which for iron at 60 Hz is \(3\times10^{-16}\). Take the curl of Ampère’s law and use Faraday’s:

\[ \nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{B}) = \mu\sigma\,\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\mu\sigma\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}. \]

A vector identity, met in chapter 13 and checked by writing out the components, says \(\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{B}) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}) - \nabla^2\mathbf{B}\), with \(\nabla^2\) the Laplacian of F2, section 12 acting on each component. Since \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0\), the left side is \(-\nabla^2\mathbf{B}\), and

\[ \frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{\mu\sigma}\,\nabla^2\mathbf{B}. \]

This is a diffusion equation, the same as for heat in a solid, and the same as the equation \(\partial n/\partial t = \mathcal{D}\,\partial^2n/\partial x^2\) for the density of a gas spreading by random steps in chapter 3. A field change at the surface soaks inwards, with diffusivity \(1/(\mu\sigma)\) (m\(^2\)/s) in place of \(\mathcal{D}\). A diffusion goes as the square root of time: in a time \(t\) it reaches a depth of order \(\sqrt{t/(\mu\sigma)}\). Two results follow.

The skin depth. Let the field at the surface of a thick conductor, \(z = 0\), oscillate as \(B_0\cos\omega t\). Try \(B = \mathrm{Re}\,[B_0 e^{-\kappa z}e^{i\omega t}]\), the complex-exponential way of writing an oscillation (F5, section 2). The time derivative brings down \(i\omega\) and the second derivative in \(z\) brings down \(\kappa^2\), so the equation \(i\omega B = \kappa^2B/(\mu\sigma)\) gives \(\kappa^2 = i\omega\mu\sigma\). And since \(\sqrt{i} = (1+i)/\sqrt2\) (square it: \((1 + 2i + i^2)/2 = i\)),

\[ \kappa = \frac{1+i}{\delta_s}, \qquad \delta_s = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega\mu\sigma}}, \]

taking the root that decays into the metal. So

\[ B(z,t) = B_0\,e^{-z/\delta_s}\cos(\omega t - z/\delta_s). \]

The field falls by \(e\) in each skin depth and lags in phase as it goes in. Tong (§8.6.2) reaches the same length from waves entering a conductor. The new point for magnets is the \(\mu\): iron’s permeability of order 1000 shrinks the skin depth by \(\sqrt{1000} = 32\) against a non-magnetic metal of the same conductivity.

At work. Copper (\(\sigma = 5.8\times10^7\) S/m, \(\mu_r = 1\)): 8.5 mm at 60 Hz, 66 μm at 1 MHz. Iron (\(\sigma = 10^7\) S/m, \(\mu_r = 1000\)) at 60 Hz: 0.65 mm. Silicon steel (\(\sigma = 2\times10^6\) S/m, \(\mu_r = 1000\)) at 60 Hz: 1.45 mm. A sheet several skin depths thick carries almost no changing flux in its middle.

The diffusion time. A ring magnet does not oscillate at one frequency; it ramps. The useful number is then how long the field takes to soak through a sheet of thickness \(d\). For a slab whose two faces are held at a new field, the slowest part of the difference between inside and outside has the shape \(\cos(\pi x/d)\), with \(x\) from the mid-plane: half a cosine wave, zero at both faces. Put \(B = \cos(\pi x/d)\,e^{-t/\tau_d}\) into the diffusion equation: the left side gives \(-1/\tau_d\) times \(B\), the right side \(-(\pi/d)^2/(\mu\sigma)\) times \(B\), so it decays as \(e^{-t/\tau_d}\) with

\[ \tau_d = \frac{\mu\sigma d^2}{\pi^2}. \]

If \(\tau_d\) is short compared with the ramp, the field inside follows the surface closely and the weak-eddy formula of the next section holds. In a steady ramp at rate \(\dot{B}\), the diffusion equation has the solution in which the whole sheet rises at \(\dot{B}\), with the centre lagging the faces by a fixed amount:

\[ B_{face} - B_{centre} = \frac{\mu\sigma\dot{B}\,d^2}{8}. \]

To be sure of this, put \(\partial B/\partial t = \dot{B}\) everywhere in the diffusion equation: \(d^2B/dx^2 = \mu\sigma\dot{B}\), a parabola, whose rise from the centre to a face at \(x = d/2\) is \(\mu\sigma\dot{B}d^2/8\). Divided by \(\mu\), the same parabola says that the field \(H\) at the faces must exceed that at the centre by \(\sigma\dot{B}d^2/8\): the eddy currents behave like a winding opposing the main one, and their opposition is set by \(\dot{B}\), not by \(B\). In a magnet with a gap there is a twist. The gap and the current fix the total flux the iron carries, so the eddy currents cannot reduce it much; they redistribute it. The field near the faces runs ahead of the average and the centre falls behind, by the same parabola, and near the faces this can drive the iron towards saturation (Halbach and Schlueter, in Chao and others, §7.2.3.3).

At work. The Cosmotron’s half-inch plates (Livingston and Blewett, §13-4, who say the choice was justified by a detailed analysis checked on models): with \(\sigma = 10^7\) S/m and \(\mu_r = 1000\), as at low field, \(\tau_d = 0.21\) s, not small against the 1 s rise, and at 1.6 T/s the centre would lag the faces by 0.41 T. At high field, where the incremental \(\mu_r\) is nearer 100, \(\tau_d = 21\) ms and the lag is 0.04 T. The opposing field is \(\sigma\dot{B}d^2/8 = 323\) A/m; along an assumed 3 m of iron that is about 970 ampere-turns, against 5750 for the gap at the 31.6 mT injection field. Such numbers, largest at low field and different in the pole tips and in the back of the yoke, are why eddy currents distort the field shape at injection, and why the magnet was laminated at all.

Exercise D1. Show that the power density \(\sigma\mathcal{E}^2\), with \(\mathcal{E} = \dot{B}x\), averaged across a sheet of thickness \(d\), is \(\sigma d^2\dot{B}^2/12\), and find it for a 0.35 mm silicon-steel lamination (\(\sigma = 2\times10^6\) S/m) at a steady 1.6 T/s.

Answer

The average of \(x^2\) over \(-d/2 < x < d/2\) is \((1/d)\int x^2\,dx = (1/d)(2/3)(d/2)^3 = d^2/12\). So \(\langle\sigma\mathcal{E}^2\rangle = \sigma\dot{B}^2d^2/12\). For the lamination, \(2\times10^6 \times 1.6^2 \times (3.5\times10^{-4})^2/12 = 0.052\) W/m\(^3\), against 344 W/m\(^3\) for the Cosmotron’s 12.7 mm iron plate at the same rate: a factor of 1300 from the square of the thickness and 5 from the conductivity.

Exercise D2. A ring is to be ramped from injection to top field in about 1 s. Estimate the diffusion time of a 50 mm solid iron plate (\(\sigma = 10^7\) S/m) for \(\mu_r = 1000\) and \(\mu_r = 100\). Can the weak-eddy loss formula be trusted for it?

Answer

\(\tau_d = \mu_r\mu_0\sigma d^2/\pi^2 = 1000 \times 1.257\times10^{-6} \times 10^7 \times 0.0025/9.87 = 3.2\) s for \(\mu_r = 1000\), and 0.32 s for \(\mu_r = 100\). At low field the plate’s interior would lag the ramp by seconds; only at high field, where the permeability has fallen, does the field soak through within the rise. The formula \(\sigma d^2\dot{B}^2/12\) is then not to be trusted at low field: the loss it gives is small, but the field lag, which distorts the field at injection, is the larger problem.

Exercise D3. At what frequency is the skin depth of copper equal to the 12.7 mm thickness of a Cosmotron plate? What does that say about a copper vacuum chamber wall 2 mm thick in a magnet ramped in 1 s?

Answer

\(\delta_s = d\) gives \(\omega = 2/(\mu_0\sigma d^2) = 2/(1.257\times10^{-6} \times 5.8\times10^7 \times 1.613\times10^{-4}) = 170\) rad/s, \(f = 27\) Hz. For a 2 mm wall, \(\tau_d = \mu_0\sigma d^2/\pi^2 = 1.257\times10^{-6} \times 5.8\times10^7 \times 4\times10^{-6}/9.87 = 30\) μs, so the field soaks through at once, and the weak-eddy picture holds. The wall’s eddy currents are then simply proportional to \(\dot{B}\) and to the width of the wall they circulate round, which is why the next section says they distort the field most where the chamber is widest, and why early rings used glass or resin chambers.

12. Eddy currents and laminated iron

A changing field induces an electric field in any conductor it threads, by Faraday’s law (Landau and Lifshitz, volume 2, section 26), and the current that flows dissipates power and makes its own field. Take a sheet of iron of thickness \(d\) (m) and conductivity \(\sigma\) (S/m; in this section only, \(\sigma\) is a conductivity, not a cross-section or a beam size), with the field changing at \(\dot{B}\) in the plane of the sheet. The field threads loops in the cross-section; for a sheet much wider than it is thick, the induced electric field runs along the sheet, perpendicular to \(\mathbf{B}\), and grows linearly across the thickness: \(\mathcal{E}(x) = \dot{B}x\), with \(x\) measured from the mid-plane, if the eddy currents are too weak to change the field. The step is Faraday’s law (F4, section 14) around a long rectangular loop of length \(\ell\) in the sheet, its two long sides at \(+x\) and \(-x\). It encloses the flux \(B \cdot 2x\ell\), which changes at \(2x\ell\dot{B}\). By symmetry the field along the two long sides is equal and opposite, so they add round the loop, and the short ends contribute little: \(2\mathcal{E}\ell = 2x\ell\dot{B}\), so \(\mathcal{E} = \dot{B}x\). The current density is \(\sigma\dot{B}x\) and the power per unit volume, averaged over the thickness, is

\[ \frac{P}{V} = \frac{\sigma\dot{B}^2}{d}\int_{-d/2}^{d/2}x^2\,dx = \frac{\sigma d^2\dot{B}^2}{12}. \]

The loss grows as the square of the thickness. Cutting the iron into thin insulated laminations divides it.

Worked numbers. For iron, \(\sigma \approx 10^7\) S/m. At the Bevatron’s 1 T/s, a 1 mm sheet loses 0.83 W/m\(^3\), and a 50 mm plate 2.1 kW/m\(^3\): slow proton rings could use thick plates. For a magnet cycled at 60 Hz with a 1 T peak (an illustration for a fast electron ring), \(B = \sin\omega t\) tesla gives \(\dot{B} = \omega\cos\omega t\), and since the mean of \(\cos^2\) over a cycle is \(1/2\), the mean of \(\dot{B}^2\) is \((2\pi\cdot60\cdot1)^2/2\); silicon steel (\(\sigma \approx 2\times10^6\) S/m) in 0.35 mm sheets loses 1.5 kW/m\(^3\), while 25 mm plate would, by the formula, lose 7.4 MW/m\(^3\). The formula has in fact failed there: the eddy currents screen the field, which then penetrates only a skin depth \(\sqrt{2/(\omega\mu\sigma)}\), about 1.5 mm for \(\mu_r = 1000\) at 60 Hz. Laminations must be thinner than the skin depth, or the inside of the iron never sees the field.

The vacuum chamber is the other conductor in the gap. Eddy currents in a metal chamber make a field of their own, proportional to \(\dot{B}\), which is largest where the wall is widest and so distorts the field shape and the field index during the rise. Early rings used chambers of non-conductors: the General Electric 70 MeV ring a glass one, which let its synchrotron light be seen; Nimrod an epoxy-resin and glass-cloth laminate (Gray). McMillan’s memoir names the control of eddy currents among the problems of the Berkeley electron ring.

13. The frequency sweep and the tunable cavity

A proton ring needs a gap voltage of a few kilovolts whose frequency follows the field over a factor of up to 7, programmed to a few parts in \(10^4\). A resonant circuit gives a large voltage for little power, but resonates at one frequency, \(\omega = 1/\sqrt{LC_{cav}}\) (here \(C_{cav}\) is the circuit’s capacitance). To sweep it, change \(L\) or \(C_{cav}\) in time.

The Bevatron’s accelerating electrode, a drift tube between two grounded planes, formed the capacitive part of the tuned circuit of the final amplifier (Crebbin and Lothrop). The master oscillator’s inductance was a ferrite core, saturated by a current proportional to the guide field: as the field rose, the core’s permeability fell, the inductance fell and the frequency rose, following the field coarsely. Fine corrections came from voltage-variable capacitors (whose capacitance is set by a control voltage), driven by an open-loop correction curve, a radial-position loop and a phase loop (Evans and Lothrop, 1971). A ferrite is the right material because it is magnetic but an insulator, so it has no eddy currents at megahertz, and its incremental permeability falls steeply under a bias field. For a 6.8 times change in frequency at fixed capacitance, the inductance must change by \(6.84^2 = 47\) times.

Birmingham, building the first proton synchrotron to be designed, solved the same problem with an uninsulated, highly non-linear inductor lowered into a bath of mercury, with fine adjustment by filing the edge of a rotating disc to shape (Wilson). The Cosmotron’s cavity was one of the first of the modern kind, with ferrite in its inductive part (Vretenar). Ferrite-loaded cavities, with a bias current to tune them, became the standard for proton rings (chapter 13).

How it was researched and built

Proof at Woolwich, 1946. Late in the war Kerst had built a portable betatron at the request of the research laboratory at Woolwich Arsenal in London. Frank Goward read McMillan’s paper and saw that a betatron already contains a synchrotron’s first half: a ring orbit, a rising field, and electrons already near the speed of light. With D. E. Barnes he mounted a quarter-wave resonator of open-weave construction, a cage of wires, on the doughnut (Livingston and Blewett, section 12-1, place it outside the vacuum chamber) and fed it RF. The magnet was driven harder than for a betatron, so that the central flux core saturated and betatron acceleration stopped; then the RF took over. In August 1946 they accelerated electrons from 4 to 8 MeV, the first synchrotron acceleration anywhere (S. Y. Lee’s textbook, in a footnote to section 1.I.4, places the work at the Telecommunications Research Laboratory rather than Woolwich; the letter’s own address was not seen). Their letter in Nature (September 1946) is titled “Experimental 8 MeV synchrotron for electron acceleration”. Wilson reproduces their oscilloscope record: with the RF on, a barely visible blip on the beam-loss trace at 80° of the field cycle, where the betatron alone had given its beam at 30°.

General Electric, 1946 to 1947. At the General Electric Research Laboratory in Schenectady, a team with long experience of betatrons (F. R. Elder, A. M. Gurewitsch, R. V. Langmuir and H. C. Pollock), urged on by John Blewett, built a 70 MeV synchrotron. It ran about two months after the Woolwich machine (Wilson). Its vacuum chamber was glass. On 24 April 1947 a technician, Floyd Haber, saw a bright arc of light in the tube; it was the radiation Blewett had seen in its effect on the orbit of the 100 MeV betatron (chapter 11), now seen with the eye. Their letter, “Radiation from electrons in a synchrotron”, was received by Physical Review on 7 May 1947. The light is the subject of chapter 20.

Berkeley and Cornell. McMillan directed the building of Berkeley’s 300 MeV electron synchrotron himself. The electrons were accelerated as in a betatron to about 2 MeV, then by a cavity, on a 1 m radius. Jackson and Panofsky list its problems: a vacuum chamber exposed to the radiation, magnets that had to give the right focusing and control eddy currents, power supplies that switched large currents to time the magnet’s cycle, and the RF. It reached full energy, 320 MeV, in January 1949 (Livingston and Blewett, section 12-1). It made pions (light, short-lived particles of rest energy about 140 MeV, F7, section 15) from photons from 1949 (McMillan and Peterson), and showed the existence of the neutral pion. At Cornell a 300 MeV electron synchrotron was under construction when R. R. Wilson arrived in 1947; he began a 1.4 GeV machine there in 1952.

Birmingham. Oliphant returned to Birmingham and argued for a 1 GeV proton synchrotron with the Department of Scientific and Industrial Research. By his own account he was shattered by McMillan’s and Veksler’s papers, which nonetheless supported his case. The project was slowed by post-war shortages and administration; much of the work force was his graduate students. Besides the mercury-bath inductor, the magnet’s pulse came from a motor-generator set servo-controlled by light reflected from 120 mirrors on a disc (Wilson). It accelerated protons to just short of 1 GeV in July 1953, a year after Brookhaven’s ring. Livingston and Blewett (section 13-2) call it the first proton synchrotron to be started, and Oliphant’s 1943 proposal, made to the British Directorate of Atomic Energy, the first proposal of a ring magnet with both field and frequency varied.

The Cosmotron. The Atomic Energy Commission approved a proton synchrotron for the new Brookhaven National Laboratory in April 1948 (Brookhaven’s history). The maximum energies had been fixed in March: about 3 GeV for Brookhaven and about twice that for Berkeley (Lawrence and His Laboratory; Lawrence’s Nobel lecture). Berkeley first built and studied a quarter-scale model. The Cosmotron team (M. S. Livingston, John and Hildred Blewett, Ernest Courant, Kenneth Green and others) settled on C-shaped magnets open to the outside, so that the vacuum chamber could be reached around its whole outer edge (Livingston and Blewett, section 13-3), 288 blocks around a ring about 23 m across, with a field index chosen for focusing in both planes. When they first switched on in early 1952 the beam would not accelerate. For two months they doubted their calculations; the fault was a burnt-out voltage divider in the RF system (Wilson). In May 1952 it passed 1 GeV, the first machine to do so, and in 1953 it reached its 3.3 GeV (Brookhaven’s history). Livingston and Blewett (section 13-2), who built it, tell the steps differently: 2.3 GeV in 1952, the most the power supply then installed allowed, and its design energy of 3.0 GeV announced in early 1954, once the supply was completed. It was the first synchrotron to give an external beam to experiments (Brookhaven’s history), and it ran until 1966.

Its top energy was limited by saturation of the C magnets, which added a negative gradient at high field: Livingston and Blewett (sections 13-3 and 13-4) put the usable field at about 14 kG, where saturation became significant, and describe the pole-face windings added to hold \(n\) at high field. Livingston proposed turning some magnets round, with their backs to the outside, to compensate; Courant was asked to check whether the alternation would spoil the focusing, and found that it improved it. Snyder recalled the optical analogy of alternating convex and concave lenses, and in 1952 Courant, Livingston and Snyder published the strong-focusing synchrotron (chapter 16).

The Bevatron. William Brobeck’s design of 1947 had an aperture of 4 ft by 14 ft, which with the magnet then planned would have given only 1.5 GeV. A quarter-scale model running in 1949 showed that the protons could be steered more accurately than feared, and the gap was cut to 2 ft by 6 ft for 3.67 GeV. In December 1951, with the model’s results, the experience with big beams in the laboratory’s cold-war MTA project (the Materials Testing Accelerator, a large linear accelerator built at Livermore to make plutonium and tritium with the neutrons from its beam), and calculations from Brookhaven, the laboratory cut it again to 1 ft by 4 ft and aimed directly for 6 GeV: “the threshold for antiproton production” (Lawrence and His Laboratory). McMillan had calculated that 6 GeV would comfortably make proton-antiproton pairs. The machine was completed in 1954 and reached 6.2 GeV (Livingston and Blewett, section 13-2, give full-power operation at 5.7 GeV in October 1954, with 6.4 GeV possible at a slower cycling rate). It had four curved and four straight sections, a magnet of about 10,000 tons, and two motor generators with heavy flywheels; the laboratory’s history quotes an estimated cost of one to two million US dollars for each GeV, about ten times the 184-inch cyclotron.

The threshold, derived. To make an antiproton from a proton beam on protons at rest, \(p + p \to p + p + p + \bar{p}\), the total energy in the centre-of-mass frame must at least equal the rest energies of the four final particles. The square of the total four-momentum, \(s\), is the same in every frame (Landau and Lifshitz, volume 2, sections 9 and 11; taught in chapter 1): \(s = E_{tot}^2 - p_{tot}^2c^2\), the total energy squared less the total momentum times \(c\) squared. In the laboratory, a beam proton of total energy \(E\) and momentum \(p\) hits a proton at rest, so the total energy is \(E + mc^2\) and the total momentum is \(p\):

\[ s = (E + mc^2)^2 - (pc)^2 = 2m^2c^4 + 2mc^2E, \]

expanding the square and using \(E^2 - p^2c^2 = m^2c^4\) for the beam proton.

At threshold the four final particles are at rest in the centre-of-mass frame, so \(s = (4mc^2)^2 = 16m^2c^4\). Then \(2mc^2E = 14m^2c^4\), \(E = 7mc^2\), and the kinetic energy is

\[ W_{th} = E - mc^2 = 6mc^2 = 5.63\ \mathrm{GeV}. \]

At 6.2 GeV, \(\sqrt{s} = 3.893\) GeV, 140 MeV above \(4mc^2\). In a nucleus the target proton moves, which lowers the effective threshold a little. In 1955 Owen Chamberlain, Emilio Segrè, Clyde Wiegand and Thomas Ypsilantis took negative particles of 1.19 GeV/\(c\) from a copper target, timed them over a flight path and identified antiprotons by their speed, \(\beta = 0.785\) against 0.993 for pions (chapter 1). Their paper was received on 24 October 1955; Chamberlain and Segrè shared the Nobel Prize in 1959.

Dubna. Veksler led the design of a 10 GeV proton synchrotron for the Soviet Union from 1949 to 1950; it was commissioned in April 1957 at what had become the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research at Dubna. It had a circumference of 208 m and a magnet of 36,000 tonnes (the mass from Baldin and colleagues, 1981). Wilson notes that it held the world’s highest energy from 1957 until the CERN Proton Synchrotron ran. Its magnet ring proved hard to keep aligned: in 1981 its operators reported field errors from deformations of the magnet’s foundation, which was not rigid enough; realigning the magnet was practically impossible without dismantling the vacuum chamber, and the pole-face windings corrected the errors only in part.

How long and how many. The Cosmotron took four years from approval to beam and the Bevatron about six. The weak-focusing synchrotron remained the choice of cautious laboratories for some years after strong focusing was known: Argonne’s Zero Gradient Synchrotron, Saturne in France and Nimrod in Britain followed the Cosmotron (Wilson). Nimrod gave 7 GeV protons, 1 to \(1.5\times10^{12}\) per pulse, in the 1960s (Gray).

Design today. Nobody now builds a weak-focusing proton synchrotron; the rings built since 1960 use strong focusing (chapter 16). But the synchrotron principle is in every ring, and a designer still works through the steps of this chapter in the same order. Choose the top momentum and the peak field, which fixes \(\rho = p/(qB)\); choose the fraction of the ring filled with bending field, which fixes \(C\); choose the injection energy, which fixes the field ratio and the frequency range; choose \(h\), which fixes the frequency and the number of bunches; choose the ramp rate, which fixes the volts per turn and the power supply. Lattice codes compute the optics and the closed orbit from the magnets; field codes compute the magnets; longitudinal tracking codes follow the bunches through capture and acceleration. The magnet and its supply are chapter 17, the cavity chapter 13.

At the bench

This section draws on the published operating papers of the Bevatron (1965 to 1971) and Nimrod (1967), and on Wilson’s history.

What is in the building. A ring of magnets in its own hall, the vacuum chamber inside the gaps, with an injector at one straight section and the accelerating electrode or cavity in another. Elsewhere the motor-generator plant: alternators and flywheels on their shafts, the induction motors that keep them turning, and the rectifier cubicles. And the control room, with oscilloscopes showing the cycle, the RF controls, the field markers and the injector.

The magnet cycle. A Bevatron pulse: field from about 300 gauss to 15.5 kilogauss and back. The 1.85 s acceleration was followed by the fall and a pause; the whole cycle took several seconds. Nimrod pulsed 22 times a minute. Later, both ran with a flat top, a period at constant field for slow spilling of the beam onto targets: half a second at Nimrod, up to 2 s at the Bevatron by 1969, with the field held flat to half a gauss.

The field clock. The RF, the injector and the targets must all be timed to the field, not to the clock on the wall, because the field’s rise varies from pulse to pulse with the speed of the flywheels. So the field is measured continuously. A winding in the gap gives a voltage proportional to \(\dot{B}\). In the Bevatron’s system of 1971 a voltage-to-frequency converter turned it into a train of pulses, one per 0.01 gauss of change, counted up on the rise and down on the fall, so that the count was the field; before each pulse the remanent field was measured and preset into the counter. Sixteen markers, one at each kilogauss, went to the rest of the machine (Evans, Frias and Hordos). This is a B-clock: field increments as pulses. Earlier systems gave a pulse when the magnet current passed set levels. Injection is triggered at a chosen field, and every later event is set by a field value.

The frequency programme. The RF oscillator follows the field coarsely through its saturated ferrite. On top of that the operator sets an open-loop correction curve, adjusted until the beam stays at the centre of the aperture. Then the loops are closed: a radial loop that compares the beam’s position at a pair of electrodes with the wanted radius and corrects the frequency, and a phase loop that compares the beam’s phase with the RF and damps the synchrotron oscillations. At Nimrod both were used for the first 40 ms of the rise (Gray). The beam’s radius could be read from the RF itself: Crebbin and Lothrop timed 18,535 RF cycles against a 10 MHz clock, so that near the 600 inch radius a radial shift of 1 inch changed the count by 100.

The current transformer over the cycle. A ring of magnetic core around the beam pipe reads the circulating current \(I = Nqf_0\) (chapter 8). A typical trace through a cycle: 1. A spike at injection as the injector’s pulse fills the ring, often a long staircase or ramp over many turns. 2. A sharp fall in the first milliseconds: the particles not captured by the RF spiral out or in as the field rises and are lost. At Nimrod, the linac sent 18 to 20 mA for 350 µs, about \(4\times10^{13}\) protons, and 1 to \(1.5\times10^{12}\) were accelerated: 2 to 4 per cent. Gray also reports a further loss of about 30 per cent in the first 25 ms that was not explained. 3. A slow rise through the acceleration, even with no further loss, because \(f_0\) rises. For \(10^{12}\) protons on the Bevatron’s 120 m the current is 58 mA at 10 MeV and 0.40 A at the top. To read the number of particles the operator divides by \(f_0\). 4. Steps or a slow decay: losses on obstacles, gas or resonances. 5. At the top, the beam goes to targets: a sharp drop for a fast target, a long ramp down for a slow spill.

The intensity at the top. The figure of merit is protons per pulse at full energy, and with the repetition rate it gives the average current. Nimrod’s \(1.5\times10^{12}\) per pulse at 22 a minute is \(5.5\times10^{11}\) protons a second, an average of 88 nA. In one year it ran 4,590 of 5,560 scheduled hours, 82.5 per cent (Gray). The Bevatron went through an improvement programme in 1962 to 1963 (Salsig and Vogel), and the Synchrophasotron’s beam rose from \(2\times10^{11}\) to \(4\times10^{12}\) protons per pulse in the five years to 1981, mainly by more current from the linac and better correction of the field (Baldin and colleagues).

The noise and the power swings. Each pulse moves tens of megajoules between flywheels and magnet; the generators slow and speed up by tens of revolutions a minute, and with a long flat top, by more. The rectifiers make ripple at multiples of the mains frequency, and hum and noise on the frequency control show as jitter in the beam’s position and in the spill. The Bevatron went digital in about 1970 because noise and hum on the analogue frequency control spoiled resonant extraction (drawing the beam out slowly by bringing its tune onto a resonance, chapter 21), which needed the field set to one part in 15,000 (Evans and Lothrop; Evans, Frias and Hordos).

Setting up, in order. 1. Pulse the magnet; check the field markers against the reference and the flat-top level. 2. Bring the injector up and time its pulse to the injection field marker. 3. Set the RF frequency at injection and the voltage programme; look on the current transformer for the beam surviving the first milliseconds. 4. Adjust the open-loop frequency correction until the radial pick-ups show the beam near the centre through the rise; then close the radial and phase loops. 5. Watch for loss on the current transformer and correct where it happens: the field index with pole-face windings near injection (as at Nimrod for the first 75 ms), the frequency later. 6. Set the flat top, move the targets in, and set the spill.

Ways it fails, with their signatures. - No beam after a few milliseconds. The RF is off, at the wrong frequency, or at the wrong phase: the uncaptured beam is lost as the field rises. The Cosmotron’s first two months were this, from a burnt-out divider in the RF system. - Beam lost at a definite field, the same every pulse. The frequency programme has drifted from the field: the beam walks to one wall at the same point in the cycle. Moving the frequency correction moves the loss. - Captured intensity stops growing with injector current. Space charge at injection and a larger emittance (the area the beam fills in the plane of position against angle, chapter 7) from the injector at high current (Nimrod; chapter 22). - The beam grows suddenly at a definite time. A coherent instability: at Nimrod the resistive-wall instability after 40 ms, cured by steering the beam off the central radius (chapter 22). - Jitter in spill and position. Hum and noise on the frequency or field control. - An ignitron misfires. One tube of a pair fails to fire and its partner carries both currents, which can destroy it (Evans, Frias and Hordos); the protection stops the following firing pulses or phases them back. - The machine stops for months. The rotating plant fails by fatigue. On 21 February 1965 one of Nimrod’s alternators failed after \(7.25\times10^6\) pulses, with a thud and smoke, from fatigue cracks in the rotor’s end plates; Nimrod could not run at 7 GeV until the end of November (Brooks and Reed). In mid-December 1966 the pole dovetails of the Bevatron’s generators were found fractured, after 37.4 million pulses; replacing the poles took six months, during which the Bevatron could not run (Salsig and Vogel).

Where it stops

The synchrotron principle itself has no energy limit: a ring of fixed radius, a field that follows the momentum and a frequency that follows the speed are used in every circular accelerator built since. What stopped was weak focusing. With tunes below 1 the beam’s oscillations, its dispersion and its orbit errors all scale with the radius, so the aperture does, and the magnet’s mass grows as about \(p^3\): 1,640 tons at 3.3 GeV, 10,000 tons at 6.2 GeV, 36,000 tonnes at 10 GeV. A 30 GeV weak-focusing ring would have needed on the order of a million tonnes of magnet.

The way past was to make the focusing strong. Alternate the sign of the gradient from magnet to magnet, with \(|n|\) in the hundreds instead of below 1, and the net effect focuses in both planes, as a pair of equal convex and concave lenses does. The tunes rise to several, the amplitudes and the dispersion shrink, and the aperture shrinks with them. Courant, Livingston and Snyder published the idea in 1952. Visiting Brookhaven in 1952, the CERN group of Odd Dahl, Frank Goward and Rolf Wideroe abandoned their planned 10 GeV weak-focusing machine for a 25 GeV strong-focusing one at the same price (Wilson). The price of strong focusing was transition: with \(\gamma_t \approx Q_x\) in the energy range, the protons must cross it. The CERN PS did so only after Wolfgang Schnell’s improvised circuit, built in a coffee tin, brought the beam through on the night of 24 November 1959 (a radial loop in Vretenar’s account, a circuit to change the RF phase at transition in Wilson’s; chapter 16); Brookhaven’s Alternating Gradient Synchrotron reached 33 GeV in July 1960. The optics are chapter 16, the magnets chapter 17, transition and the longitudinal motion chapter 19, and for electrons the next wall, radiation, chapter 20.

Exercises

1. Find \(B\rho\) and the field at the top of the Bevatron’s cycle, for 6.2 GeV protons on a 15.24 m radius.

Answer

\(E = 6.2 + 0.9383 = 7.138\) GeV, \(pc = \sqrt{E^2 - (mc^2)^2} = 7.076\) GeV, \(B\rho = 3.3356 \times 7.076 = 23.60\) T m, and \(B = 23.60/15.24 = 1.549\) T.

2. For the Bevatron with \(C = 120\) m and \(h = 1\), find the RF frequency at injection (10 MeV) and at the top (6.2 GeV), and the field at which the sweep is half done.

Answer

At 10 MeV, \(\gamma = 1.0107\), \(\beta = 0.1448\), \(f = \beta c/C = 0.362\) MHz. At 6.2 GeV, \(\beta = 0.9913\), \(f = 2.477\) MHz; the ratio is 6.84. Half-way in frequency, 1.42 MHz, means \(\beta = 0.568\), \(\beta\gamma = 0.690\), \(p = 0.648\) GeV/\(c\), \(B\rho = 2.16\) T m and \(B = 0.142\) T: half the frequency sweep is done at 9 per cent of the peak field. (From \(f(B) = (c/C)\,B/\sqrt{B^2 + B_m^2}\) with \(B_m = 0.205\) T, the same: \(B = B_m\,\beta/\sqrt{1-\beta^2}\).)

3. Derive the threshold kinetic energy for \(p + p \to p + p + p + \bar{p}\) on a stationary proton, and find by how much \(\sqrt{s}\) exceeds four proton masses at 6.2 GeV.

Answer

\(s = 2m^2c^4 + 2mc^2E\); at threshold \(s = 16m^2c^4\), so \(E = 7mc^2\) and \(W_{th} = 6mc^2 = 5.630\) GeV. At \(W = 6.2\) GeV, \(E = 7.138\) GeV and \(\sqrt{s} = \sqrt{2(0.9383)^2 + 2(0.9383)(7.138)} = 3.893\) GeV, against \(4mc^2 = 3.753\) GeV: 140 MeV above threshold.

4. The Bevatron’s field rose on average at 0.82 T/s. Find the energy gain per turn, the synchronous phase for a 5 kV gap, and check against four million turns in 1.85 s.

Answer

\(\Delta W = eC\rho\dot{B} = 120 \times 15.24 \times 0.82\) eV \(= 1.50\) keV a turn. \(\sin\phi_s = 0.30\); the ring is above transition, so \(\phi_s = 180^\circ - 17.5^\circ = 162.5^\circ\). Four million turns at 1.50 keV give 6.0 GeV, and \((6.2\ \text{GeV} - 10\ \text{MeV})/4\times10^6 = 1.55\) keV a turn: consistent, given the rounded figures.

5. A weak-focusing ring has \(n = 0.6\) and no straight sections. Find \(Q_x\), \(Q_y\), \(\alpha_c\), \(\gamma_t\) and \(\eta\) at 10 MeV and at 6.2 GeV for protons. Does the ring cross transition?

Answer

\(Q_x = \sqrt{0.4} = 0.632\), \(Q_y = \sqrt{0.6} = 0.775\), \(\alpha_c = 1/0.4 = 2.5\), \(\gamma_t = 1/\sqrt{2.5} = 0.632\). At 10 MeV, \(\gamma = 1.0107\) and \(\eta = 2.5 - 0.979 = 1.52\); at 6.2 GeV, \(\gamma = 7.608\) and \(\eta = 2.5 - 0.0173 = 2.48\). Since \(\gamma_t < 1 \le \gamma\), the ring is above transition from injection on and never crosses it.

6. In the ring of exercise 5 with \(\rho = 15.24\) m, how precisely must the RF frequency follow the field at injection to keep the beam within 5 mm of the centre? What happens to the beam if the frequency is set 0.1 per cent high?

Answer

\(D_x = \rho/(1-n) = 38.1\) m, and \(\Delta x = -(D_x/\eta)\,\Delta f/f = -25.0\ \mathrm{m}\times\Delta f/f\). For 5 mm, \(\Delta f/f = 2.0\times10^{-4}\). At \(+10^{-3}\) the beam moves 25 mm inward (a shorter path for a shorter period), five times the 5 mm allowed. In the Bevatron’s gap, 1.2 m wide, that is only 2 per cent of the width, but at injection the beam fills most of the horizontal aperture (section 10), so such a shift scrapes beam on the inner wall.

7. Assuming the magnet mass scales as \(p^3\), predict the Dubna synchrophasotron’s magnet from the Cosmotron’s (288 blocks of about 5.7 tons, 3.3 GeV). Compare with 36,000 tonnes, and find the exponent that would fit exactly.

Answer

\(p = 4.133\) GeV/\(c\) at 3.3 GeV and 10.898 GeV/\(c\) at 10 GeV. \(288 \times 5.7 = 1642\) tons, and \(1642 \times (10.898/4.133)^3 = 30{,}100\) tons. The exponent that fits exactly is \(\ln(36000/1642)/\ln(10.898/4.133) = 3.18\). (Short tons and tonnes differ by 10 per cent, which is within the looseness of the comparison.)

8. Show that a synchrotron with no straight sections needs the same volts per turn as a betatron of the same radius and the same \(\dot{B}\) at the orbit. Then find the flux a betatron would need to take protons to 6.2 GeV on 15.24 m.

Answer

Synchrotron: \(\hat{V}\sin\phi_s = C\rho\dot{B} = 2\pi\rho^2\dot{B}\). Betatron: the two-to-one rule gives \(d\langle B\rangle/dt = 2\dot{B}\) and the voltage per turn \(d\Phi/dt = \pi\rho^2 \cdot 2\dot{B} = 2\pi\rho^2\dot{B}\). The same. Integrating, the betatron needs \(\Delta\Phi = 2\pi\rho^2 B_{top} = 2\pi(15.24)^2(1.549) = 2260\) Wb, a core of about 750 m\(^2\) swung through 3 T. The synchrotron gets the same volts from a resonant gap, a few kilovolts on one electrode, because it uses them a few million times.

9. At Nimrod the 15 MeV linac delivered 18 to 20 mA for 350 µs, and 1 to \(1.5\times10^{12}\) protons were accelerated at 22 pulses a minute. What fraction of the injected protons was accelerated, and what was the average proton current at 7 GeV?

Answer

Injected: \((18\ \text{to}\ 20)\times10^{-3} \times 350\times10^{-6}/e = 3.9\) to \(4.4\times10^{13}\) protons. Accelerated: \(10^{12}\) to \(1.5\times10^{12}\), about 2 to 4 per cent. Average: \(1.5\times10^{12}\times22/60 = 5.5\times10^{11}\) protons a second, or 88 nA. Most injected particles were lost at injection and capture.

10. Derive the eddy-current loss per unit volume in a lamination of thickness \(d\) and conductivity \(\sigma\) in a field changing at \(\dot{B}\). Compare 0.35 mm silicon steel with a 25 mm plate for a 1 T, 60 Hz sinusoidal field, and say why the second answer is wrong.

Answer

Faraday’s law around a loop of half-width \(x\) in the sheet gives \(\mathcal{E}(x) = \dot{B}x\); the power density is \(\sigma\mathcal{E}^2\), averaged over the thickness: \(\sigma\dot{B}^2 d^2/12\). With \(\langle\dot{B}^2\rangle = (2\pi\cdot60)^2/2\) T\(^2\)/s\(^2\) and \(\sigma = 2\times10^6\) S/m: 1.45 kW/m\(^3\) for 0.35 mm and 7.4 MW/m\(^3\) for 25 mm. The second is wrong because the formula assumes the eddy currents do not change the field. At 60 Hz with \(\mu_r = 1000\) the skin depth is 1.5 mm, so a 25 mm plate screens its interior: the loss concentrates in the surface and most of the iron carries no flux. Laminations thinner than the skin depth are needed both to cut the loss and to let the field into the iron.
