10 The cyclotron

1929 to the 1940s: Lawrence’s idea, Livingston’s 4-inch and 11-inch machines, the 27-inch (later 37-inch) and the 60-inch Crocker cyclotron at Berkeley; the wall is the voltage one gap can hold, and the length a straight resonant accelerator needs.

In one page

The wall. In 1929 nobody could hold a million volts across a vacuum tube (chapter 4). Wideroe had shown in 1928 that a particle can take a modest radio-frequency voltage many times in a row of gaps (chapter 9), but for megavolt protons the row becomes many metres long.

The idea. Ernest Lawrence, at Berkeley, read Wideroe’s paper early in 1929 and bent the row of gaps into a spiral. A uniform magnetic field turns a charged particle round in a time that does not depend on its speed, so one gap, fed at one fixed frequency, can accelerate it on every half turn. The orbit grows as the particle gains energy, and the particle spirals out to the edge of the magnet. In January 1931 M. Stanley Livingston’s 4-inch model gave hydrogen molecular ions of about 80 keV with under 1 kV on the dee. On 9 January 1932 the 11-inch machine gave 1.22 MeV protons with 4 kV.

The equations to remember. For a particle of charge \(q\), rest mass \(m\), Lorentz factor \(\gamma\), in a field \(B\), with a gap voltage of amplitude \(\hat{V}\) crossed twice a turn at phase \(\phi\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \omega_0 &= \frac{qB}{\gamma m}, \qquad f_0 = \frac{qB}{2\pi\gamma m} \quad (15.25\ \text{MHz per tesla for slow protons}) \\ W &= \frac{q^2 B^2 R^2}{2m} \quad \text{(final kinetic energy at radius } R\text{, non-relativistic)} \\ \Delta W_{turn} &= 2q\hat{V}\sin\phi, \qquad N = \frac{W}{2q\hat{V}\langle\sin\phi\rangle}, \qquad \Delta r = \frac{R}{2N} \ \text{at the edge} \\ Q_x &= \sqrt{1-n}, \qquad Q_y = \sqrt{n}, \qquad 0 < n < 1, \qquad n = -\frac{r}{B}\frac{\partial B}{\partial r} \\ \Delta\phi_{turn} &= 2\pi\left(\frac{\gamma B_c}{B(r)} - 1\right), \qquad W_{max} \approx 2\sqrt{\frac{q\hat{V} mc^2}{\pi}} \ \text{(field tuned to the centre)} \end{aligned} \]

\(B_c\) is the field that is in resonance for a slow particle, and \(W_{max}\) the energy at which the phase has slipped by half a cycle.

Numbers worth knowing. A slow proton circles at 15.25 MHz in 1 T, a deuteron at 7.63 MHz. 1.22 MeV protons at 11.5 cm need 1.39 T and about 21 MHz. A uniform field cannot hold the beam vertically; the field must fall slightly with radius, which slows the revolution. So does the relativistic mass: 16 MeV deuterons are 0.85% heavier than at rest, and every turn they slip about 3° behind the voltage. With 50 kV across the gap the simple estimate stops deuterons near 11 MeV, protons near 8 MeV; Rose, with a carefully shaped field, found about 20 MeV for deuterons. Berkeley’s machines grew from 80 keV (1931) to 1.22 MeV (1932), 6.3 MeV deuterons (1936) and 16 MeV deuterons on the 60-inch (1939), whose magnet weighed 220 tons.

The wall

A million volts, and none to be had

By the late 1920s Rutherford’s experiments of 1919 had shown that fast alpha particles could change one nucleus into another, and physicists wanted beams of their own: more particles than a radium source, of a chosen kind and a chosen energy. A direct voltage was the obvious route, and it stopped at a few hundred kilovolts. Transformers, rectifiers and glass tubes broke down long before a megavolt (chapter 4, chapter 5). Cockcroft and Walton at the Cavendish and Van de Graaff at Princeton were pushing the direct voltage up (chapter 6), and each kilovolt was paid for in insulation.

Repeated small kicks, in a straight line

The way round was to apply a modest voltage many times. Gustaf Ising proposed it in 1924, and Rolf Wideroe built it at Aachen in 1927 and 1928: a drift tube between two gaps, so that an ion was accelerated at the first gap, hidden inside the tube while the voltage reversed, and accelerated again at the second (chapter 9). Craddock gives Wideroe’s numbers as a 1 MHz, 25 kV oscillator and sodium and potassium ions of about 50 keV; Heilbron and Seidel give 20 kV across each gap and 40 keV ions. At Berkeley, David Sloan built such machines for heavy mercury ions; Heilbron and Seidel record mercury ions of over 1 MeV from a 30-electrode tube by the end of May 1931, and 1.26 MeV from its final version.

The difficulty for light ions is length. A drift tube must be half a radio-frequency wavelength of travel long, \(\beta\lambda/2\), and a proton is fast. Take the voltage and frequency of the 11-inch cyclotron below, 4 kV per gap at 21.2 MHz: a straight machine needs 305 gaps for 1.22 MeV, and its drift tubes add up to 73 m. With 25 kV per gap it still needs 49 gaps and 12 m.

Lawrence reads Wideroe

Ernest Lawrence came to Berkeley from Yale in 1928, aged 27. Wideroe’s thesis was published in the Archiv für Elektrotechnik on 17 December 1928, and Heilbron and Seidel found that the issue reached the university library on 23 January 1929. Lawrence came across it before April 1929; his Nobel lecture says early in 1929, while Livingston and Blewett, from Lawrence’s own telling, put it in the summer. He could not read German easily; as he later said, he looked only at the diagrams and photographs. Craddock points out that the same paper reported Wideroe’s failed attempt at an induction accelerator, blamed on inadequate stabilisation, that is, focusing; Lawrence missed the warning.

The idea he took from it was to use a magnetic field to bring the particle back to the same gap again and again. When he worked out the motion he found that the radius cancels from the period: in a uniform field a slow particle takes the same time for every turn, large or small. One oscillator at one frequency could therefore drive the particle all the way out.

Others had the idea first and did nothing with it: Craddock lists Gabor (1924), Flegler (1926) and Steenbeck (1927), and Szilard filed a patent application in 1929. None published or built.

The first trials did not work

Early in 1930 Lawrence enlisted Niels Edlefsen, who had just finished his doctorate. They built two crude models, one with electrodes made by silvering a flattened glass flask and one with copper electrodes. They saw signals at a detector at the edge but no definite resonance. Lawrence nevertheless described the method at the National Academy of Sciences on 19 September 1930 (published in Science), though Heilbron and Seidel find considerable doubt that Edlefsen achieved any resonance acceleration at all. Edlefsen left for another post in the university, and in September 1930 the work passed to a graduate student who had come to Berkeley in 1929, M. Stanley Livingston.

The physics

1. A charge in a uniform magnetic field

The picture. A magnetic field pushes a moving charge sideways, at right angles to both the velocity and the field. The push never does work, so the speed stays the same and the path curves into a circle. A faster particle needs a larger circle, because it is harder to turn; but it also goes round that larger circle faster. In the non-relativistic limit the two effects cancel exactly.

The derivation. Let the field \(\mathbf{B} = B\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) be uniform, with \(y\) vertical, and let the particle (charge \(q\) in C, rest mass \(m\) in kg) move in the horizontal plane with speed \(v\). The equation of motion is (Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 2, §17 and §21)

\[ \frac{d\mathbf{p}}{dt} = q\,\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}, \qquad \mathbf{p} = \gamma m \mathbf{v}. \]

This is Newton’s second law with the magnetic part of the Lorentz force on the right (chapter 1, section 3), and with the relativistic momentum on the left: \(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}\) is the Lorentz factor, which is 1 for a slow particle and grows without limit as \(v\) approaches the speed of light \(c\) (chapter 1, section 2.1). The cross product \(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\) is a vector at right angles to both \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\), of size \(vB\) when they are perpendicular (F2, section 3).

Since \(\mathbf{v}\cdot(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}) = 0\), the force does no work, the energy and hence \(\gamma\) are constant, so \(\gamma m\, d\mathbf{v}/dt = q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\). The force has constant magnitude \(qvB\) and is always perpendicular to \(\mathbf{v}\): uniform circular motion, whose acceleration points to the centre with size \(v^2/r\) (F2, section 4, where this very calculation is done for \(\gamma = 1\)). Setting the force equal to the mass times the centripetal acceleration,

\[ \frac{\gamma m v^2}{r} = qvB \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = \frac{\gamma m v}{qB} = \frac{p}{qB}, \]

with \(r\) the radius in m. The magnetic rigidity is \(B\rho = p/q\) in T m (chapter 1, section 4.1): the product of field and radius needed to bend a given particle, written with \(\rho\) for the radius of the bend. For a singly charged particle \(B\rho\,[\mathrm{T\,m}] = 3.3356\,p\,[\mathrm{GeV}/c]\), because a momentum of 1 GeV/\(c\) is \(10^9 e/c\) in kg m/s (F7, section 3), and dividing by \(q = e\) leaves \(10^9/c = 3.3356\) T m. The angular frequency of revolution is

\[ \omega_0 = \frac{v}{r} = \frac{qB}{\gamma m}, \qquad f_0 = \frac{\omega_0}{2\pi} = \frac{qB}{2\pi\gamma m}. \]

For \(\gamma \approx 1\), that is \(v \ll c\), \(\omega_0 = qB/m\) does not depend on the speed or the radius. This is the cyclotron frequency. The approximation fails gradually: the frequency falls in proportion to \(1/\gamma\), and \(\gamma - 1 = W/mc^2\), with \(W\) the kinetic energy and \(mc^2\) the rest energy (chapter 1, section 2.3: 938.27 MeV for a proton, 1875.61 MeV for a deuteron). That relation is the total energy \(\gamma mc^2\) split into rest energy and kinetic energy, \(\gamma mc^2 = mc^2 + W\).

Numbers. For slow particles in 1 T, \(f_0\) is 15.25 MHz for protons, 7.626 MHz for deuterons (the nucleus of heavy hydrogen, deuterium: a proton and a neutron bound together, charge \(e\), about twice the proton’s mass), 7.675 MHz for alpha particles (helium nuclei, two protons and two neutrons, charge \(2e\)) and 7.621 MHz for the hydrogen molecular ion H\(_2^+\) (two protons held together by one electron, charge \(e\); F7, section 10 for the nuclei). For an electron it is 27.99 GHz, and since \(\gamma - 1\) is already 0.098 at 50 keV, the electron leaves resonance almost at once; the cyclotron is a machine for ions.

2. The dees and the resonance

The picture. Cut a flat, hollow metal pillbox in half along a diameter. The two halves, called dees from their shape, sit between the poles of the magnet, in vacuum, with a narrow gap between their straight edges. An oscillator puts an alternating voltage between them. Inside a dee there is no electric field, so a particle there moves on a half circle. Each time it crosses the gap it is accelerated, provided the voltage has reversed in the time it took to go half round; since that time is fixed, one frequency serves for every half turn. The ions start at the centre and spiral outward.

Figure 10.1. The cyclotron seen from above, not to scale. The magnetic field points out of the page, so a positive ion circles clockwise. The oscillator drives the two dees against each other; the field \(\mathcal{E}\) is only in the gap between them and reverses every half period. Each crossing of the gap adds energy, so each half circle is a little larger than the one before; the radius grows as the square root of the number of crossings (section 4), and the turns crowd together at the edge. There an electrostatic deflector, a curved electrode at high negative voltage with a thin grounded septum on its inner side, straightens the last turn and lets the beam out (section 10).

The first machines often used one dee and a grounded electrode in place of the second, a “dummy dee”; the particle still crosses a gap twice a turn.

The condition. Let the voltage across the gap be \(V(t) = \hat{V}\sin(\omega_{RF} t)\), with \(\hat{V}\) the amplitude in V and \(\omega_{RF} = 2\pi f_{RF}\) the angular frequency of the oscillator in rad/s. RF stands for radio frequency: the oscillator runs at millions of cycles a second, the frequencies of radio. The phase \(\phi = \omega_{RF}t\) (rad), taken modulo \(2\pi\), says where in its cycle the voltage is when the particle crosses, as in chapter 9, section 1; the sine and its phase in radians are in F1, section 3. A particle crossing at a time when the RF phase is \(\phi\) gains

\[ \Delta W = q\hat{V}\sin\phi, \]

the largest gain at \(\phi = \pi/2\), the crest. Half a revolution later it crosses the gap in the opposite direction; the voltage has also reversed if half a revolution lasts half an RF period, that is if

\[ \omega_{RF} = h\,\omega_0, \qquad h = 1, 3, 5, \ldots \]

Why odd numbers only? Half a revolution lasts \(\pi/\omega_0 = h\pi/\omega_{RF}\), which is \(h\) half periods of the RF. An odd number of half periods turns \(\sin\) into \(-\sin\): the voltage has reversed, and so has the particle’s direction of crossing, so the gain is the same. An even number brings the voltage back to the same sign, and a particle going the other way across the gap is slowed by exactly what it gained.

The harmonic number \(h\) is 1 in the classical cyclotron, and \(h = 1\) from here on. The particle then gains \(2q\hat{V}\sin\phi\) a turn. The magnetic field that is in resonance for a slow particle is

\[ B_c = \frac{m\,\omega_{RF}}{q}. \]

A real number. Heilbron and Seidel give the 4-inch model’s first clear resonance (December 1930) with a 2.5 MHz oscillator near 3,300 gauss (0.33 T). At 0.33 T a slow proton circles at 5.03 MHz and an H\(_2^+\) ion at 2.51 MHz. The match identifies the ions as H\(_2^+\), as Heilbron and Seidel say: a known frequency and field weigh the ion.

3. The final energy

At the edge of the usable field, at radius \(R\), the momentum is \(p = qBR\), from \(r = p/(qB)\) of section 1. Non-relativistically \(W = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2\) and \(p = mv\), so \(W = p^2/2m\), and

\[ W = \frac{q^2 B^2 R^2}{2m}. \]

The voltage on the dees does not appear. The final energy is set by the magnet alone, by the product \(BR\); the voltage decides only how many turns it takes. This is why Lawrence’s programme was a programme of ever larger magnets. The relativistic form is \(W = \sqrt{(qBRc)^2 + (mc^2)^2} - mc^2\): the total energy is \(\sqrt{(pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2}\) (chapter 1, the four-momentum pre-requisite), and the kinetic energy is what is left after the rest energy is taken away.

Cyclotron builders compress this into the K-value. For an ion of mass number \(A\) (the number of protons and neutrons in its nucleus, F7, section 10) and charge state \(Q_i\) (the number of electrons it has lost, so \(q = Q_i e\)), the mass is \(m \approx A m_u\), with \(m_u = 1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\) kg the atomic mass unit. Putting these into \(W = q^2B^2R^2/2m\) and dividing by \(A\),

\[ \frac{W}{A} = K\left(\frac{Q_i}{A}\right)^2, \qquad K = \frac{(eBR)^2}{2m_u}, \]

with \(K\) in MeV. It is a property of the magnet. (The symbol \(Q_i\) is used only here, to keep it apart from the tunes.)

Worked numbers.

  • The 11-inch, January 1932. Lawrence and Livingston reported 1.22 MeV protons; Heilbron and Seidel place the collector at a radius of 11.5 cm and the oscillator at about 20 MHz. Then \(B\rho = 0.1597\) T m and \(B = 0.1597/0.115 = 1.39\) T, for which a slow proton circles at 21.2 MHz (wavelength 14.2 m). The non-relativistic formula gives 1.221 MeV against the exact 1.220 MeV: at \(\gamma - 1 = 0.0013\) the correction is negligible.
  • The 4-inch, January 1931. At its maximum field of 12.7 kG (1.27 T) an 80 keV H\(_2^+\) ion has a radius of 4.6 cm, which fits a chamber 4 inches (10 cm) across.
  • The 60-inch, 1939. For 16 MeV deuterons \(B\rho = 0.819\) T m. The pole radius is 0.762 m, so the field had to be at least 1.07 T, and more in practice since the beam leaves inside the pole edge. The same \(BR\) gives \(K \approx 32\) MeV, and alpha particles of 32.2 MeV (exercise 5).

4. The spiral: turns and turn separation

The picture. The particle gains the same energy each turn (if it keeps its phase), so its energy grows linearly with the turn number \(n\) and its radius as the square root. The turns crowd together at the outside.

The derivation. With \(\Delta W_{turn} = 2q\hat{V}\sin\phi\) and a constant \(\phi\), after \(n\) turns \(W_n = n\,\Delta W_{turn}\). Non-relativistically \(p = \sqrt{2mW}\), so \(r_n = p_n/(qB) = \sqrt{2mW_n}/(qB)\). The radius goes as the square root of the energy, and so as the square root of \(n\). Dividing \(r_n\) by the radius \(R\) reached after \(N\) turns, everything but \(\sqrt{n/N}\) cancels:

\[ r_n = R\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}, \qquad N = \frac{W_{final}}{2q\hat{V}\sin\phi}, \]

with \(N\) the number of turns to reach \(R\). Treating \(n\) as a smooth variable and differentiating (F1, section 2), \(dr/dn = R\,\tfrac{1}{2}n^{-1/2}N^{-1/2} = r/(2n)\). A step of one turn, \(\Delta n = 1\), is then \(\Delta r \approx r/(2n)\), and since \(W = n\,\Delta W_{turn}\), \(1/n = \Delta W_{turn}/W\):

\[ \Delta r = \frac{r}{2}\frac{\Delta W_{turn}}{W}, \qquad \Delta r(R) = \frac{R}{2N}. \]

The total path length is the sum of the circumferences, \(L = \sum 2\pi r_n\). Replacing the sum by an integral (fair when \(N\) is large; the ODE pre-requisite below checks it) and using \(\int_0^N n^{1/2}\,dn = \tfrac{2}{3}N^{3/2}\) (F1, section 7),

\[ L \approx 2\pi R \int_0^N \sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}\,dn = \frac{2\pi R}{\sqrt{N}}\cdot\frac{2}{3}N^{3/2} = \frac{4\pi}{3} R N , \]

and the time in the machine is \(N\) revolution periods, \(N/f_0\).

Figure 10.2. The 11-inch cyclotron’s spiral, from \(r_n = R\sqrt{n/N}\) with \(R = 11.5\) cm and \(N = 153\) turns of 8 keV. Top: the radius after \(n\) turns. Bottom: the step from one turn to the next (dots, logarithmic scale), 9.3 mm on the first turn and 0.38 mm at the collector; the dashed line is the smooth estimate \(R/(2\sqrt{nN})\), indistinguishable from the dots after the first few turns.

Worked numbers.

  • The 11-inch. With 4 kV the ratio \(W/(q\hat{V}) = 1.22\ \mathrm{MeV}/4\ \mathrm{keV} = 305\), the amplification of over 300 that Heilbron and Seidel record for that day. At most 8 keV a turn means at least 153 turns (\(1.22/0.008 = 152.5\)), a path of 73 m, 7.2 μs in the machine, and turns 0.38 mm apart at the collector. The 73 m is the length of the straight machine of the wall: the cyclotron is that machine rolled up.
  • The 4-inch. Heilbron and Seidel give 41 turns (82 gap crossings) to about 80 keV with under 1 kV on the dee, about 2 keV a turn.
  • The 60-inch. Heilbron and Seidel give about 200 kV across the dees in May 1939. Then up to 0.4 MeV a turn, 40 turns to 16 MeV, and the last turns 1.25% of the radius apart, 7.5 to 8.8 mm for an extraction radius of 0.6 to 0.7 m. Large dee voltage separates the turns, which makes extraction possible, and shortens the time the particle has to slip out of phase (section 8).

Pre-requisite (continued): Biot-Savart and Ampere’s law

Pre-requisite. Chapter 2 taught Ampère’s law as a statement about a closed path, \(\oint \mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \mu_0 I_{enc}\) (first met in F4, section 12), and used it on a coil. This section shrinks the path to a tiny rectangle, which turns the law into a statement about the field at a point: in the empty gap between the poles of a magnet the field has no curl. From that, and the symmetry of the poles, follows the small radial field above and below the median plane of a field that weakens outward. Section 5 uses it for the force that holds the beam to that plane, and section 6 turns the force into vertical focusing. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, section 3.1; Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 2, §43; Lee, chapter 2, sections I.2 and I.4; and Livingston and Blewett, section 6-3.

Coordinates. As in the chapter, use cylindrical coordinates about the axis of the poles: \(r\) the distance from the axis (m), \(\theta\) the angle round it, and \(y\) the height above the median plane, the plane midway between the poles. The field has a vertical component \(B_y\) and a radial component \(B_r\) (both in T). A partial derivative such as \(\partial B_y/\partial r\) means the rate at which \(B_y\) changes as you step outward, with \(y\) and \(\theta\) held fixed (F1, section 9; the cylindrical coordinates are those of F2, section 13, with the axis called \(y\) instead of \(z\)).

From a big loop to a small one. Ampère’s law holds for any closed path. Choose a very small rectangle standing upright at one value of \(\theta\), with sides \(\Delta r\) and \(\Delta y\) and its lower inner corner at \((r, y)\). Go round it: out along the bottom, up the outer side, in along the top, down the inner side. The four sides contribute

\[ \begin{aligned} \oint \mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{l} &\approx B_r(r, y)\,\Delta r + B_y(r+\Delta r, y)\,\Delta y - B_r(r, y+\Delta y)\,\Delta r - B_y(r, y)\,\Delta y \\ &\approx \left(\frac{\partial B_y}{\partial r} - \frac{\partial B_r}{\partial y}\right)\Delta r\,\Delta y . \end{aligned} \]

The second line uses \(f(u + \Delta u) - f(u) \approx (\partial f/\partial u)\,\Delta u\) for each pair of opposite sides, the first term of a Taylor series (F1, section 8). For example, the bottom and top sides together give \([B_r(r, y) - B_r(r, y + \Delta y)]\,\Delta r \approx -(\partial B_r/\partial y)\,\Delta y\,\Delta r\). Ampère’s law sets this equal to \(\mu_0\) times the current through the rectangle. Let current cross the rectangle with density \(j\) (A/m²), counted positive in the direction a right thumb points when the fingers curl the way we went round. That current is \(j\,\Delta r\,\Delta y\), and the area cancels:

\[ \frac{\partial B_y}{\partial r} - \frac{\partial B_r}{\partial y} = \mu_0\, j . \]

This is one component (the one round the axis, up to the sign set by the direction of going round) of the local form of Ampère’s law, \(\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{j}\) (Tong, section 3.1; Landau and Lifshitz, §43). The curl \(\nabla\times\mathbf{B}\) is the circulation of a field round a small loop per unit area of the loop, as F2, section 11 defines it; the step just taken is that definition worked out for one loop. The other components say similar things about other pairs of field components; this one is all the cyclotron needs. Because the rectangle stands flat in the \((r, y)\) plane, its sides are true lengths and no extra geometric factors appear.

In the gap of a cyclotron magnet there is no current. The coils are wound round the poles, outside the gap, and the beam’s own current, microamperes spread over the chamber, is negligible. So between the poles

\[ \frac{\partial B_r}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial B_y}{\partial r} . \]

A vertical field that changes with radius must come with a radial field that changes with height, at the same rate.

Symmetry fixes the rest. The magnet is built the same above and below its median plane. A field line that leaves the lower pole and arrives at the upper one must then be the mirror image of itself in the median plane. If it leans outward on the way up from the lower pole to the plane, it must lean inward on the way from the plane to the upper pole. So \(B_r\) has opposite signs above and below, and is zero on the plane itself. Expanding in \(y\) about the plane and keeping the first term,

\[ B_r(r, y) \approx y\,\frac{\partial B_r}{\partial y}\bigg|_{y=0} = y\,\frac{\partial B_y}{\partial r} . \]

This is the result section 5 uses. The approximation is good while \(y\) is small compared with the distance over which the field changes, which in a cyclotron is the pole radius.

The shape of a field line. Describe how fast the field falls with the dimensionless field index of section 6, \(n = -(r/B)\,\partial B/\partial r\), positive for a field that falls outward. Then \(B_r \approx -n B_y\,y/r\), and a field line, which runs along the field, has slope

\[ \frac{dr}{dy} = \frac{B_r}{B_y} = -\frac{n\,y}{r}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad r(y) \approx r_{mid} - \frac{n\,y^2}{2r}, \]

with \(r_{mid}\) the radius at which the line crosses the median plane. For \(n > 0\) the line is a parabola that reaches furthest out at the median plane: the lines bow outward like the staves of a barrel. For \(n = 0\) they are straight, and for \(n < 0\) (a field rising outward) they are pinched in at the median plane, like a waist.

The force. The magnetic force on the circulating particle is perpendicular both to its velocity, which runs round the axis, and to the field. In the median plane the field is vertical and the force points straight to the centre. Above the plane the field line leans inward (for \(n > 0\)), so it is tilted from the vertical by the small angle \(B_r/B_y = -ny/r\), and the force, which stays perpendicular to it, tilts by the same angle. A force that points to the centre and is tilted with the line points slightly downward, back to the plane. Its vertical part is the bending force \(qvB_y\) times the tilt:

\[ F_y \approx -\,qvB_y\,\frac{n\,y}{r} . \]

It is proportional to the height and opposite to it: a restoring force, which is what section 6 turns into an oscillation. If \(n = 0\) there is no tilt and no force, the uniform field of section 5. If \(n < 0\) the tilt reverses and the force pushes particles away from the plane.

Numbers: the 60-inch at the edge. Take 16 MeV deuterons, \(B\rho = 0.819\) T m, extracted near \(R = 0.65\) m, so \(B = 1.26\) T. In Rose’s field (section 6) the index at the edge is \(n = 0.0080\). At \(y = 5\) mm above the plane,

\[ B_r = n B\,\frac{y}{R} = 0.0080 \times 1.26 \times \frac{0.005}{0.65} = 7.8 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{T}, \]

78 μT, about one and a half times the Earth’s field, in a magnet of 1.26 T. The field lines there lean by \(6.2 \times 10^{-5}\) rad, and the restoring force is that fraction of the bending force. It is tiny, but it acts on every turn, and section 6 shows it is enough to make the particle oscillate about the plane once in eleven turns.

Exercises.

  1. A field map in the median plane gives 1.5000 T at \(r = 0.60\) m and 1.4990 T at \(r = 0.62\) m. Find \(\partial B_y/\partial r\), the field index at 0.61 m, and the radial field 10 mm above the plane. Which way does it point?
Answer

\(\partial B_y/\partial r = (1.4990 - 1.5000)/0.02 = -0.050\) T/m. The index is \(n = -(r/B)\,\partial B/\partial r = 0.61 \times 0.050/1.4995 = 0.020\). Then \(B_r = y\,\partial B_y/\partial r = 0.010 \times (-0.050) = -5.0 \times 10^{-4}\) T, 0.50 mT pointing inward above the plane, and outward 10 mm below it. The lines bow outward, and the force on the beam points back to the plane.

  1. A shim left in the wrong place makes the field rise by 0.1% between 0.30 m and 0.32 m, at about 1.5 T. What does it do to a particle 5 mm above the plane at 0.31 m?
Answer

\(\partial B_y/\partial r = +0.0015/0.02 = +0.075\) T/m, so \(n = -0.31 \times 0.075/1.5 = -0.016\). Now \(B_r = y\,\partial B_y/\partial r = +3.75 \times 10^{-4}\) T at \(y = 5\) mm: it points outward above the plane, the field lines are pinched in at the plane, and the force on the particle points away from it. Over the turns the particle spends in that region its height grows instead of oscillating (the next pre-requisite works out how fast).

  1. Ampère’s law gave the radial field. The other law of a static magnetic field, that as much flux leaves any closed surface as enters it (\(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0\), F4, section 13), tells how \(B_y\) changes with height. Apply it to a thin ring-shaped box between radii \(r\) and \(r + \Delta r\) and heights \(y\) and \(y + \Delta y\) to show that \(\partial B_y/\partial y = -(1/r)\,\partial(rB_r)/\partial r\). Then, for Rose’s field \(B_y(r, 0) = B_c(1 - a r^2/R^2)\) with \(a = 0.004\), find how much \(B_y\) changes between the median plane and 1 cm above it, at \(R = 0.65\) m.
Answer

Flux out through the outer wall minus in through the inner wall: \(2\pi(r+\Delta r)B_r(r+\Delta r)\Delta y - 2\pi r B_r(r)\Delta y \approx 2\pi\,\partial(rB_r)/\partial r\,\Delta r\,\Delta y\). Out through the top minus in through the bottom: \(2\pi r\,\Delta r\,[B_y(y+\Delta y) - B_y(y)] \approx 2\pi r\,(\partial B_y/\partial y)\,\Delta r\,\Delta y\). The sum is zero, which gives the result.

With \(B_r = y\,G(r)\) and \(G = \partial B_y/\partial r = -2aB_c r/R^2\), we have \(rG = -2aB_c r^2/R^2\) and \((1/r)\,d(rG)/dr = -4aB_c/R^2\). So \(\partial B_y/\partial y = 4aB_c\,y/R^2\), and integrating, \(B_y(r, y) = B_y(r, 0) + 2aB_c\,y^2/R^2\). At \(y = 1\) cm the change is \(2 \times 0.004 \times (0.01/0.65)^2 = 1.9 \times 10^{-6}\) of the field. The vertical field is the same at every height to two parts in a million; everything that matters for focusing is in the small radial component.

5. Why a uniform field loses the beam

The picture. In a perfectly uniform field nothing pushes a particle back towards the median plane between the poles. A particle that starts with a small vertical angle drifts steadily up or down and hits a dee. Along the 73 m path of the 11-inch, an angle of 1 mrad becomes 73 mm, larger than the space inside the dees. The horizontal motion is no better off: a particle whose orbit is not centred stays off centre, because every circle in a uniform field is an equilibrium.

What saves the beam is a field that weakens slightly with radius. The field lines between pole faces that open outward bow outward, like a barrel. Above the median plane the field then has a component pointing inward or outward, and the force \(q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\) from that component points back to the plane.

The field near the median plane. In the vacuum between the poles \(\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = 0\) (the pre-requisite above derives what follows step by step, and Figure 10.3 draws it). In cylindrical coordinates \((r, \theta, y)\) with the field symmetric about the plane \(y = 0\), the azimuthal component of the curl gives

\[ \frac{\partial B_r}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial B_y}{\partial r}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad B_r(r, y) \approx y\,\frac{\partial B_y}{\partial r} \]

to first order in \(y\). A field that falls with \(r\) has \(B_r\) of opposite sign above and below the plane.

Pre-requisite (continued): Ordinary differential equations (linear, constant and varying coefficients)

Pre-requisite. Chapter 2 taught the linear equation \(r'' = -K r\) and its solutions, first integrals and separation of variables; chapter 6 expanded an equation about a steady state. This section adds four tools: linearising about a reference motion rather than a state of rest; the equation \(\ddot{x} + Kx = 0\) for both signs of \(K\), with cyclotron numbers; the response to a constant extra force; and two ways of handling the turn count, removing it by dividing one rate by another and treating it as a continuous variable. Section 6 uses the first two, section 8 the last two, and section 10 the constant force. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 1, §21 and §22; Tong, Classical Mechanics, sections 2.1 and 5.3.2; and Lee, chapter 2, sections I.3 and I.4.

1. Linearising about a motion. In chapter 6 the reference was a voltage that did not change. In a cyclotron the reference is itself a motion: the circle \(r = r_0\), \(y = 0\), traversed at the angular frequency \(\omega_0\). The method is the same. Write the actual coordinate as the reference plus a small deviation, \(r = r_0 + x\); put it into the equation of motion; expand every function of \(r\) as a Taylor series about \(r_0\),

\[ f(r_0 + x) \approx f(r_0) + f'(r_0)\,x, \]

and drop the terms in \(x^2\), \(x\dot{x}\) and higher. The terms with no \(x\) in them cancel, because the reference motion solves the equation by itself. What remains is linear in \(x\), and linear equations can be solved exactly. (Tong, section 5.3.2, does this for a circular orbit in a central force, such as a planet’s; the cyclotron’s version is in section 6 of the chapter.)

Two expansions do all the work in section 6:

\[ \frac{1}{r_0 + x} = \frac{1}{r_0}\left(1 - \frac{x}{r_0} + \frac{x^2}{r_0^2} - \ldots\right), \qquad \left(\frac{r_0 + x}{r_0}\right)^{-n} = 1 - n\,\frac{x}{r_0} + \frac{n(n+1)}{2}\,\frac{x^2}{r_0^2} - \ldots \]

So does one observation about speed. A static magnetic field does no work, so the speed \(v\) is fixed. The radial and vertical velocities are first order in the deviation, and \(v_\theta = \sqrt{v^2 - v_r^2 - v_y^2}\) differs from \(v\) only at second order. To first order, the particle goes round at the full speed \(v\).

How good is this? For an oscillation of 5 mm about \(r_0 = 0.5\) m, \(x/r_0 = 0.01\), and the first neglected term in each expansion is a hundredth of the term kept. The linear force is right to about 1 per cent at the extreme of the swing, and better everywhere else.

2. The equation \(\ddot{x} + Kx = 0\), both signs. Try \(x = e^{st}\): then \(\ddot{x} = s^2e^{st}\), and the equation becomes \((s^2 + K)e^{st} = 0\), so \(s^2 = -K\). For \(K < 0\) the two roots \(s = \pm\sqrt{|K|}\) are real, and the solutions are a growing and a dying exponential. For \(K > 0\) the roots are imaginary, \(s = \pm i\sqrt{K}\), and Euler’s formula \(e^{i\alpha} = \cos\alpha + i\sin\alpha\) (F5, section 2) turns the pair into a cosine and a sine. For \(K < 0\) it is convenient to combine the two exponentials into the hyperbolic functions

\[ \cosh u = \frac{e^u + e^{-u}}{2}, \qquad \sinh u = \frac{e^u - e^{-u}}{2}, \]

which behave like cosine and sine at the start (\(\cosh 0 = 1\), \(\sinh 0 = 0\), and each is the derivative of the other) but grow as \(e^u/2\) instead of turning back. With \(x_0\) and \(\dot{x}_0\) the starting position and rate,

  • \(K > 0\): \(x = x_0\cos\sqrt{K}\,t + (\dot{x}_0/\sqrt{K})\sin\sqrt{K}\,t\). It oscillates and stays bounded.
  • \(K = 0\): \(x = x_0 + \dot{x}_0\,t\). It drifts.
  • \(K < 0\): \(x = x_0\cosh\sqrt{|K|}\,t + (\dot{x}_0/\sqrt{|K|})\sinh\sqrt{|K|}\,t\). It grows, in the end as \(e^{\sqrt{|K|}\,t}\).

Each line can be checked by differentiating twice. The sign of \(K\) decides everything: a restoring force, none, or a pushing-away force.

In section 6 the vertical equation is \(\ddot{y} + n\,\omega_0^2\,y = 0\), so \(K = n\omega_0^2\). Three cases, in numbers:

  • \(n = 0.008\), Rose’s field at the edge: \(y\) oscillates with angular frequency \(\sqrt{n}\,\omega_0\), once in \(1/\sqrt{0.008} = 11\) turns, and never exceeds its starting amplitude.
  • \(n = 0\), a uniform field: a particle that starts with a vertical angle of 1 mrad drifts 1 mm per metre of path, 73 mm over the 73 m of the 11-inch (section 5).
  • \(n = -0.01\), a field rising slightly outward: \(y\) grows by \(e^{2\pi\sqrt{0.01}} = 1.87\) every turn, a factor of \(8 \times 10^{10}\) in the 40 turns of the 60-inch. A micrometre becomes 80 km; in practice the particle hits a dee within a few turns.

3. A constant extra force. Add a constant acceleration \(a_0\) (m/s²) to an oscillator:

\[ \ddot{x} + \omega^2 x = a_0 . \]

A constant \(x_p = a_0/\omega^2\) solves it, since then \(\ddot{x}_p = 0\) and \(\omega^2 x_p = a_0\). Adding the free oscillation, which solves the equation with zero on the right, gives the general solution; for a particle that starts at rest at \(x = 0\),

\[ x(t) = \frac{a_0}{\omega^2}\left(1 - \cos\omega t\right). \]

The constant force does not change the frequency. It moves the centre of the oscillation to \(a_0/\omega^2\), and a particle that starts at the old centre swings between \(0\) and \(2a_0/\omega^2\) (Landau and Lifshitz, §22, where a constant force simply shifts the equilibrium). A weak spring makes the shift large: it goes as \(1/\omega^2\).

A median-plane error. If the two poles are not quite symmetric, a pole face slightly tilted or a shim on one side only, the field has a small radial part \(B_{r0}\) even on the median plane. Call \(b = B_{r0}/B_0\) the fractional error. It gives a constant vertical force \(qvB_{r0}\), and dividing by \(\gamma m\), with \(qB_0/(\gamma m) = \omega_0\) and \(v = \omega_0 r_0\),

\[ \ddot{y} + n\,\omega_0^2\,y = \omega_0^2\,r_0\,b \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad y_c = \frac{r_0\,b}{n} . \]

The beam then oscillates about a plane displaced by \(y_c\). With \(b = 10^{-4}\), a field tilted by a tenth of a milliradian, in a 60-inch-sized magnet (\(R = 0.65\) m) with Rose’s field:

\(r/R\) \(r\) (m) \(n\) \(y_c\) (mm) largest excursion (mm)
0.25 0.16 0.0005 32 65
0.5 0.33 0.0020 16 32
1.0 0.65 0.0080 8.1 16

Because the vertical focusing is so weak, a tilt of a tenth of a milliradian moves the beam’s plane by centimetres, most of all near the centre. This is the scale of care behind poles made parallel to thousandths of an inch.

The deflector. In section 10 an outward electric field \(E = f_{def}\,vB\) reduces the bending by a fraction \(f_{def}\). That is an outward acceleration \(qE/(\gamma m) = f_{def}\,\omega_0 v = f_{def}\,\omega_0^2\rho\), constant over the deflector, so the radial equation is \(\ddot{x} + Q_x^2\omega_0^2 x = f_{def}\,\omega_0^2\rho\), where \(Q_x^2 = 1 - n\) is the radial restoring term of section 6 and \(Q_x\) is close to 1. Measure time by the angle travelled, \(\theta = \omega_0 t\); then \(d^2x/d\theta^2 + Q_x^2 x = f_{def}\,\rho\), and with \(Q_x \approx 1\),

\[ x(\theta) \approx \rho\,f_{def}\,(1 - \cos\theta). \]

For 16 MeV deuterons (\(v = 3.89 \times 10^7\) m/s) in 1.26 T, \(vB = 4.9 \times 10^7\) V/m. An assumed deflector field of 6 MV/m (60 kV across 1 cm, an illustration, not a historical figure) gives \(f_{def} = 0.12\); with \(\rho = 0.65\) m the orbit moves out 40 mm after 60° of arc and 80 mm after a quarter turn, far more than the 8 mm between turns. Keeping \(Q_x = 0.996\) instead of 1 changes these by less than 0.2 per cent. The linear equation is the larger approximation here: with \(f_{def}\) as large as 0.12, the exact path in a uniform field, a circle of radius \(\rho/(1 - f_{def})\), moves out 41 mm and 85 mm.

4. Removing the turn count. Section 8 has two coupled first-order equations in the turn number \(n\):

\[ \frac{dW}{dn} = F(\phi), \qquad \frac{d\phi}{dn} = G(W). \]

(Here, as in sections 4 and 8, \(n\) is a turn number, not the field index.) The chain rule gives the rate of one with respect to the other, \(d\phi/dW = (d\phi/dn)/(dW/dn) = G(W)/F(\phi)\). The variables now separate:

\[ F(\phi)\,d\phi = G(W)\,dW, \qquad \int_{\phi_0}^{\phi} F\,d\phi = \int_0^{W} G\,dW . \]

The result is a relation between \(W\) and \(\phi\), the path of the particle in the plane of energy and phase, with the turn count gone. When the turn count is wanted back, it is a second integral along that path, \(n = \int dW/F(\phi(W))\).

The 11-inch. Protons gain \(F = 2q\hat{V}\sin\phi\) a turn, with \(q\hat{V} = 4\) keV, and slip \(G = 2\pi W/mc^2\) a turn in a uniform field set to the slow-proton resonance. Then \(\sin\phi\,d\phi = (\pi/q\hat{V}mc^2)\,W\,dW\), and starting at the crest, \(\phi_0 = 90°\),

\[ \cos\phi = \cos\phi_0 - \frac{\pi W^2}{2q\hat{V}mc^2} = -\frac{\pi \times 1.22^2}{2 \times 0.004 \times 938.27} = -0.623 , \]

so at 1.22 MeV the phase is \(128.5°\), 38.5° past the crest. (Section 8’s small-slip estimate, \(\pi W^2/(2q\hat{V}mc^2) = 0.62\) rad, is the same number read as an angle, 36°.) The turn count, from the second integral done numerically, is 159.7, against 152.5 if the phase had stayed at the crest.

5. The turn count as a continuous variable. The machine works in steps: the energy jumps at each crossing of the gap. Writing \(dW/dn\) replaces the difference \(W_{k+1} - W_k\) by a derivative. That is fair when each step is a small part of the whole and the quantities change little from one step to the next. A crossing-by-crossing calculation tests it. For the 11-inch above, the step-by-step answer is 160 turns and a final phase of 128.8°, against 159.7 turns and 128.5°. For deuterons with \(q\hat{V} = 50\) kV, starting at \(\phi_0 = 10°\) in a field tuned to the centre, the continuous formula gives 10.886 MeV when the phase reaches 180°, after 209.4 turns; the steps give 10.886 MeV after 209.5 turns.

The same idea turns sums into integrals. The 11-inch’s path is \(\sum_{k=1}^{N} 2\pi R\sqrt{k/N}\), which section 4 replaced by \(\tfrac{4\pi}{3}RN\). For \(N = 153\) and \(R = 0.115\) m the sum is 74.05 m and the integral 73.70 m, 0.5 per cent apart; for \(N = 40\), 1.8 per cent. The difference is about half of the last term, a relative error near \(3/(4N)\): the fewer the steps, the worse the continuous picture. It fails altogether on the first crossing or two, where one step is the whole of the energy.

Pre-requisite (continued): Simple and damped harmonic oscillator

Pre-requisite. Chapter 7 taught simple harmonic motion, first met in F3, section 9: a restoring force proportional to the displacement, the equation \(\ddot{x} + \omega^2 x = 0\) and its sinusoidal solution. This section adds the energy of an oscillation in terms of its amplitude, and the tune: an oscillation’s frequency counted in units of the revolution frequency, which is how section 6 states its result and how every circular machine after it is described. The next pre-requisite uses the energy to show why the amplitude shrinks. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 1, §21; Lee, chapter 2, section II.3 (the betatron tune); and Livingston and Blewett, section 6-3.

Energy and amplitude. Take a particle of mass \(M\) (kg) obeying \(\ddot{x} + \omega^2 x = 0\), with \(\omega\) in rad/s. Multiply the equation by \(M\dot{x}\) and notice that each term is a time derivative:

\[ M\dot{x}\ddot{x} + M\omega^2 x\dot{x} = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}M\dot{x}^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}M\omega^2 x^2\right) = 0 . \]

So the energy of the oscillation

\[ E = \tfrac{1}{2}M\dot{x}^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}M\omega^2 x^2 \]

stays constant (in J). At a turning point \(\dot{x} = 0\) and \(x = A\), the amplitude, so

\[ E = \tfrac{1}{2}M\omega^2 A^2 . \]

At the centre all of it is kinetic, and the velocity amplitude is \(\omega A\). With \(x = A\cos(\omega t + \psi)\), the average of \(\cos^2\) over a cycle is \(\tfrac{1}{2}\), so \(\langle x^2\rangle = A^2/2\): on average the energy is half kinetic and half potential.

In a cyclotron the oscillating particle has mass \(M = \gamma m\); the energy \(E\) is only the part in the transverse oscillation, not the particle’s kinetic energy \(W\).

Numbers: a vertical oscillation in the 60-inch. A 16 MeV deuteron at the edge of Rose’s field has \(Q_y = 0.0896\) (section 6) and revolution frequency \(\omega_0 = 5.99 \times 10^7\) rad/s, so its vertical angular frequency is \(\omega_y = Q_y\omega_0 = 5.36 \times 10^6\) rad/s. For an amplitude of 5 mm, with \(M = \gamma m_d = 3.37 \times 10^{-27}\) kg,

\[ E_y = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 3.37 \times 10^{-27} \times (5.36 \times 10^6)^2 \times (0.005)^2 = 1.2 \times 10^{-18}\ \mathrm{J} = 7.6\ \mathrm{eV}. \]

Seven and a half electronvolts of a 16 MeV particle are in its vertical motion. The vertical velocity amplitude is \(\omega_y A = 2.7 \times 10^4\) m/s against a forward speed of \(3.9 \times 10^7\) m/s, an angle of 0.69 mrad. The transverse motions are a tiny part of the whole, which is why the linear equations of section 6 work so well.

Counting in turns: the tune. In a circular machine the natural clock is the particle’s own revolution. Measure time by the angle the particle has travelled round the machine, \(\theta = \omega_0 t\) (rad). Then \(d/dt = \omega_0\,d/d\theta\), and \(\ddot{x} + \omega^2 x = 0\) becomes

\[ \frac{d^2x}{d\theta^2} + Q^2 x = 0, \qquad Q = \frac{\omega}{\omega_0}, \]

with solution \(x = A\cos(Q\theta + \psi)\). In one turn \(\theta\) grows by \(2\pi\) and the phase of the oscillation by \(2\pi Q\). So \(Q\) is the number of oscillations per turn, the tune, and \(1/Q\) the number of turns per oscillation. Along the orbit of radius \(r\) one oscillation stretches over a length \(2\pi r/Q\), its wavelength. Section 6 finds \(Q_x = \sqrt{1-n}\) and \(Q_y = \sqrt{n}\) (Lee, chapter 2, section II.3, writes the same number as \(\nu\) or \(Q\)).

Numbers. At the edge of Rose’s field, \(n = 0.0080\): \(Q_y = 0.0896\), so 11.2 turns per vertical oscillation, and at \(r = 0.65\) m a wavelength of \(2\pi \times 0.65/0.0896 = 46\) m. Radially \(Q_x = 0.9960\).

What a probe sees. A probe, or any detector at one place round the machine, sees each particle once a turn. If it records the displacement, the record is

\[ x_k = A\cos(2\pi Q k + \psi), \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots \]

Adding a whole number to \(Q\) adds a whole number of \(2\pi\) to each phase and changes nothing in the record. A probe sees only how far \(Q\) is from the nearest whole number.

That explains a remark in section 5. A radial oscillation with \(Q_x = 1\) exactly takes the particle out once and in once per turn: it is a circle of the same size with its centre shifted, an off-centre orbit. With \(Q_x\) slightly below 1, the probe sees the displacement change by only \(2\pi(1 - Q_x)\) a turn: the off-centre circle’s centre creeps slowly round the machine. At the edge of Rose’s field, \(1 - Q_x = 0.0040\), one circuit of the centre in 250 turns; at half radius, \(n = 0.0020\), one in 1000 turns. In a machine of 40 to 150 turns an orbit that starts off centre simply stays off centre. The vertical oscillation, by contrast, is slow enough to be seen directly: at the edge the probe sees it advance by \(360° \times 0.0896 = 32°\) a turn.

Pre-requisite (continued): Adiabatic invariants

Pre-requisite. Chapter 7 taught one adiabatic invariant, the magnetic moment of a gyrating particle, which stays constant while the field it moves in changes slowly. This section gives the general rule for any oscillator whose frequency changes slowly: its energy is not conserved, but its energy divided by its frequency, the action, is. From that follows the amplitude law \(A \propto \omega^{-1/2}\), the condition for it, and numbers showing where it holds in a cyclotron. Section 6 uses it for the vertical amplitude, which shrinks as \(n^{-1/4}\); chapter 11 uses it again for the betatron. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 1, §49; Tong, Classical Mechanics, section 10.6; and Livingston and Blewett, section 6-3.

The setting. An oscillator of fixed mass \(M\) obeys

\[ \ddot{x} + \omega(t)^2\,x = 0, \]

where the angular frequency \(\omega\) changes slowly with time. In a cyclotron, \(x\) is the height \(y\) above the median plane, and \(\omega = Q_y\omega_0 = \sqrt{n}\,\omega_0\) changes because the particle spirals outward into a region where the field index \(n\) is larger.

Energy is not conserved. Define the energy with the frequency of the moment, \(E = \tfrac{1}{2}M\dot{x}^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}M\omega^2x^2\). Its rate of change is

\[ \frac{dE}{dt} = M\dot{x}\ddot{x} + M\omega^2 x\dot{x} + M\omega\dot{\omega}\,x^2 = M\dot{x}\left(\ddot{x} + \omega^2 x\right) + M\omega\dot{\omega}\,x^2 = M\omega\dot{\omega}\,x^2 . \]

The first two terms cancel by the equation of motion, as in the previous pre-requisite, but the third, which comes from the changing spring, does not. As the spring stiffens, it does work on the oscillator.

Its slow average. If \(\omega\) changes little during one period, the motion over one period is very nearly \(x = A\cos(\omega t + \psi)\), and the average of \(x^2\) over that period is \(A^2/2 = E/(M\omega^2)\). The factor \(\dot{\omega}\) hardly changes within the period, so the average rate is

\[ \left\langle\frac{dE}{dt}\right\rangle = M\omega\dot{\omega}\,\frac{E}{M\omega^2} = \frac{\dot{\omega}}{\omega}\,E . \]

That is \(d(\ln E)/dt = d(\ln\omega)/dt\), so \(\ln E - \ln\omega\) does not change:

\[ \frac{E}{\omega} = \text{constant}. \]

The energy rises and falls in proportion to the frequency. The ratio \(E/\omega\) (J s) is the oscillator’s action; \(2\pi E/\omega\) is the area of the ellipse the motion traces in the plane of \(x\) and \(M\dot{x}\), whose semi-axes are \(A\) and \(M\omega A\). Landau and Lifshitz (§49) prove the general case, for any periodic motion, with the action written as \(\oint p\,dq/2\pi\); Tong (section 10.6) does the same. The harmonic oscillator is all the cyclotron needs.

The amplitude. With \(E = \tfrac{1}{2}M\omega^2A^2\), the invariant reads \(E/\omega = \tfrac{1}{2}M\omega A^2\), so

\[ A \propto (M\omega)^{-1/2} . \]

A stiffer spring gives a smaller swing, though more energy. In a cyclotron \(M = \gamma m\) changes by under 1 per cent and can be held fixed; \(\omega_0\) is constant, and \(\omega_y = \sqrt{n}\,\omega_0\), so

\[ A_y \propto n^{-1/4}, \]

the law of section 6. The same argument with the turn angle \(\theta\) in place of \(t\) and \(Q\) in place of \(\omega\) gives \(QA^2\) constant, which is the same statement.

When is a change slow? The averaging needs the frequency to change by a small fraction during one radian of the oscillation:

\[ \varepsilon = \frac{|\dot{\omega}|}{\omega^2} \ll 1 . \]

(The symbol \(\varepsilon\) is local to this section.) In Rose’s field \(Q_y \approx \sqrt{2a}\,r/R\), and \(r \propto \sqrt{k}\) with \(k\) the turn number, so \(Q_y \propto \sqrt{k}\). Then \(dQ_y/d\theta = Q_y/(4\pi k)\) and

\[ \varepsilon = \frac{1}{4\pi k\,Q_y} . \]

At half radius, \(k = N/4\) and \(Q_y = 0.0447\). For the 11-inch, \(N = 153\): \(\varepsilon = 0.046\), slow. For the 60-inch with 40 turns: \(\varepsilon = 0.18\), only marginally slow. At the edge of the 11-inch, \(\varepsilon = 0.006\).

Testing it. Integrate \(d^2y/d\theta^2 + Q_y(\theta)^2\,y = 0\) numerically from half radius to the edge, with \(Q_y\) following Rose’s field turn by turn, for starting phases spread over half a cycle. The invariant predicts that the amplitude at the edge is \((0.0080/0.0020)^{-1/4} = 0.707\) of its value at half radius.

machine turns \(N\) vertical oscillations from \(R/2\) to \(R\) \(\varepsilon\) at \(R/2\) amplitude ratio found action held to
11-inch 153 8.0 0.046 0.700 to 0.714 ±2%
60-inch 40 2.1 0.18 0.679 to 0.735 ±8%

Where the change is slow the law is accurate, whatever the starting phase. Where it is only marginally slow, the law still gives the average, but the result scatters with the phase. Near the centre \(Q_y \to 0\) and \(\varepsilon\) grows without limit: there is no slow oscillation to average, and the electric focusing of section 7 takes over anyway.

6. Weak focusing: the field index and the betatron frequencies

The field index. Describe the fall of the field near the reference radius \(r_0\) by

\[ B_y(r, 0) = B_0\left(\frac{r}{r_0}\right)^{-n}, \qquad n = -\frac{r}{B}\frac{\partial B}{\partial r}, \]

with \(B_0\) the field at \(r_0\) and \(n\) the dimensionless field index, positive for a falling field.

The equations of motion. Let the particle circulate with speed \(v\) in the direction that makes the magnetic force on it point inward; that direction depends on the sign of \(q\) and of \(B\), and only magnitudes matter below. The reference orbit is the circle \(r = r_0\), \(y = 0\), with \(\gamma m v^2/r_0 = qvB_0\) and angular frequency \(\omega_0 = v/r_0 = qB_0/(\gamma m)\). Write \(r = r_0 + x\) and keep terms of first order in \(x\), \(y\) and their rates. The speed is constant in a static magnetic field, and the azimuthal velocity equals \(v\) to first order.

Radial. The radial equation is

\[ \gamma m\left(\ddot{r} - \frac{v_\theta^2}{r}\right) = -qvB_y(r). \]

Read it as Newton’s second law along the radius. The right side is the magnetic force, which points inward (hence the minus sign, with \(r\) counted outward). On the left, \(\ddot{r}\) is the rate at which the outward speed changes, and \(v_\theta^2/r\) is the centripetal acceleration of F2, section 4: a particle going round at speed \(v_\theta\) at radius \(r\) needs that much inward acceleration just to keep the same radius. Only the inward force beyond that amount pulls the radius in, so the radial acceleration is \(\ddot{r} = -qvB_y/(\gamma m) + v_\theta^2/r\), which is the equation above. (The same split of the velocity into an outward part \(\dot{r}\) and a sideways part \(v_\theta = r\dot{\theta}\) is the one F3, section 8 uses for orbits; the outward term \(mv_\theta^2/r\) is the one that appears there as \(L^2/(mr^3)\), from the slope of \(L^2/(2mr^2)\).) On the reference circle, \(\ddot{r} = 0\) and the two terms balance.

To first order \(v_\theta^2/r \approx (v^2/r_0)(1 - x/r_0)\) and \(B_y \approx B_0(1 - n x/r_0)\), the two expansions of the ODE pre-requisite with \(v_\theta = v\), and \(\ddot{r} = \ddot{x}\) because \(r_0\) is fixed. The zero-order terms cancel by the definition of \(r_0\), and what remains is

\[ \gamma m\,\ddot{x} = -\frac{\gamma m v^2}{r_0^2}\,x + \frac{qvB_0}{r_0}\,n\,x = -\frac{\gamma m v^2}{r_0^2}(1 - n)\,x, \]

using \(qvB_0 = \gamma m v^2/r_0\). So

\[ \ddot{x} + (1 - n)\,\omega_0^2\,x = 0. \]

The first term, \(1\), is the geometric focusing of any circle: a particle displaced outward with the same speed follows a circle of the same size, which crosses the reference circle twice a turn. The term \(-n\) is a defocusing correction: outside, the weaker field bends less.

Vertical. The vertical force comes from \(B_r = y\,\partial B_y/\partial r = -nB_0\,y/r_0\); with the direction of circulation fixed as above it is restoring when \(n > 0\):

\[ \gamma m\,\ddot{y} = qv\,y\frac{\partial B_y}{\partial r} = -\frac{qvB_0 n}{r_0}\,y = -\frac{\gamma m v^2}{r_0^2}\,n\,y \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot{y} + n\,\omega_0^2\,y = 0. \]

The result. Both motions are simple harmonic about the reference orbit (Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 1, §21). Their frequencies in units of the revolution frequency, the tunes, are

\[ Q_x = \sqrt{1 - n}, \qquad Q_y = \sqrt{n}, \qquad Q_x^2 + Q_y^2 = 1. \]

Both are real only if

\[ 0 < n < 1. \]

For \(n < 0\) (field rising outward) the vertical motion grows exponentially; for \(n > 1\) the radial motion does. These are the betatron oscillations, named after the machine for which Kerst and Serber worked them out in full in 1941 (chapter 11). In the cyclotron literature of 1938 Rose and Wilson derived the vertical frequency \(\sqrt{n}\); Craddock remarks that radial motion was apparently of no concern to them. The general theory of transverse optics is in chapter 16.

Approximations. Linear in the displacements; \(n\) taken as constant over the oscillation; the field static. All three hold well in a cyclotron, where amplitudes are millimetres and the energy changes little in one oscillation.

A real field. Rose (1938) gave as one satisfactory field shape one that varies as \(1 - 4\times10^{-3}(r/R)^2\), with \(R\) the radius of the exit. Then \(n = 2a\xi^2/(1 - a\xi^2)\) with \(a = 0.004\) and \(\xi = r/R\):

\(r/R\) \(n\) \(Q_y\) turns per vertical oscillation \(Q_x\)
0.25 0.0005 0.022 45 0.9997
0.5 0.0020 0.045 22 0.9990

The vertical focusing is very weak: a particle takes 11 turns, at the edge, to complete one vertical oscillation, and at the centre the magnetic focusing vanishes altogether. A fall of 0.4% across the whole magnet is all it takes, and all the resonance can afford (section 8).

The amplitude shrinks as the focusing grows. The vertical oscillation changes slowly compared with its own period, so its action is an adiabatic invariant (Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 1, §49). For an oscillator of mass \(M\) and angular frequency \(\omega\) the invariant is \(E/\omega = \tfrac{1}{2}M\omega A^2\), with \(A\) the amplitude. Here \(M = \gamma m \approx m\) and \(\omega = Q_y\omega_0\), so \(A \propto Q_y^{-1/2} = n^{-1/4}\). Wilson (1938) found exactly this dependence, \((-\tfrac{r}{H}\tfrac{\partial H}{\partial r})^{-1/4}\) in his notation, for ions in phase, and Livingston and Blewett (section 6-3) use the same law, amplitudes varying roughly as \(n^{-1/4}\). In Rose’s field the amplitude at the edge is 0.71 of its value at half radius. (Chapter 11 works the adiabatic invariant through in full, for a betatron whose field rises in time.)

Pre-requisite (continued): Gauss’s law and potentials of simple shapes

Pre-requisite. Chapter 1 taught Gauss’s law (first met in F4, section 4), and chapter 2 the uniform field \(E = V/d\) between parallel plates and the fringing of that field at the plates’ edges. This section asks what Gauss’s law says about a field that changes along the beam’s direction. In empty space such a field must also have a part across the beam, proportional to the distance from the axis, and that part acts as a lens. It derives the focal length of an aperture lens, \(1/f = q(E_2 - E_1)/(4W)\), which section 7 quotes, and the stronger lens of a long slit. The magnetic twin of the argument, \(B_r \approx -(r/2)\,dB_z/dz\), appeared in chapter 2. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, section 2.1; Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 2, §36; and Livingston and Blewett, section 5-3.

The idea. Field lines begin and end only on charges. In a region with no charge, if the field along the axis is stronger a little further on, more lines leave through the far end of any small region than entered through the near end. The extra lines must have come in through the sides. So the lines converge towards the axis where the axial field grows, and diverge where it falls, and the field has a component across the axis.

A round hole. Let the axis be \(z\), the direction of the beam, and let \(E_z(z)\) (V/m) be the field on it. Draw a small closed cylinder of radius \(r\) and length \(\Delta z\) around the axis, in a region with no charge. (A beam’s own charge is neglected here; chapter 7 deals with it.) Gauss’s law says the total flux out of it is zero. Out through the far end minus in through the near end: \([E_z(z + \Delta z) - E_z(z)]\,\pi r^2 \approx (dE_z/dz)\,\Delta z\,\pi r^2\), taking \(E_z\) as uniform across a small end face. Out through the curved side: \(E_r\,2\pi r\,\Delta z\). Setting the sum to zero,

\[ E_r = -\frac{r}{2}\,\frac{dE_z}{dz} . \]

The transverse field grows in proportion to the distance from the axis, the property that makes a lens: a ray twice as far out gets twice the kick.

A long slit. If the opening is a long slot, as the gap between two dees is, the field does not depend on the coordinate along the slot. Take a box of width \(\Delta x\) along the slot, length \(\Delta z\), reaching from \(-y\) to \(+y\). The ends give \((dE_z/dz)\,\Delta z\,2y\,\Delta x\); the top and bottom faces, by symmetry, give \(2E_y\,\Delta x\,\Delta z\); nothing crosses the faces along the slot. So

\[ E_y = -\,y\,\frac{dE_z}{dz} , \]

twice the round-hole value at the same distance: the extra lines can come in only from above and below, not from all round.

The lens. A particle of charge \(q\) (C), mass \(m\) and kinetic energy \(W\) (J) moves along \(z\) at speed \(v\), a small distance \(r\) off the axis. Where \(E_z\) changes from \(E_1\) to \(E_2\), its transverse momentum changes by

\[ \Delta p_r = \int qE_r\,dt = \int qE_r\,\frac{dz}{v} = -\frac{qr}{2v}\int\frac{dE_z}{dz}\,dz = -\frac{qr}{2v}\,(E_2 - E_1). \]

Two approximations are made: the region where \(E_z\) changes is short enough that \(r\) and \(v\) hardly change across it (a thin lens), and the angles are small. The slope of the path changes by \(\Delta r' = \Delta p_r/p\). Non-relativistically \(pv = mv^2 = 2W\), so

\[ \Delta r' = -\frac{q\,(E_2 - E_1)}{4W}\,r, \qquad \frac{1}{f} = \frac{q\,(E_2 - E_1)}{4W} , \]

since a lens of focal length \(f\) (m) turns a ray parallel to the axis at distance \(r\) by \(-r/f\). For a slit the same steps give \(1/f = q(E_2 - E_1)/(2W)\). Here \(E_1\) and \(E_2\) are counted positive when they accelerate the particle, and \(W\) is its energy at the opening. If the field beyond is the stronger accelerating field, \(f > 0\) and the lens converges; if the field beyond is weaker, it diverges (Livingston and Blewett, section 5-3, Eq. 5-20). For a fast particle replace \(2W\) by \(pv\). In practical units, with \(W\) in eV and \(q = e\): \(1/f = (E_2 - E_1)/(4W)\) with \(E\) in V/m.

At the cyclotron gap. The particle enters the gap from the field-free inside of a dee, \(E_1 = 0\), \(E_2 = E\): a converging lens of strength \(qE/(4W_1)\). It leaves into the other dee, \(E_1 = E\), \(E_2 = 0\): a diverging lens of strength \(qE/(4W_2)\). It has gained energy in between, \(W_2 > W_1\), so the diverging lens is the weaker one. Two thin lenses close together add their strengths:

\[ \frac{1}{f} \approx \frac{qE}{4W_1} - \frac{qE}{4W_2} = \frac{qE\,(W_2 - W_1)}{4W_1W_2} , \]

the formula of section 7. This is the velocity effect alone, with the field taken as constant while the particle crosses.

Numbers: the 11-inch. Take 4 kV across the gap and, as an assumption for illustration, a gap of 1 cm, so \(E = 4 \times 10^5\) V/m, and a gain of 4 keV at every crossing.

Vertically the particle obeys \(y'' + (n/r^2)\,y = 0\) along its path, so the half turn of length \(\pi r\) between two crossings acts as a lens of strength \(1/f = (n/r^2)\,\pi r = n\pi/r\). 7. Electric focusing at the gaps

The picture. Near the centre the magnetic focusing is nil, and the beam survives there by the electric field of the gap. The field lines of the gap bulge into the openings of the dees. On the way in, the field converges towards the median plane and pushes a particle back towards it; on the way out it diverges and pushes it away. Figure 10.5 draws it. Two effects make the sum focusing:

  1. The velocity effect. The particle leaves the gap faster than it entered, so the defocusing exit field acts for a shorter time and with less effect on its direction.
  2. The time effect. If the voltage is falling while the particle crosses (\(\phi\) past the crest, \(\phi > \pi/2\) in the convention of section 2), the exit field is weaker than the entrance field, which adds focusing. If it is rising, the exit field is stronger, and the gap defocuses.

Figure 10.5. The gap between two dees, cut through the median plane, at an instant when the left dee is positive; schematic. Field lines leave the metal at right angles. Those that start on the inner faces of the left dee’s opening bend in towards the median plane as they enter the gap, and bend out again into the right dee’s opening. A positive ion a little above the plane is pushed back towards it on the way in and away from it on the way out. The outward push is the smaller, because the ion is faster by then (the velocity effect) and, if the voltage is falling, because the field is weaker (the time effect).

A rough measure. An aperture between a region of field \(E_1\) and a region of field \(E_2\) acts on a slow particle of kinetic energy \(W\) as a thin lens of focal length \(f_{lens}\) with \(1/f_{lens} = q(E_2 - E_1)/(4W)\), with the sign chosen so that entering an accelerating field focuses. This is the Davisson and Calbick aperture-lens formula, derived in the pre-requisite just before this section; Livingston and Blewett derive it in section 5-3, Eq. (5-20), as \(1/f = (E_2 - E_1)/(4T)\) with \(T\) the kinetic energy in volts, converging when the second field is the stronger, and Humphries, chapter 6, derives the electrostatic lenses. Treat the gap as two apertures, a field \(E\) inside, energies \(W_1\) before and \(W_2\) after:

\[ \frac{1}{f_{lens}} \approx \frac{qE}{4}\left(\frac{1}{W_1} - \frac{1}{W_2}\right) = \frac{qE\,(W_2 - W_1)}{4W_1W_2}. \]

The difference \(W_2 - W_1\) is one crossing’s gain, so for a fixed voltage the strength falls roughly as \(1/W^2\). With a gain \(\Delta W\) at every crossing, \(W_1 = (k-1)\Delta W\) and \(W_2 = k\Delta W\) at crossing \(k\), so the strength goes as \(1/[k(k-1)]\): by the tenth crossing it is 1/45 of its value at the second. (At the first crossing, from rest, the formula diverges; the thin-lens picture fails there.) Electric focusing is strong only for the first few turns.

What Rose and Wilson found. Rose (Cornell) and Wilson (Berkeley) analysed the problem in papers printed one after the other in Physical Review in 1938. Rose found electric focusing dominant in the first part of the path and magnetic focusing beyond, the boundary at about half the exit radius; a falling RF voltage gives slow oscillations about the median plane, a rising one defocuses unless the ions cross very near the peak. Livingston and Blewett (section 6-3) summarise Rose’s electric focusing alone as long-period oscillations inside an envelope that diverges slowly, as \(T^{1/4}\) in the kinetic energy, that is as \(r^{1/2}\). Wilson found that at the lowest energies the gap defocuses, that higher up it focuses but weakly, with vertical amplitudes growing as \(r^{3/4}\), and that magnetic focusing then takes over. He checked the beam’s width at several radii by the radioactivity induced in a probe, and found agreement within one or two millimetres.

8. The conflict: phase slip

The picture. Vertical focusing needs a field that falls with radius; a falling field means a lower revolution frequency at large radius. The relativistic mass increase lowers the revolution frequency too. The oscillator’s frequency is fixed. So as the particle spirals out it takes a little longer each turn than one RF period, arrives a little later at the gap each time, and its phase \(\phi\) creeps upward. Once it passes \(\phi = \pi\) the gap decelerates it. The design problem is to arrange that the particle reaches the edge before that.

The slip per turn. A turn lasts \(T_{rev} = 2\pi/\omega_0 = 2\pi\gamma m/(qB(r))\), and the RF period is \(T_{RF} = 2\pi/\omega_{RF} = 2\pi m/(qB_c)\). The phase at the next crossing of the same gap advances by \(\omega_{RF}(T_{rev} - T_{RF})\): if the turn takes longer than one RF period, the particle arrives late by the difference, and the RF phase runs on by \(\omega_{RF}\) times that delay. Since \(\omega_{RF}T_{RF} = 2\pi\),

\[ \Delta\phi_{turn} = 2\pi\left(\frac{T_{rev}}{T_{RF}} - 1\right) = 2\pi\left(\frac{\gamma B_c}{B(r)} - 1\right), \]

where the ratio of the periods is \([2\pi\gamma m/(qB)]/[2\pi m/(qB_c)] = \gamma B_c/B\).

Write the field as \(B(r) = B_c[1 + \varepsilon_c - \delta_B(r)]\), where \(\varepsilon_c\) is the fractional excess over resonance at the centre (positive if the field is set high) and \(\delta_B(r)\) the fractional fall towards radius \(r\). With \(\gamma = 1 + W/mc^2\), the ratio is \((1 + W/mc^2)/(1 + \varepsilon_c - \delta_B)\). Every correction is a fraction of a per cent, so use \(1/(1 + u) \approx 1 - u\) for small \(u\) (F1, section 8) and drop products of two small quantities:

\[ \frac{\gamma B_c}{B} \approx \left(1 + \frac{W}{mc^2}\right)(1 - \varepsilon_c + \delta_B) \approx 1 + \frac{W}{mc^2} - \varepsilon_c + \delta_B , \]

so

\[ \Delta\phi_{turn} \approx 2\pi\left(\frac{W}{mc^2} - \varepsilon_c + \delta_B(r)\right). \]

The mass increase and the field fall enter in the same way. (The symbols \(\varepsilon_c\) and \(\delta_B\) are local to this section; they are not an emittance or a momentum deviation.)

Numbers. At 16 MeV a deuteron is 0.85% heavier than at rest and slips 3.1° a turn in a field tuned to the centre; at 8 MeV, 1.5°. In Rose’s field the fall at the edge is 0.4%, about half the mass effect at 16 MeV. For 1.22 MeV protons the mass effect is only 0.13%, 0.47° a turn.

The energy limit, field tuned to the centre. Treat the turn number \(n\) as continuous and set \(\varepsilon_c = \delta_B = 0\):

\[ \frac{dW}{dn} = 2q\hat{V}\sin\phi, \qquad \frac{d\phi}{dn} = \frac{2\pi W}{mc^2}. \]

Divide the second by the first to remove \(n\) (item 4 of the ODE pre-requisite): \(d\phi/dW = (2\pi W/mc^2)/(2q\hat{V}\sin\phi)\), and multiplying across,

\[ \sin\phi\,d\phi = \frac{\pi}{q\hat{V}mc^2}\,W\,dW . \]

Integrate both sides from the start, \(\phi_0\) and \(W = 0\), to \(\phi\) and \(W\). The integral of \(\sin\phi\) is \(-\cos\phi\), and that of \(W\) is \(W^2/2\):

\[ \cos\phi_0 - \cos\phi = \frac{\pi W^2}{2q\hat{V}mc^2}, \]

with \(\phi_0\) the starting phase. The particle gains energy while \(0 < \phi < \pi\), so the left side can be at most 2, reached by starting just after \(\phi = 0\) and ending at \(\phi = \pi\):

\[ W_{max} = 2\sqrt{\frac{q\hat{V}mc^2}{\pi}}. \]

(Set the left side to 2 and solve for \(W\): \(W^2 = 4q\hat{V}mc^2/\pi\).) The limit grows only as the square root of the voltage and of the rest energy. Heavier ions and higher voltages go further.

The same formula gives the total slip of a particle that stays near the crest: \(\Delta\phi_{total} \approx \pi W^2/(2q\hat{V}mc^2)\). For the 60-inch, 16 MeV deuterons with 0.4 MeV a turn slip 1.07 rad (61°) in all; for the 11-inch, 1.22 MeV protons at 8 keV a turn slip 0.62 rad (36°). Both fit inside half a cycle.

Setting the field high at the centre. If the field at the centre is set above resonance, \(\varepsilon_c > 0\), the particle at first arrives earlier each turn, its phase falls, and only after \(W/mc^2\) exceeds \(\varepsilon_c\) does it begin to rise. The same integration with \(\varepsilon_c\) kept, \(d\phi/dn = 2\pi(W/mc^2 - \varepsilon_c)\), gives

\[ \cos\phi_0 - \cos\phi = \frac{\pi}{q\hat{V}}\left(\frac{W^2}{2mc^2} - \varepsilon_c W\right) = \frac{\pi}{q\hat{V}}\cdot\frac{(W - \varepsilon_c mc^2)^2 - \varepsilon_c^2 (mc^2)^2}{2mc^2}, \]

where the second form completes the square. Rearranged,

\[ \frac{(W - \varepsilon_c mc^2)^2}{2mc^2} = \frac{q\hat{V}}{\pi}\left(\cos\phi_0 - \cos\phi\right) + \frac{\varepsilon_c^2 mc^2}{2}. \]

Starting at the crest, \(\phi_0 = \pi/2\), the phase must not fall below zero at its turning point \(W = \varepsilon_c mc^2\), where the left side is zero. There the equation reads \((q\hat{V}/\pi)(\cos\phi_0 - \cos\phi_{min}) = -\varepsilon_c^2 mc^2/2\); with \(\cos\phi_0 = 0\) and \(\cos\phi_{min}\) at most 1, this allows at most \(\varepsilon_c^2 mc^2/2 = q\hat{V}/\pi\), that is \(\varepsilon_c = \sqrt{2q\hat{V}/(\pi mc^2)}\). Put that \(\varepsilon_c\) and \(\cos\phi = -1\) back in: \((W - \varepsilon_c mc^2)^2 = 2mc^2(q\hat{V}/\pi + q\hat{V}/\pi) = 4q\hat{V}mc^2/\pi\), so \(W - \varepsilon_c mc^2 = 2\sqrt{q\hat{V}mc^2/\pi}\), while \(\varepsilon_c mc^2 = \sqrt{2}\sqrt{q\hat{V}mc^2/\pi}\). The particle therefore reaches \(\phi = \pi\) at

\[ W_{max} = (2 + \sqrt{2})\sqrt{\frac{q\hat{V}mc^2}{\pi}}. \]

For deuterons at 50 kV that is 18.7 MeV with the centre 0.41% above resonance.

Starting at the crest is not the best choice. For a general \(\phi_0\) the turning-point condition reads \(\varepsilon_c^2 mc^2/2 \le (q\hat{V}/\pi)(1 - \cos\phi_0)\), and putting it into the equation above at \(\phi = \pi\) gives \(W - \varepsilon_c mc^2 \le 2\sqrt{q\hat{V}mc^2/\pi}\) whatever \(\phi_0\) is. The offset itself can be largest when the particle starts just short of \(\phi_0 = \pi\), where \(\varepsilon_c mc^2\) can reach \(2\sqrt{q\hat{V}mc^2/\pi}\). The particle then sweeps the whole accelerating half cycle, from near \(\pi\) down to near \(0\) and back, and the bound is

\[ W_{max} = 4\sqrt{\frac{q\hat{V}mc^2}{\pi}}, \]

twice the value for a field tuned to the centre: 21.9 MeV for deuterons at 50 kV, with the centre 0.58% above resonance. Figure 10.6 draws the phase histories. It is approached but not reached, because a particle that starts near \(\phi = \pi\) gains almost nothing on its first turns. A step-by-step calculation (crossing by crossing, best starting phase and best \(\varepsilon_c\)) gives 21.8 MeV with a uniform field (centre 0.58% high, starting at about 166°) and 18.4 MeV with Rose’s 0.4% fall, taking \(R\) as the radius at which that energy is reached; the fall needed for focusing costs about 3.4 MeV here.

What Bethe and Rose said. Hans Bethe and Morris Rose, at Cornell, published this conflict in a letter of December 1937. Craddock summarises their conclusion as a deuteron limit of about \(8\sqrt{V(\mathrm{kV})/50}\) MeV. Rose’s longer paper of March 1938 went further: a field 0.9% above resonance at the centre and falling as \(1 - 4\times10^{-3}(r/R)^2\) gave, with a 50 kV peak dee voltage, protons of 13.9 MeV, deuterons of 19.7 MeV and alpha particles of 39.4 MeV, rising as the square root of the voltage; a more complicated field gave about 7% more (the 15, 21 and 42 MeV that Livingston and Blewett quote, section 6-12); the absolute maximum was about 5% higher still and needed extremely careful shimming. The proportions 1 : \(\sqrt{2}\) : \(2\sqrt{2}\) for protons, deuterons and alphas (13.9 : 19.7 : 39.4) are exactly those of \(\sqrt{q\hat{V}mc^2}\), since \(qm\) goes as 1 : 2 : 8. The simple model and Rose’s figures agree in scale, not in detail; the papers define the dee voltage differently (Rose writes \(2V_0\)), and the letter of Bethe and Rose itself was not consulted for this chapter. Rose drew the practical moral: the compromise between phase and focusing is why the beam was so sensitive to shimming.

9. How sharp the resonance is

The picture. If the field is wrong by a fraction \(\varepsilon\) (a fractional field error, as in section 8, not an emittance), the particle slips \(2\pi\varepsilon\) a turn, and over \(N\) turns it slips \(2\pi\varepsilon N\). It must not slip more than half a cycle. The more turns a machine needs, the more precisely its field must be set.

The estimate. Ignore the mass increase and let the phase start at one edge of the accelerating half cycle. Then \(2\pi|\varepsilon|N < \pi\), or

\[ |\varepsilon| < \frac{1}{2N}, \]

a full window of at most \(1/N\) in field. The particle must also gain enough energy on the way, which narrows the window further. A turn-by-turn calculation for the 11-inch (protons to 11.5 cm, 4 kV, uniform field, starting phases spread over the half cycle) finds beam at the collector only for field offsets between \(-0.14\%\) and \(+0.27\%\), a full width of 0.42%, or \(0.64/N\) with \(N = 153\). For the 4-inch (H\(_2^+\) to 80 keV, 1 kV, 41 turns) the window is \(-0.79\%\) to \(+0.80\%\), a full width of 1.59%, or \(0.65/N\). The 11-inch window is not centred on zero because the mass increase, small as it is (0.13% at the collector), shifts it toward a higher field; at 80 keV the mass increase of the 4-inch is negligible and its window is almost centred.

So the field has to be uniform, or shaped, to a few parts in a thousand over the whole pole, and the magnet current held to the same precision. This is the physics behind shimming (At the bench).

10. The ion source and extraction

At the centre. The ions must start at the centre, inside the magnet. In the 4-inch, Heilbron and Seidel describe hydrogen molecular ions made by firing electrons from a filament into hydrogen gas at the centre of the chamber; later machines used an arc between a hot cathode and an anode in a small chamber of gas, open to the gap (chapter 7). On the first turn the radius is small: 9 mm for 8 keV protons at 1.39 T (the 11-inch), 9.2 cm for 0.4 MeV deuterons at about 1.4 T. The source and its housing must not stand in the way of the first turns.

Extraction. At the edge the beam is on a nearly circular orbit, and the turns are separated by millimetres or less. An electrostatic deflector pulls it out: a curved electrode at high negative voltage (for positive ions) facing a thin grounded plate, the septum, placed between the last turn and the one before. Inside the deflector the outward electric force \(qE\) subtracts from the inward magnetic force, so the orbit straightens.

To reduce the curvature \(1/\rho\) by a fraction \(f_{def}\), the field must be \(E = f_{def}\,vB\). Because the radial tune is close to 1, a constant extra curvature \(f_{def}/\rho\) applied over an arc \(\theta\) displaces the orbit outward by \(x = \rho f_{def}(1 - \cos\theta)\), that is \(\rho f_{def}\) over a quarter turn. The deflector must therefore hold a strong field in vacuum across a gap of millimetres, in a place where stray ions strike it (chapter 4). The 4-inch model already had a deflector (Craddock’s figure 2), and by 1936 Lawrence and Cooksey described the apparatus that delivered deuterons of up to 6.3 MeV.

Limits of the model. Real orbit centres are displaced and wander, and the field near the edge falls faster than any simple law; design codes track these.

How it was researched and built

The 4-inch model, 1930 to 1931

Livingston began on 20 September 1930 with a new brass model, a chamber 4 inches (10 cm) across, in a magnet that gave at most 5,500 gauss. Through October and November he saw only feeble signs of resonance; Craddock gives evidence of magnetic resonance in November and 13 keV ions. Heilbron and Seidel date the first clear success to 1 December 1930, when the dependence of the resonant field on the dee voltage, which had confused earlier runs, was gone. Over Christmas Livingston borrowed a stronger magnet, of almost 13 kG. On 2 January 1931 he had resonance at 12.4 kG for a calculated 70 keV; at the magnet’s maximum, 12.7 kG, and with under 1,000 V on the dee, the ions reached about 80 keV in 41 turns. The pressure was about \(10^{-5}\) mm of mercury (1.3 mPa), and Livingston assumed that fewer than one ion in a thousand completed the spiral. The energy was not measured directly: collimating the beam tightly enough to measure it by electrostatic deflection left too little current to read, so it was inferred from the field and the radius. Livingston’s own later account (Livingston and Blewett, section 6-1) gives 2,000 V of radio frequency on the electrode and says the 80 keV was confirmed by electric deflection; the notebooks that Heilbron and Seidel read are followed here. Craddock’s figure shows the chamber with its single dee and a deflector. Livingston submitted his thesis on it in April 1931.

The Berkeley laboratory’s own history calls this the first working cyclotron, five inches across, giving 80 keV protons on 2 January 1931; Heilbron and Seidel, whose account is the more detailed, say the ions were H\(_2^+\), and the resonance numbers of section 2 agree with them.

The 11-inch machine, 1931 to 1932

On 9 January 1931 Lawrence specified the next machine: a radius of 10 cm and a field of up to 15 kG. Kenneth Bainbridge estimated the materials at about USD 700; the university advanced money and the National Research Council gave USD 500. The Federal Telegraph Company built the magnet, which arrived on 3 July 1931; a 500 W oscillator was borrowed from Federal. With 9-inch poles, Lawrence and Livingston announced ions of over 500 keV in August 1931 in Physical Review.

The poles were enlarged to 11 inches (Livingston and Blewett give pole faces of 10 inches for the machine that reached 1 MeV). The amplification then stuck at about seventy-five steps. The cause was not relativity but irregularities in the magnetic field, and the cure was to correct the field with thin iron shims. On 9 January 1932, with 4,000 V on the dee, protons reached 1.22 MeV (the laboratory’s own history records the first megavolt reading on 8 January). The current at the collector was about \(10^{-9}\) A. Lawrence and Livingston’s paper, received on 20 February 1932, states that the focusing action of the fields gave beams less than 1 mm across, and that a simple correction of the magnetic field allowed a large amplification; it already understood vertical focusing by both the magnetic and the electric fields. It also announced that a magnet with 114 cm pole faces was being installed.

In April 1932 Cockcroft and Walton at Cambridge split lithium with protons of a few hundred keV from a direct voltage (chapter 5). Berkeley had the energy but, as Craddock puts it, no nuclear-physics experience, and confirmed the result by September 1932.

The Radiation Laboratory and the 27-inch magnet

On 26 August 1931 the university’s president, Robert Gordon Sproul, gave Lawrence the old Civil Engineering Testing Laboratory, which became the Radiation Laboratory. On 1 July 1936 it became an independent entity within the Physics Department.

The large magnet came from Federal Telegraph through Leonard Fuller: one of a pair of derelict Poulsen-arc magnets built for a First World War radio link (the Poulsen arc made radio waves with an electric arc burning in a strong magnetic field, so its transmitters needed large electromagnets), about 80 tons, valued at USD 25,000. The Pelton Water Wheel Company rebuilt its poles to 27.5 inches (70 cm), parallel to four thousandths of an inch; the field was uniform up to 18 kG. It reached the laboratory in early February 1932. Rebuilding, moving and setting up the magnet needed about USD 12,000, paid by the Research Corporation (USD 5,000), the Chemical Foundation (USD 2,500) and Sproul (the rest).

Heilbron and Seidel give the 27-inch’s first results as H\(_2^+\) ions of 1.6 MeV in mid-September 1932 and 3.6 MeV about a week later, at about 1 μA. Deuterium, separated by the chemist Gilbert Lewis, was first accelerated in April 1933. In 1934 Lawrence and Livingston reported hydrogen ions of up to 5 MeV, and wider dees made room for a larger ion source, giving 0.7 μA of 3 MeV deuterons. By September 1935 it gave 9 μA of 3.5 MeV deuterons. In 1936 Lawrence and Cooksey reported deuterons of up to 6.3 MeV at many microamperes and doubly charged helium ions of 11 MeV; Craddock gives 20 μA of 6.3 MeV deuterons by 1936.

The 37-inch

In 1937 the poles were enlarged to 37 inches. The laboratory’s history gives 8 MeV deuterons and 16 MeV alpha particles; Craddock gives 100 μA at 8.5 MeV in 1938, and Heilbron and Seidel 100 μA of 6.4 MeV deuterons, mostly for making radioactive materials. The figures differ with the date and the purpose of the run; about 8 MeV and up to about 100 μA is the fair summary. It was the laboratory’s workhorse for nuclear physics and for isotopes in the late 1930s.

The 60-inch Crocker cyclotron

Lawrence’s brother John, a physician, joined the laboratory in 1935, and the medical promise of the cyclotron brought money. The Crocker Radiation Laboratory was built with money from the university regent William H. Crocker (Mrs Sproul recalled USD 75,000). The 60-inch magnet weighed 220 tons, stood 11 feet high, had a 22-inch gap for the chamber, and cost USD 31,546 assembled; it was complete in August 1938. In May 1939, after much shimming and trimming, it gave a few microamperes of 7.5 MeV protons with 10 kW into the oscillator, and then about 200 kV across the dees with 80 kW. Deuterons followed in June; the current rose from 5 μA of 16 MeV deuterons at the end of June to over 100 μA by October, and from mid-February 1940 it ran at up to 200 μA. Per microampere it gave five times as many neutrons as at 8 MeV. Through September 1939 the cyclotron had cost about USD 63,500, the labour USD 20,750 and the building USD 75,000. The first paper, on its initial performance, appeared in July 1939 under eight names, Lawrence, Alvarez, Brobeck, Cooksey, Corson, McMillan, Salisbury and Thornton.

The 60-inch made neptunium (announced June 1940); Kamen’s work on carbon isotopes that found carbon-14 (27 February 1940) used both the 37-inch and the 60-inch; and the first plutonium was made in the 60-inch.

Pre-requisite (continued): Nuclear reactions and Q value

Pre-requisite. Chapter 1 taught reaction notation, the Q value from mass excesses, the Coulomb barrier, half-life, activity and the curie. This section adds two things the cyclotron’s results rest on. First, why a beam of deuterons makes radioactivity and neutrons in almost any light target: the deuteron is loosely bound, so reactions that hand over its neutron or its proton release several MeV. Second, how a radioactive product builds up in a target under steady bombardment, which turns “so many millicuries a day” into a reaction rate. The next section uses both. Taught from Livingston and Blewett, section 14-6 (deuteron reactions as neutron sources, and their yields), with the method of chapter 1; the mass excesses are from the 2020 Atomic Mass Evaluation and the half-lives from the evaluated nuclear data tables.

Notation, reminded. In \(^{23}\)Na(d,p)\(^{24}\)Na the target is sodium-23, the projectile a deuteron, the light product a proton, and the heavy product sodium-24, which is radioactive. The Q value is the kinetic energy released: the mass excesses \(\Delta\) (MeV) of the particles going in, minus those coming out. The mass excess is an atom’s mass less \(A\) atomic mass units, as an energy, \(\Delta = (M - A\,\mathrm{u})c^2\); since the count of nucleons balances in a reaction, only the excesses matter (F7, section 12).

Why deuterons. The deuteron’s binding energy is

\[ B_d = \Delta_n + \Delta_p - \Delta_d = 8.071 + 7.289 - 13.136 = 2.225\ \mathrm{MeV}, \]

about 1.1 MeV per nucleon, against about 8 MeV per nucleon in most nuclei (F7, section 11). (A nucleon is a proton or a neutron; the binding energy is the energy needed to pull a nucleus apart into them.) In a (d,p) reaction the target keeps the deuteron’s neutron. The energy released is what the neutron’s binding in the product gives, its separation energy \(S_n\), less what it cost to free the neutron from the deuteron:

\[ Q(\mathrm{d,p}) = S_n - B_d . \]

For sodium-24, \(S_n = 6.96\) MeV, so \(Q = 4.73\) MeV. Separation energies of 5 to 9 MeV are usual, so (d,p) and, in the same way, (d,n) reactions usually release several MeV. They need no threshold: any deuteron that reaches the nucleus can make them. One picture often given, though not taught in the books used here, is Oppenheimer and Phillips’s of 1935: the deuteron is large and loosely held together, and near a nucleus its proton is pushed away by the nuclear charge while its neutron, which feels no repulsion, is captured.

That, with the positive Q values, is why deuterons “made activity in almost everything they struck”, and why every deuteron beam on a light target made neutrons. Livingston and Blewett (section 14-6) note that deuterons on deuterium, lithium and beryllium have the largest neutron-producing cross-sections, and that beryllium, a metal that can be water-cooled, is the practical target. They give a measured thick-target yield at the 60-inch of one neutron per 200 deuterons of 15 MeV on beryllium. A beam of 100 μA is \(6.2 \times 10^{14}\) deuterons a second, so that is \(3.1 \times 10^{12}\) neutrons a second.

Build-up under bombardment. A steady beam makes radioactive nuclei at a constant rate \(R\) (s⁻¹), and they decay at the rate \(\lambda N\), with \(\lambda = \ln 2/T_{1/2}\) the decay constant (s⁻¹) and \(N\) the number present (F7, section 13: the activity in becquerels, 1 Bq one decay a second, and the curie, 1 Ci \(= 3.7 \times 10^{10}\) Bq). (Here \(R\) is a rate, not the radius of the rest of the chapter.) So

\[ \frac{dN}{dt} = R - \lambda N , \]

a first-order equation with a constant source, the same as that of a charging resistor column in chapter 6. Separate the variables as in chapter 2: \(dN/(R - \lambda N) = dt\), so \(-(1/\lambda)\ln(R - \lambda N) = t + \text{constant}\). Starting from none, \(N = 0\) at \(t = 0\) fixes the constant, and

\[ N(t) = \frac{R}{\lambda}\left(1 - e^{-\lambda t}\right), \qquad A(t) = \lambda N = R\left(1 - e^{-\lambda t}\right). \]

The activity \(A\) (Bq) rises towards \(R\), the saturation activity, reaching half of it after one half-life and \(1 - 2^{-t/T_{1/2}}\) of it after a run of length \(t\). When the beam stops, the activity decays as \(e^{-\lambda t}\). Making far more than one half-life’s worth gains nothing: decay then removes nuclei as fast as the beam makes them.

At 1 μA, \(6.24 \times 10^{12}\) deuterons a second, that would be \(1.8 \times 10^{-3}\) reactions per deuteron. This is the yield that the figure of the chapter’s next section implies. It is a third of the best neutron yield that Livingston and Blewett quote, from deuterons of 15 MeV, while the 27-inch’s deuterons had a few MeV, so it is a number to check against the original records. The 200 mCi themselves are \(5.7 \times 10^{14}\) atoms of sodium-24, \(2.3 \times 10^{-8}\) g: an activity that is easy to measure in an amount far too small to weigh.

Exercises.

  1. Find the Q value of \(^{31}\)P(d,p)\(^{32}\)P from the mass excesses \(\Delta(^{31}\mathrm{P}) = -24.441\) MeV, \(\Delta(^{32}\mathrm{P}) = -24.305\) MeV, \(\Delta_d = 13.136\) MeV and \(\Delta_p = 7.289\) MeV, and from it the neutron separation energy of phosphorus-32.
Answer

\(Q = -24.441 + 13.136 - 7.289 - (-24.305) = +5.711\) MeV. Then \(S_n = Q + B_d = 5.711 + 2.225 = 7.936\) MeV.

  1. John Lawrence’s phosphorus-32 came from the 37-inch at about 15 mCi a day. If that is the activity at the end of one day’s run, what was the production rate, what is the saturation activity, and what would a week’s run give? (\(T_{1/2} = 14.27\) d.)
Answer

One day reaches \(1 - 2^{-1/14.27} = 4.74\) per cent of saturation, so \(R = 15 \times 3.7 \times 10^{7}/0.0474 = 1.17 \times 10^{10}\) s⁻¹ and the saturation activity is \(R\), which is 316 mCi. A week reaches \(1 - 2^{-7/14.27} = 28.8\) per cent, 91 mCi. A long-lived product rewards long runs, or many short runs added together, since it hardly decays between them.

  1. A patient is given radiosodium made at the end of a run. What fraction of its activity remains two days later?
Answer

\(2^{-48/15.0} = 0.11\). Radiosodium had to be used within a day or two of being made, which tied its medical use to the cyclotron’s schedule.

  1. In 1935 the 27-inch made about \(10^{10}\) neutrons a second. If that came from the 9 μA of 3.5 MeV deuterons it gave that year, what was the yield per deuteron, and how does it compare with the 60-inch’s one in 200 at 15 MeV?
Answer

9 μA is \(5.6 \times 10^{13}\) deuterons a second, so the yield was \(1.8 \times 10^{-4}\) per deuteron, about a twenty-eighth of \(5 \times 10^{-3}\). The thick-target yield rises steeply with energy: the deuteron goes deeper before it stops, and more of its path lies above the barrier.

What the cyclotrons did

  • Artificial radioactivity. Irène Curie and Frédéric Joliot announced in 1934 that alpha particles make some light nuclei radioactive. At Berkeley, deuterons made activity in almost everything they struck. Lawrence made radiosodium by deuterons on table salt, \(^{23}\)Na(d,p)\(^{24}\)Na, half-life 15 hours; two years later he could make 200 mCi a day with 1 μA. In December 1936 Paul Aebersold announced that the biological effect of the cyclotron’s neutrons equalled that of the gamma rays from a large quantity of radium, which a news report valued at 5 million US dollars.
  • Phosphorus-32. John Lawrence used cyclotron-made radiophosphorus first in leukaemic mice and then in patients: the laboratory’s history gives a patient with polycythaemia vera in 1937, and another account a leukaemia patient in 1936. By the late 1930s the 37-inch made about 15 mCi of phosphorus-32 a day for him.
  • Technetium. Emilio Segrè visited Berkeley in the summer of 1936 and asked for discarded radioactive parts. Early in 1937 Lawrence sent him, cut up in several letters, a molybdenum strip from the 27-inch; Heilbron and Seidel say it had protected the edge of a dee at the exit slot, other accounts that it was part of the deflector. In Palermo, Segrè and the mineralogist Carlo Perrier showed chemically that some of its activity belonged to element 43, not then reliably known in nature, and published in the Journal of Chemical Physics in 1937. It was named technetium in 1947.
  • Neutrons for medicine. In 1935 and 1936 the group found that a mouse tumour responded more strongly to neutrons than to X-rays. Robert Stone, John Lawrence and Paul Aebersold then treated patients with fast neutrons from the cyclotrons and published a preliminary report in Radiology in September 1940. Heilbron and Seidel count 24 patients treated at the 37-inch from September 1938 and 226 at the 60-inch from 1939 to 1943 (Craddock gives about 200).

The Nobel prize

The prize in physics for 1939 went to Ernest Lawrence alone, “for the invention and development of the cyclotron”, and for its results, especially artificial radioactive elements. It was announced in November 1939. Because of the war he received it at Berkeley, in Wheeler Hall, on 29 February 1940, from the Swedish consul general in San Francisco.

The laboratory’s way of working

Lawrence ran the laboratory as a team around a machine. The staff grew from five to about sixty during the 1930s, physicists and chemists in about equal numbers, later physicians; among them McMillan, Alvarez, Seaborg, Segrè, Kamen, Livingood, Abelson, Cooksey and Brobeck. Livingston left for Cornell in 1934 and built a cyclotron there, the first outside Berkeley, for 2 MeV. Money came from many sources at once: the university, the Research Corporation (which also took the cyclotron patent, granted in February 1934), foundations, regents and donors; when the power company refused to cut its charges, Sproul paid the bills from an emergency fund.

The machine was run in shifts. On 30 November 1936 a sign-up sheet appeared: crews of two, six men a day under a captain, from 8:30 in the morning to midnight, seven days a week. An owl shift was added in May 1937, and from 4 July 1937 the laboratory ran for a time round the clock in four six-hour shifts; in 1938 it averaged about twenty hours a day. Berkeley-trained “cyclotroneers” built machines elsewhere: by 1940, Craddock counts 24 cyclotrons operating in the United States, 3 in Japan, 2 each in Britain and the USSR, and one each in Denmark, France, Germany and Sweden.

How such a machine is designed today

The classical cyclotron with a uniform, slightly falling field is no longer built for research; its energy limit was removed by the isochronous cyclotron, whose field rises with radius and varies with azimuth so that the revolution frequency stays constant while vertical focusing comes from the azimuthal variation (Thomas, 1938; chapter 12). The design questions are still those of this chapter: the K-value sets the magnet; the frequency and harmonic number set the dees; the gain per turn sets the turn separation and hence the extraction; the field must keep the phase within a narrow band. The field is computed in three dimensions with finite-element codes (programs that split the magnet and the air round it into many small cells and solve the field equations cell by cell, as in F1, section 11), measured on a grid with Hall probes (small semiconductor chips whose voltage is proportional to the field through them) and corrected by shimming; orbits are tracked turn by turn through the measured map to find the phase history, the tunes and the turn separation, and the central region is designed by tracking through computed electric fields. The magnet itself is the subject of chapter 17.

At the bench

What there is to read and set

At a 1930s cyclotron the operator’s quantities are few. The magnet current, on an ammeter, sets the field. The oscillator’s plate current and voltage, and later a measurement of the dee voltage, set \(\hat{V}\); its frequency is fixed by the tuned circuit of the dees and their stems. The ion source has a filament current, an arc current and a gas feed. A vacuum gauge reads the chamber pressure. The beam is read as a current on an electrometer or galvanometer (sensitive meters for very small charges and currents) connected to a collector or probe: a nanoampere at the 11-inch in 1932, a microampere at the 27-inch in 1932, tens to hundreds of microamperes at the 37-inch and 60-inch.

The timescales are those of the physics: a period of 0.05 to 0.4 μs, a few to some hundreds of turns, a few microseconds in the machine, so the beam is continuous on any meter.

Finding resonance

The oscillator runs at a fixed frequency, so the operator finds resonance by sweeping the magnet current slowly while watching the current to a probe at a fixed radius. The probe current is zero over most of the sweep and rises in a narrow peak, the resonance curve. Section 9 gives its width: for the 11-inch about 0.4% of the field at the collector, for the 4-inch about 1.6%. A probe moved outward shows a narrower peak, because more turns are needed to reach it. Heilbron and Seidel date the 4-inch’s first success to the day the resonant field stopped depending on the dee voltage, as it must for true resonance, where the field is set by the frequency and the ion alone.

The probe on a radial arm

A probe on an arm that can be moved in radius through a vacuum seal maps the beam current against radius. A healthy machine shows current falling slowly with radius; a sudden drop at one radius marks a field error, an obstruction, or the radius at which the phase slips out. Wilson used the radioactivity induced in the probe to measure the beam’s width and height at several radii of the 27-inch. Lawrence and Livingston, in 1932, found the beam less than 1 mm across.

Shimming

Shimming means adding or removing thin pieces of iron, in the gap or on the pole faces, to change the field locally. In 1931 and 1932 it was done by trial: run, find from the probe where the beam is lost, add or move iron, run again. On the 11-inch, shimming took the number of gap crossings from about 75 to over 300. Section 9 says why: 300 crossings need the field right to a few parts in a thousand everywhere along the path. Rose’s 1938 analysis explained the sensitivity of the beam to shimming as the price of the compromise between phase and focusing. The 60-inch needed “much shimming and trimming” before its first beam in May 1939.

The beam on a target

Inside the chamber the beam struck a collector or a target on the probe; outside, once beams were brought through a thin window, it could be seen. Ions of a few MeV stop within a fraction of a millimetre of metal and dump all their power there: 100 μA of 16 MeV deuterons is 1.6 kW, so targets and deflectors were water-cooled, and Lawrence and Livingston already pointed to cooling as the way to more usable current in 1934. A beam in air excites the nitrogen and glows blue-violet along its range; a fluorescent screen shows its spot.

The ion source

The source sets the current. A burnt-out filament stops the machine until the chamber is opened; too much gas raises the pressure and scatters the beam, too little starves the arc. In 1934 new dees, wider at the centre, made room for a larger source, and the deuteron current rose from 0.02 μA (1933) to 0.7 μA.

Vacuum leaks

The chamber is a flat box as wide as the poles, sealed with wax and gaskets, with feedthroughs for the dee stems, the source, the probe and the deflector. A large flat chamber has a long perimeter of seals, and every new feedthrough is a new place to leak. At the 27-inch, leaks in the tank were a persistent problem; in 1933 vacuum troubles and other misfortunes kept the machine down for most of September. The signature is a pressure that will not fall, a beam that fades as the pressure rises, and a glow discharge in the gap (chapter 3).

Sparking of the dees

The dee voltage is limited by breakdown across the gap, along the insulators of the dee stems, and to the walls (chapter 4). A spark shows as a sudden drop in dee voltage, a jump in oscillator current and a loss of beam; bad vacuum makes it worse. The oscillator itself was dangerous: Jack Livingood received a 10,000 V spark that jumped fourteen inches from the high-frequency generator to his head. In 1937 Wesley Coates, a Berkeley graduate, died in New York after brushing a high-voltage line on an X-ray machine of Sloan’s design.

Neutrons in the room

Every deuteron beam on a light target makes neutrons, and in 1935 the 27-inch made about \(10^{10}\) a second. Heilbron and Seidel remark that the laboratory was so full of stray neutrons that anyone who had tested the fillings in his teeth might have discovered artificial radioactivity there. Lawrence began to worry about neutron exposure in the autumn of 1933; in 1935 John Lawrence showed undesirably large exposures among the staff; the 27-inch’s controls were moved thirty feet from the beryllium target. Robert Cornog, who put his hand in the deuteron beam, was the one person whose blood counts showed overexposure. By 1938 almost all the metal in the laboratory was radioactive. X-rays were present too: in December 1932, with Sloan’s X-ray tube running in the same laboratory, Lawrence wrote that they could see X-rays almost everywhere in it with a fluoroscope.

Where it stops

The classical cyclotron stops where the phase slip of section 8 pushes the particle past the decelerating half of the cycle. The relativistic mass increase is the root: it is 0.85% for 16 MeV deuterons and 2.7% for 25 MeV protons, while the window of section 9 is a fraction of a percent for a few hundred turns. The vertical focusing makes it worse, because it needs a field that falls with radius. With 50 to 200 kV on the dees the estimates of section 8 give about 8 to 31 MeV for protons and 11 to 44 MeV for deuterons, depending on how the field is set and the particle started, before any fall for focusing is paid for; Bethe and Rose’s estimate as Craddock summarises it was about 8 MeV for deuterons at 50 kV, growing as the square root of the voltage. Livingston and Blewett (section 6-12) re-evaluate Rose’s limit for the more than 200 kV of later large machines as 30 MeV protons and 42 MeV deuterons, and record that the two largest fixed-frequency cyclotrons reached 22 MeV protons and 24 MeV deuterons. Electrons are out of reach entirely: their \(\gamma\) changes by 10% at 50 keV.

Lawrence’s first answer was brute force: Craddock records his plan to run the dees of the 184-inch at 1 or 2 MV, with a 4,300-ton magnet. The square-root law shows how little that buys: the simple limit for deuterons is 49 MeV at 1 MV and 69 MeV at 2 MV.

The ways past were two ideas. Thomas showed in 1938 that a field varying with azimuth gives extra vertical focusing, enough to let the average field rise with radius in step with \(\gamma\), so that the revolution frequency stays constant: the isochronous cyclotron. Veksler and McMillan showed independently, by the end of the war, that particles can stay in phase while the frequency is lowered during acceleration, because a particle that arrives early or late is pushed back towards a stable phase: phase stability, and with it the synchrocyclotron, into which the 184-inch was converted in 1946. Both are taken up in chapter 12. For electrons the answer was the betatron (chapter 11).

Exercises

1. Compute the cyclotron frequency in 1 T for a slow proton, deuteron, alpha particle and electron.

Answer

\(f_0 = qB/(2\pi m)\): proton 15.25 MHz, deuteron 7.626 MHz, alpha particle (\(q = 2e\)) 7.675 MHz, electron 27.99 GHz. The deuteron and the alpha particle differ by 0.64%, because the alpha’s mass is not exactly twice the deuteron’s.

2. In December 1930 Livingston’s 4-inch model showed resonance with a 2.5 MHz oscillator at about 0.33 T. Which ion was being accelerated? At the maximum field of 1.27 T, what is its frequency and the radius at 80 keV? About how much did it gain a turn, if it took 41 turns?

Answer

At 0.33 T a proton circles at 5.03 MHz and H\(_2^+\) at 2.51 MHz, so the ion is H\(_2^+\); exact resonance at 2.5 MHz needs 0.328 T. At 1.27 T the frequency is 9.68 MHz (a wavelength of 31 m). With \(mc^2 = 1877\) MeV, an 80 keV H\(_2^+\) ion has \(B\rho = 0.0578\) T m and a radius of 4.6 cm, inside the 5 cm radius of the chamber. 80 keV in 41 turns is about 2 keV a turn, two crossings of about 1 kV, matching the “under 1,000 V on the dee”.

3. The 11-inch gave 1.22 MeV protons at a radius of 11.5 cm with 4 kV on the dee. Find the field, the frequency, the least number of turns, the length of the path and the turn separation at the collector.

Answer

\(B\rho = 0.1597\) T m, so \(B = 1.39\) T and \(f_0 = 21.2\) MHz. At most \(2\times 4 = 8\) keV a turn gives at least \(1.22/0.008 = 152.5\), so 153 turns. The path is \(\tfrac{4\pi}{3}RN = 73\) m, taking 7.2 μs. The turn separation at the collector is \(R/(2N) = 0.38\) mm. A straight resonant accelerator with the same 305 gaps and the same frequency would have drift tubes adding up to 73 m: half a turn at radius \(r\) is \(\pi r = v\,T_{RF}/2 = \beta\lambda/2\), so the two sums are the same.

4. Show that the turn separation at the edge is \(R/(2N)\) for a constant gain per turn, and evaluate it for the 60-inch, with 200 kV across the dees, 16 MeV deuterons and an extraction radius of 0.6 to 0.7 m.

Answer

\(r_n = R\sqrt{n/N}\), so \(dr/dn = R/(2\sqrt{nN})\), which at \(n = N\) is \(R/(2N)\). With 0.4 MeV a turn at the crest, \(N = 40\), \(\Delta r/R = 1.25\%\), and \(\Delta r = 7.5\) to \(8.8\) mm. The first turn is at 9.2 cm (for 0.4 MeV at about 1.4 T), so the turns run from widely spaced at the centre to under a centimetre apart at the edge.

5. The 60-inch accelerated deuterons to 16 MeV. If alpha particles follow the same orbit, at the same field, what is their energy? The oscillator frequency is fixed: by how much must the field change when switching from deuterons to alphas?

Answer

The same orbit in the same field means the same \(p/q\): \(B\rho = 0.8189\) T m. For the alpha particle, \(p = 2eB\rho\), giving \(W = 32.2\) MeV, twice the deuteron’s, as the K-value predicts (\(W/A = K(Q_i/A)^2\) is the same for \(Q_i/A = 1/2\)). The alpha’s \(q/m\) is 0.64% larger than the deuteron’s, so at a fixed frequency the field must be lowered by 0.64%. That is inside the resonance window of a 40-turn machine (about \(0.65/N\), 1.6%) but enough to change the phase history, and in a machine of 150 turns (window about 0.4%) it would lose the beam: the field must be retuned.

6. Show that \(Q_x^2 + Q_y^2 = 1\) for any field index. For Rose’s field \(B \propto 1 - 0.004(r/R)^2\), find \(n\), \(Q_y\) and the number of turns per vertical oscillation at \(r = R/2\) and \(r = R\). By how much does the vertical amplitude shrink between them?

Answer

\(Q_x^2 + Q_y^2 = (1 - n) + n = 1\): the field index only shares a fixed amount of focusing between the planes. With \(a = 0.004\), \(n = 2a\xi^2/(1 - a\xi^2)\): at \(\xi = 0.5\), \(n = 0.0020\), \(Q_y = 0.045\), 22 turns per oscillation; at \(\xi = 1\), \(n = 0.0080\), \(Q_y = 0.090\), 11 turns. The adiabatic invariant gives \(A \propto n^{-1/4}\), so the amplitude at the edge is \((0.0080/0.0020)^{-1/4} = 0.71\) of that at half radius.

7. Derive \(W_{max} = 2\sqrt{q\hat{V}mc^2/\pi}\) for a field tuned to resonance at the centre, and evaluate it for the 11-inch (protons, 4 kV) and for deuterons at 50, 100 and 200 kV. What would 1 MV on the dees give for deuterons?

Answer

With \(dW/dn = 2q\hat{V}\sin\phi\) and \(d\phi/dn = 2\pi W/mc^2\), dividing gives \(\sin\phi\,d\phi = \pi W\,dW/(q\hat{V}mc^2)\), so \(\cos\phi_0 - \cos\phi = \pi W^2/(2q\hat{V}mc^2) \le 2\). Hence the result. Protons at 4 kV: 2.19 MeV, so the 11-inch at 1.22 MeV was within its limit. Deuterons: 10.9 MeV at 50 kV, 15.5 MeV at 100 kV, 21.9 MeV at 200 kV, 48.9 MeV at 1 MV. The square-root law is why raising the dee voltage was not a way to hundreds of MeV.

8. Show that a field error \(\varepsilon\), constant over the machine, allows acceleration through \(N\) turns only if \(|\varepsilon| < 1/(2N)\) (ignore the mass increase). Evaluate for the 11-inch and the 4-inch, and compare with the full widths found turn by turn, 0.42% and 1.59%.

Answer

The slip per turn is \(2\pi\varepsilon\) (from \(\Delta\phi = 2\pi(B_c/B - 1)\) with \(B = B_c(1 + \varepsilon)\), to first order, sign aside). Over \(N\) turns it is \(2\pi|\varepsilon|N\), which must stay below the accelerating half cycle, \(\pi\). So \(|\varepsilon| < 1/(2N)\): 0.33% for the 11-inch (\(N = 153\)), 1.2% for the 4-inch (\(N = 41\)), a full window of at most 0.65% and 2.4%. The turn-by-turn widths are smaller, 0.42% and 1.59%, because the particle must also gain enough energy on the way, which it does not if it spends many turns near the ends of the half cycle where \(\sin\phi\) is small. Both scale as \(1/N\): about \(0.6/N\).

9. An electrostatic deflector acts on 16 MeV deuterons on an orbit of radius 0.6 m (an assumed radius). Find \(v\), \(B\) and \(vB\). What field is needed to move the orbit out by 1 cm and by 2 cm over a quarter turn? What voltage is that across a 6 mm gap?

Answer

\(\beta = 0.1298\), \(v = 3.89\times10^7\) m/s; \(B = B\rho/\rho = 0.819/0.6 = 1.36\) T; \(vB = 531\) kV/cm. A displacement \(x = \rho f_{def}\) over a quarter turn needs \(f_{def} = x/\rho\): 1.7% for 1 cm, 3.3% for 2 cm. Then \(E = f_{def}\,vB = 8.9\) kV/cm or 17.7 kV/cm, that is 5.3 kV or 10.6 kV across 6 mm. This is the field the deflector must hold, in vacuum, beside a beam that strikes its septum; the sum ignores the fall of the field at the magnet’s edge, which helps.
