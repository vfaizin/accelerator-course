In one page

The wall. By 1930 a transformer, a rectifier or a gap in air held a few hundred kilovolts. The multiplier of chapter 5 reached about 700 kV by sharing the voltage among many parts. What breaks down is the field, volts per metre, not the voltage.

The idea. Carry charge on a moving belt into a hollow, smooth conductor. Charge brought inside a closed conductor goes to its outer surface whatever the conductor’s voltage, so the belt can keep adding charge until the field outside reaches breakdown. Make the conductor large and round, put it in a tank of compressed insulating gas, fix the potential of the space between with rings, and let the beam fall through a sectioned vacuum tube down the middle. Then send negative ions up to a positive terminal, strip them there, and accelerate them down again: the tandem uses the voltage twice or more.

The equations to remember. Here \(\mathcal{E}\) is an electric field in V/m, as everywhere in the course (F4), and \(\phi\) or \(V\) an electric potential in volts.

\[ I = \sigma_s w v_b, \qquad \sigma_s \le \varepsilon_0 \mathcal{E}_b \]

the charging current \(I\) (A) of a belt of width \(w\) (m) and speed \(v_b\) (m/s) carrying surface charge density \(\sigma_s\) (C/m\(^2\)), limited by the breakdown field \(\mathcal{E}_b\) of the gas.

\[ \mathcal{E} = \frac{V}{a}\ \text{(isolated sphere)}, \qquad \mathcal{E}_{max} = \frac{4V}{b}\ \text{(best sphere, radius } b/2\text{, in a tank of radius } b) \]

\[ \mathcal{E}_b \propto p \]

on the far side of the Paschen minimum (the pressure-times-gap at which a gas is easiest to break down, chapter 4) the breakdown field rises roughly in proportion to the gas pressure \(p\) (here and in section 5 \(p\) is a pressure, in Pa or bar; elsewhere in the chapter it is a momentum, as in the rest of the course).

\[ W = W_{inj} + (1 + n_q)\,eV_T \]

the kinetic energy of a tandem beam injected as singly charged negative ions with energy \(W_{inj}\) and stripped to charge state \(n_q\) at terminal voltage \(V_T\).

\[ \frac{\Delta W}{W} \approx 2\,\frac{\Delta x}{D_x} \]

the energy deviation of slow ions seen as a displacement \(\Delta x\) at the slits of an analysing magnet with dispersion \(D_x\) (m), the sideways shift at the slits per unit fractional change of momentum (section 8).

Numbers to know. Air at one atmosphere breaks down near 3 MV/m, so a belt in air carries at most 27 µC/m\(^2\). Sulphur hexafluoride holds about three times what air or a nitrogen-carbon dioxide mixture holds at the same pressure; tanks run at about 5 to 7 bar. Van de Graaff’s 1929 model made 80 kV, his 1931 pair of spheres more than 1 MV; Herb’s pressure tank gave 4.5 MV by 1940. A terminal is held steady to about one part in ten thousand. The largest machines reached 20 to 25 MV, and there they stopped, limited by the energy stored in the tank and by its size.

The wall

In 1927 Rutherford asked for a copious supply of particles more energetic than those from radioactive sources, and in 1928 Gamow’s tunnelling made a few hundred kilovolts look enough for protons on light nuclei. Insulation stood in the way. Transformers and rectifiers held a few hundred kilovolts. Pulsed sources such as Tesla coils went higher but only for microseconds, with no steady energy; at the Carnegie Institution’s Department of Terrestrial Magnetism in Washington, Gregory Breit, Merle Tuve, Lawrence Hafstad and Odd Dahl reported a Tesla coil, its calibration and discharge tubes for it in a series of papers in 1930 (Livingston and Blewett, section 2-2). Cockcroft and Walton’s multiplier reached about 700 kV in 1932.

Robert Jemison Van de Graaff, an Alabama engineer who had studied physics at the Sorbonne and at Oxford, came to Princeton as a National Research Fellow in 1929. Machines that carried charge on a moving band were old (Walckiers de St. Amand rubbed charge onto a silk belt in 1784, and in 1872 Righi carried it on metal cylinders strung on an insulating cord; McKay, 2005). Van de Graaff saw that the voltage of such a machine is not set by its mechanism but by how much charge the high-voltage electrode holds before the field at its surface breaks down the air. Make the electrode large and smooth, put the charging mechanism inside it, and the mechanism never sees the high voltage.

His first model, built at Princeton in the autumn of 1929, was described by Karl Compton in 1933 as made from “a tin can, a silk ribbon and a small motor, at no expense” (quoted by Burrill, 1967). It reached 80 kV, limited by corona from the edges of the can (corona is a glowing, hissing discharge confined to the thin layer of gas where the field near a sharp edge or point exceeds breakdown; it does not bridge the gap, but it carries charge away; chapter 4): the field at a sharp edge is far above the average, which is the problem of this chapter in one sentence. By the autumn of 1931 two machines with aluminium spheres 24 inches (61 cm) across on glass rods 7 feet (2.1 m) high, each charged by its own motor-driven silk belt (Livingston and Blewett, section 3-1), built for under USD 100, made more than a million volts between the spheres; the figure usually given is 1.5 MV. Van de Graaff showed them at the inaugural dinner of the American Institute of Physics in New York in November 1931 (on the 10th or the 11th; accounts differ).

Two walls remained. The first was air: a sphere in open air holds about 3 MV per metre of radius, so the next machine, at Round Hill in 1933, had spheres 4.6 m across in an airship hangar. The second was the tube, which must hold the whole voltage in vacuum along an insulator. Raymond Herb and Glen Havens at Wisconsin, starting soon after Van de Graaff’s first results, tried to insulate a belt-charged terminal by vacuum alone and could not pass 300 kV in about a year, with no understanding of why the vacuum broke down. The ways past were compressed gas, a graded column, a sectioned tube, and later the tandem.

The physics

Pre-requisite (continued): Gauss’s law and potentials of simple shapes

Pre-requisite (continued). Gauss’s law and the isolated sphere were taught in chapter 1, the pillbox on a single charged sheet in chapter 2, and nested spheres, coaxial cylinders and the field \(\sigma/\varepsilon_0\) at a conductor’s surface in chapter 4. This section adds three things a belt-charged terminal needs: why charge put inside a hollow conductor ends up on its outside whatever its voltage, how a charged sheet’s field divides between its two faces when conductors lie near it, which limits what a belt can carry, and the field just outside a conductor read as induced charge, which is what the generating voltmeter measures. Sections 1, 2 and 8 use them. Taught from Tong’s Electromagnetism, sections 2.1.4 (surface charge and discontinuities) and 2.4 (conductors).

A conductor in equilibrium. A metal holds electrons that are free to move. If there were a field anywhere inside the metal, they would move; so once everything has settled, which takes far less than a microsecond, the field inside the metal is zero. Two consequences follow.

First, draw a closed surface lying wholly inside the metal. The field is zero at every point of it, so the flux through it is zero, and by Gauss’s law

\[ \oint \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot d\mathbf{A} = \frac{Q_{enc}}{\varepsilon_0} \]

(the flux of the field out through a closed surface equals the charge inside divided by \(\varepsilon_0\); F4, section 4), the charge it encloses is zero. That holds for every such surface, however close to the skin, so any net charge on a conductor sits on its surface.

Second, the whole conductor is at one potential. The potential difference between two points \(A\) and \(B\) is \(\phi(B) - \phi(A) = -\int_A^B \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot d\mathbf{l}\) (a line integral, F2, section 7: add up the field’s component along each small step \(d\mathbf{l}\) of the path), and along any path inside the metal \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = 0\).

The hollow conductor. Now let the conductor be hollow, with an empty cavity inside. The cavity’s wall is part of the conductor, so it is all at the terminal’s potential \(V\). Inside the cavity there is no charge, so the potential obeys Laplace’s equation, \(\nabla^2\phi = 0\) (F2, section 12), with \(\phi = V\) on the whole boundary. The constant \(\phi = V\) is a solution, and by uniqueness (chapter 2) it is the only one. So the field inside an empty cavity is zero, however much charge sits on the outside and however high \(V\) is.

Next carry a charge \(q\) into the cavity on an insulator: a patch of belt. Draw a closed surface inside the metal, around the cavity. The flux through it is still zero, so the charge inside it is zero: the inner wall now carries \(-q\), drawn there by the patch. The conductor’s total charge has not changed, so \(+q\) has appeared on its outer surface. Then let a comb of points touch the patch’s charge off onto the inner wall. The \(q\) and the \(-q\) cancel, and \(+q\) remains on the outside. Nothing in this argument used the terminal’s voltage or the charge already on it. Faraday showed it in 1843 with a metal ice pail and an electroscope (two light leaves of gold foil hung together, which spread apart when they are charged): lowering a charged ball into the pail made the leaves spread by the same amount whether or not the ball touched the inside. The belt generator runs his experiment continuously.

Where then is the work done? On the way up. Carrying \(q\) from ground to the terminal’s opening crosses the field between ground and terminal, and the motor pays \(qV\) there. Inside the cavity the field is zero and nothing more is paid.

Round Hill. One sphere of radius 2.3 m at about 2.5 MV (half of the 5.1 MV between the pair) held \(Q = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 aV = 640\) µC. Its surface field was \(V/a = 1.09\) MV/m, and its surface charge density, from the result below, \(\varepsilon_0 \mathcal{E} = 9.6\) µC/m\(^2\). All of it was on the outside; the inside of the sphere was field-free.

The pillbox on a charged sheet. Take a thin sheet carrying surface charge density \(\sigma_s\) (C/m\(^2\)). Draw a pillbox: a short cylinder whose two flat faces, each of area \(A\), lie just either side of the sheet. Shrink its height to zero so that its curved side contributes no flux. Let \(\mathcal{E}_1\) and \(\mathcal{E}_2\) be the normal components of the field on the two faces, each counted positive when it points away from the sheet. The flux out is \((\mathcal{E}_1 + \mathcal{E}_2)A\) and the charge enclosed is \(\sigma_s A\), so

\[ \mathcal{E}_1 + \mathcal{E}_2 = \frac{\sigma_s}{\varepsilon_0} . \]

Gauss’s law fixes only the sum. How it divides depends on what lies around the sheet.

  • An isolated sheet, far from everything, is symmetric: \(\mathcal{E}_1 = \mathcal{E}_2 = \sigma_s/2\varepsilon_0\).
  • A sheet between two grounded plates, at distance \(d\) from one and \(D\) from the other, sits at some potential \(V_s\). With plates and sheet wide compared with the gaps, the field in each gap is uniform: \(\mathcal{E}_1 = V_s/d\) and \(\mathcal{E}_2 = V_s/D\). Their sum is \(\sigma_s/\varepsilon_0\), so \(V_s = \sigma_s/[\varepsilon_0(1/d + 1/D)]\) and

\[ \mathcal{E}_1 = \frac{\sigma_s}{\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{D}{d + D} . \]

The nearer plate takes the larger share of the field lines. When \(d \ll D\), nearly all of them go that way and \(\mathcal{E}_1 \to \sigma_s/\varepsilon_0\): the one-sided case of section 2.

A belt near a grounded surface. With the belt 2 cm from a grounded plate and 20 cm from the column on its other side, \(\mathcal{E}_1 = 0.91\,\sigma_s/\varepsilon_0\). For breakdown at \(\mathcal{E}_b = 3\) MV/m in air the belt may carry \(\sigma_s \le \varepsilon_0 \mathcal{E}_b(1 + d/D) = 29.2\) µC/m\(^2\), against 26.6 µC/m\(^2\) for the worst case. The safe bound \(\sigma_s \le \varepsilon_0 \mathcal{E}_b\) of section 2 is the limit \(d/D \to 0\).

The field just outside a conductor. Put the pillbox across the surface of a conductor, one face in the metal, where the field is zero, and one just outside. Only the outer face carries flux, so \(\mathcal{E}_n A = \sigma A/\varepsilon_0\):

\[ \mathcal{E}_n = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0} . \]

The field there is normal to the surface, since a component along the surface would move the surface charges. Read the other way, a grounded plate facing a field \(\mathcal{E}_n\) carries an induced charge of size \(\varepsilon_0 \mathcal{E}_n A\) on its area \(A\), of the opposite sign to the terminal’s. That is the generating voltmeter of section 8: in a wall field of 2 MV/m a 0.01 m\(^2\) plate holds 0.177 µC while uncovered, and gives it back when the vane covers it.

Where the surface is sharply curved, the same charge per unit area gives the same field just outside, but the charge itself crowds onto the points and edges. Why it does was shown in chapter 4: two spheres of radii \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) joined by a wire share one potential, so \(Q/R\) is the same for both, and the surface field \(Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R^2)\), which is \(V/R\), is larger on the smaller one. A sharp edge acts as a very small sphere, and that is the reason the first can-and-ribbon model stopped at 80 kV.

Exercises

A1. A belt runs 1 cm from a grounded plate and 5 cm from a column on its other side, in air (\(\mathcal{E}_b = 3\) MV/m). What surface charge density may it carry before the near-side field reaches breakdown, and what current would a belt 0.5 m wide at 20 m/s then deliver?

Answer

The near side takes \(D/(d + D) = 5/6\) of \(\sigma_s/\varepsilon_0\), so \(\sigma_s \le \varepsilon_0 \mathcal{E}_b \times 6/5 = 31.9\) µC/m\(^2\), and \(I = \sigma_s w v_b = 31.9 \times 10^{-6} \times 0.5 \times 20 = 319\) µA. The geometry gives 20 per cent more than the safe bound, but edges and pulleys still concentrate the field, so real belts run well below either figure.

A2. A terminal at 5 MV holds 300 µC. A patch of belt carrying 0.1 µC enters it. What charge is on the inner wall and on the outer surface before the comb touches the patch, and after? How much work did the motor do on that charge?

Answer

Before: \(-0.1\) µC on the inner wall, \(300.1\) µC on the outside. After: nothing on the inner wall, 300.1 µC on the outside. The motor did \(qV = 0.1 \times 10^{-6} \times 5 \times 10^6 = 0.5\) J, all of it while carrying the patch up through the field between ground and terminal; none inside, where the field is zero.

A3. A generating voltmeter plate of 0.02 m\(^2\) faces a wall field of 1.5 MV/m and is uncovered and covered 200 times a second. Find the induced charge and the average rectified current.

Answer

\(q = \varepsilon_0 \mathcal{E}_n A = 8.854 \times 10^{-12} \times 1.5 \times 10^6 \times 0.02 = 0.266\) µC. Each cycle moves \(q\) onto the plate and off again, so the average of the rectified current is \(2qf = 2 \times 0.266 \times 10^{-6} \times 200 = 106\) µA. It is proportional to \(\mathcal{E}_n\), and so to the terminal voltage.

1. Why a belt can keep charging a terminal

In equilibrium the field inside a conductor is zero, so by Gauss’s law any net charge sits on its outer surface. Charge carried inside the terminal on a belt and touched to the inner wall by a brush or comb of points therefore leaves the inside and spreads over the outside, whatever the terminal’s potential: Faraday’s ice-pail experiment, run continuously. The background is in Tong’s Electromagnetism, section 2.4 (conductors, charge and curvature, capacitors), and Landau and Lifshitz volume 2, sections 36 and 37.

The motor does the work. Carrying charge \(dQ\) from ground to a terminal at voltage \(V\) against the field takes work \(V\,dQ\), so the mechanical power going into charge is

\[ P = VI \]

in watts, with \(I\) the charging current in amperes. Below breakdown the belt is a current source: it delivers the same current whatever the terminal voltage.

Figure 6.1. A belt-charged generator, in side view and not to scale. A comb of sharp points at the bottom, fed from a supply of a few tens of kilovolts, sprays positive charge onto the rising run of an insulating belt. The belt carries it up into the hollow terminal, where a second comb, wired to the terminal’s inner wall, takes it off. The charge then moves to the outer surface, so the field is zero inside and the comb never meets the terminal’s high voltage across a gap; outside, the field lines leave the positive terminal. The motor does the work of carrying the charge up the column.

2. The belt current and the surface-charge limit

A belt of width \(w\) moving at speed \(v_b\) carries surface charge density \(\sigma_s\) (subscript \(s\), because \(\sigma\) is a cross-section elsewhere in the course), sprayed on at the bottom by corona from a row of points. In time \(dt\) a length \(v_b\,dt\) enters the terminal with charge \(\sigma_s w v_b\,dt\), so

\[ I = \sigma_s w v_b . \]

What limits \(\sigma_s\)? Draw a Gauss pillbox around a patch of belt. If the belt runs near a grounded or equipotential surface on one side, nearly all its field lines go that way and the field beside it is \(\mathcal{E} = \sigma_s/\varepsilon_0\); if it hangs far from everything, the lines divide between its faces and \(\mathcal{E} = \sigma_s/2\varepsilon_0\). (In general the normal fields on the two faces add up to \(\sigma_s/\varepsilon_0\).) The bound that is safe in any geometry, because it covers the worst case in which every line leaves by one face, is

\[ \sigma_s \le \varepsilon_0 \mathcal{E}_b . \]

The belt is treated as a sheet wide compared with its distance to its neighbours, and the terminal and column fields are ignored. Both fail at the pulleys and edges, where the field concentrates, so real belts run below this bound.

In air. With \(\mathcal{E}_b = 3\) MV/m, \(\sigma_s \le 26.6\) µC/m\(^2\).

A real belt. The Vivitron at Strasbourg ran a belt 0.38 m wide at 10.4 m/s, 101.3 m long, once round every 9.76 s (Foehrenbacher and others, 1998). At 15 MV the down-charge current (the paper’s term for the charging current the belt delivered) was 45 µA, so \(\sigma_s = 45\ \text{µA}/(0.38 \times 10.4) = 11\) µC/m\(^2\), and \(\sigma_s/\varepsilon_0 \approx 1.3\) MV/m. The paper’s estimates for the charges actually on the four runs of the belt gave fields between the runs of 0.2 to 1.0 MV/m, of the same order as the column’s average longitudinal field of 0.75 MV/m. That is less than half of what even air allows; at the air bound the belt would carry 105 µA.

The first giant. Round Hill had one paper belt 47 inches (1.19 m) wide in each column (Livingston and Blewett, section 3-5). The belts ran at up to 5,650 feet per minute (28.7 m/s) and the charging current at full capacity was given as 2.1 mA. That needs 73 µC per metre of belt length, or 2.75 m of belt width at the air bound: more than one belt offers even with both runs charged (2.38 m), so the 2.1 mA was most likely the total for the two spheres, about 1 mA each.

Belt and chain machines deliver tens to a few hundred microamperes. For nuclear physics that is plenty (a microampere of protons is \(6.2 \times 10^{12}\) a second); it is also why electrostatic machines never became high-current machines.

Pre-requisite (continued): Dielectrics, capacitance and field energy

Pre-requisite (continued). Capacitance, \(Q = CV\) and the stored energy \(\tfrac{1}{2}CV^2\), found as the work of charging, were taught in chapter 1, and capacitors and stored energy again in chapter 4. This section recalls the work of charging and adds the power it takes, the capacitance of a terminal inside a tank, the charge balance that sets the terminal voltage from moment to moment, and charging by induction, which is how a Pelletron chain picks up its charge. Sections 3, 10 and 12 use them. Taught from Tong’s Electromagnetism, sections 2.3 (electrostatic energy) and 2.4.1 (capacitors), and Livingston and Blewett, section 3-8.

The work to add charge. A conductor stands at potential \(V\) above ground. To bring a further small charge \(dQ\) from ground onto it takes work \(V\,dQ\): that is what a potential means, energy per unit charge. As the charge grows, so does the voltage, \(V = Q/C\), so charging from nothing to \(Q\) takes

\[ U = \int_0^Q \frac{Q'}{C}\,dQ' = \frac{Q^2}{2C} = \tfrac{1}{2}CV^2 . \]

This is the stored energy of chapter 1, by the same argument: the energy in the field is the work done to build it. What is new here is the rate. If charge arrives at a steady rate \(I = dQ/dt\), the work per second is the power

\[ P = V\,\frac{dQ}{dt} = VI , \]

in watts for \(V\) in volts and \(I\) in amperes.

Numbers. The Vivitron’s 45 µA of down-charge current at 15 MV carried 675 W up the column. Oak Ridge’s terminal of 600 pF at 20 MV stored 120 kJ; brought up from zero by a steady 100 µA, the power drawn rises from nothing to \(VI = 2\) kW at the top, and averages 1 kW over the two minutes.

The capacitance of a terminal in a tank. For a sphere of radius \(a\) inside a grounded sphere of radius \(b\), the field at the terminal’s surface is \(\mathcal{E}(a) = Vb/[a(b-a)]\) (section 4). The charge on the terminal is its surface area times \(\varepsilon_0 \mathcal{E}(a)\):

\[ Q = 4\pi a^2\,\varepsilon_0\,\frac{Vb}{a(b-a)}, \qquad C = \frac{Q}{V} = \frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,ab}{b - a} . \]

As \(b \to \infty\) this becomes the isolated sphere’s \(4\pi\varepsilon_0 a\). At the best size for the field, \(a = b/2\), it is \(8\pi\varepsilon_0 a\), twice the isolated value: the tank’s wall brings ground close and the terminal must hold more charge for the same voltage. A tank of radius 1 m with a terminal of 0.5 m gives 111 pF. Real terminals, with columns and hoops around them, reach hundreds of picofarads (600 pF at Oak Ridge).

The charge balance. Charge is conserved, so the charge on the terminal changes at the rate at which current flows in, less the rate at which it flows out. With \(Q = CV\) and \(C\) fixed,

\[ C\,\frac{dV}{dt} = \sum I_{in} - \sum I_{out} . \]

The assumption that \(C\) is fixed is worth noticing. If the terminal vibrates, \(C\) changes while \(Q\) does not, so \(V = Q/C\) wobbles: a fractional change \(\Delta C/C\) gives \(\Delta V/V = -\Delta C/C\). That is one reason the ANU (the Australian National University in Canberra, whose 14UD Pelletron tandem appears through this chapter) put its two pickoff plates (metal plates in the tank wall facing the terminal, whose signal follows the terminal voltage at once; section 8) on opposite sides of the terminal, so that a sideways swing raises one signal as it lowers the other.

Livingston and Blewett’s example. A generator at 4 MV with 300 pF, whose control current changes by 100 µA, changes voltage at \(dV/dt = 10^{-4}/(3 \times 10^{-10}) = 333\) kV/s, so 1 per cent (40 kV) takes 0.12 s. A large capacitance makes a terminal steady against fast disturbances and slow to correct.

Charging by induction. Hold a conductor at ground, say a metal pellet touching a grounded pulley, near an electrode, the inductor, at voltage \(V_i\). Laplace’s equation is linear, so the charge on any conductor is a linear combination of the potentials of all the conductors. With the pellet at zero, its charge is proportional to the inductor’s voltage alone:

\[ q = -C_m V_i , \]

where \(C_m\) (F) is the mutual capacitance between pellet and inductor, a positive number fixed by their shapes and spacing. The sign says the pellet takes charge opposite to the inductor: field lines from the inductor end on the pellet, and by Gauss’s law each carries its charge with it. An inductor at a negative voltage (up to \(-60\) kV in the machines of National Electrostatics Corporation, NEC, the Pelletron’s maker (section 10), as the Handbook gives it) draws positive charge onto the pellets.

When the pellet rolls off the pulley, the contact breaks. An isolated conductor keeps its charge, so the pellet carries \(q\) away, though its own potential now changes as it moves. In the terminal the process runs backwards: a pellet touching the terminal’s pulley gives up its charge to it. The charge per pellet is set by a geometry and a voltage, not by a corona discharge, so it is reproducible, and because the transfer happens while metal touches metal, nothing has to jump a gap.

An illustration (not NEC’s design data). A chain rated at 150 µA (Herb, 1971) with 600 pellets passing a second (say 15 m/s and a pellet every 2.5 cm) must carry 0.25 µC on each pellet. At 50 kV on the inductor that needs a mutual capacitance of 5 pF.

Answer

\(P = VI = 14 \times 10^6 \times 150 \times 10^{-6} = 2.1\) kW, on top of friction and other losses in the drive.

Pre-requisite (continued): Ordinary differential equations (linear, constant and varying coefficients)

Pre-requisite (continued). Separation of variables was taught in chapter 2, and first-order linear equations, with the way \(V\,dP/dt = Q - SP\) relaxes to its steady value with a time constant, in chapter 3. This section adds a load that is not linear: where a terminal settles, whether it stays there, and how fast it returns when disturbed. Sections 3 and 8 use it. Taught from Tong’s Classical Mechanics, section 2.1.2 (expanding about an equilibrium point), with Livingston and Blewett, section 3-8, and Lobanov and others (2012) for the numbers.

The equation. A charging current \(I\) (A), steady, flows into a terminal of capacitance \(C\) (F). The current leaving, through the column, by corona and with the beam, depends on the voltage: call it \(I_L(V)\). The charge balance gives

\[ C\,\frac{dV}{dt} = I - I_L(V) . \]

This is first order, but not linear, since \(I_L\) may be any rising curve.

Where it settles. At a steady state \(dV/dt = 0\), so the voltage \(V^*\) satisfies

\[ I_L(V^*) = I . \]

Draw \(I_L(V)\) against \(V\) and a horizontal line at height \(I\): the terminal settles where they cross. Raise the charging current and the crossing moves to a higher voltage; how far depends on how steep the curve is there.

Whether it stays. Write \(V = V^* + v\) with \(v\) small, and expand the load in a Taylor series about \(V^*\), keeping only the first-order term (F1, section 8):

\[ I_L(V^* + v) \approx I_L(V^*) + g\,v, \qquad g = \left.\frac{dI_L}{dV}\right|_{V^*}, \]

with \(g\) in A/V (siemens), the slope of the load curve. Since \(I_L(V^*) = I\), the equation for the deviation is

\[ C\,\frac{dv}{dt} = -g\,v, \qquad v(t) = v(0)\,e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \tau = \frac{C}{g} . \]

This is the decay equation \(dN/dt = -\lambda N\) of F1, section 10 with \(\lambda = g/C\); check by differentiating, \(dv/dt = -v(0)e^{-t/\tau}/\tau = -v/\tau\). The units work: \(C/g\) is farads times volts per ampere, coulombs per ampere, which is seconds.

If the load current rises with voltage (\(g > 0\)), a terminal pushed high draws more current than it is given and comes back: the steady state is stable, and it returns with the time constant \(\tau\). If the load current fell as the voltage rose (\(g < 0\)), a small rise would grow exponentially: a runaway. The approximation is good while \(v\) is small enough that the slope does not change across it.

If the charging current itself wanders by \(\delta I(t)\), the deviation obeys \(C\,dv/dt = -gv + \delta I\). A steady change \(\delta I\) moves the settled voltage by

\[ \delta V = \frac{\delta I}{g} . \]

(Once the deviation has settled, \(dv/dt = 0\) and \(gv = \delta I\).) A steep load holds the terminal tightly and returns it quickly; a shallow one lets it wander and recover slowly. Figure 6.2 shows the picture for the corona load below.

Three loads.

  • A resistor column alone: \(I_L = V/R\), so \(g = 1/R\) and \(\tau = RC\). Here the equation is linear and can be solved from switch-on: \(V(t) = IR\,(1 - e^{-t/RC})\). As an illustration, a divider carrying 15 µA at 14 MV has \(R = 0.93\) TΩ, and with \(C = 300\) pF, \(\tau = 280\) s. A resistor column alone takes minutes to settle.
  • Corona: the current rises very steeply above an onset voltage. As an illustrative model, let \(I_{cor} = k(V - V_0)^2\) above onset \(V_0 = 7\) MV, with 30 µA at 8 MV. Then \(g = 2k(V - V_0) = 60\) µA per MV and \(\tau = C/g = 5\) s for 300 pF. This is the crude natural regulator of section 3.

Figure 6.2. Where a terminal settles, for the illustrative corona load \(I_L = k(V - V_0)^2\) above onset \(V_0 = 7\) MV (30 µA at 8 MV). A charging current of 30 µA meets the curve at 8 MV, where its slope is \(g = 60\) µA per MV. Below the crossing the charging current exceeds the load and the voltage rises; above it the load wins and the voltage falls: the arrows show the terminal pushed back from either side. Raising the charging current to 50 µA moves the crossing to 8.29 MV (exercise C1), only 0.29 MV, because the curve is steep there. - The measured machine: the ANU 14UD’s open-loop response to a step had a time constant of 10.2 s at 8 MV (Lobanov and others, 2012). The paper does not give the terminal’s capacitance; taking Livingston and Blewett’s illustrative 300 pF, the load slope is \(g = C/\tau = 2.9 \times 10^{-11}\) A/V, or 29 nA per kilovolt.

Why feedback is needed. With that slope, a change of only 1 µA in the charging current moves the settled terminal by \(\delta V = 10^{-6}/(2.9 \times 10^{-11}) = 34\) kV, while one part in \(10^4\) at 8 MV is 0.8 kV. The chain’s own fluctuations would spoil the energy many times over. Section 8’s stabiliser and the feedback section before it deal with that.

Exercises

C1. With the corona model above (\(V_0 = 7\) MV, \(k\) set by 30 µA at 8 MV) and a charging current of 50 µA, find the settled voltage, the slope \(g\) there and the time constant for 300 pF.

Answer

\(k = 30 \times 10^{-6}/(10^6)^2 = 3 \times 10^{-17}\) A/V\(^2\). \(V^* = V_0 + \sqrt{I/k} = 7 + 1.29 = 8.29\) MV. \(g = 2k(V^* - V_0) = 2\sqrt{kI} = 7.7 \times 10^{-11}\) A/V (77 µA per MV), and \(\tau = C/g = 3.9\) s. More current puts the terminal higher up the curve, where it is steeper and returns faster.

C2. For the 14UD figures (\(C = 300\) pF, \(\tau = 10.2\) s), a 1 µA step in charging current occurs. How far does the terminal finally move, and how long until it is within 10 per cent of its new value?

Answer

\(\delta V = \delta I/g = \delta I\,\tau/C = 10^{-6} \times 10.2/(3 \times 10^{-10}) = 34\) kV. The deviation from the new value decays as \(e^{-t/\tau}\), so 10 per cent remains after \(t = \tau\ln 10 = 23.5\) s.

3. The terminal: voltage, field and capacitance

An isolated sphere of radius \(a\) carrying charge \(Q\) has

\[ V = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a}, \qquad \mathcal{E} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a^2} = \frac{V}{a}, \qquad C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 a . \]

So it holds at most \(V_{max} = \mathcal{E}_b a\).

The 1931 spheres. With \(a = 0.3\) m in air, \(V_{max} = 0.9\) MV per sphere, 1.8 MV between one positive and one negative sphere. The reported 1.5 MV means each ran at about 0.75 MV. The bound is optimistic: columns and floor were near, and every seam raises the local field.

Round Hill. With \(a = 2.3\) m, \(V_{max} = 6.9\) MV per sphere and \(C = 256\) pF. The machine reached about 5.1 MV between its two spheres; walls, floor and columns brought ground much closer than “isolated” assumes.

Charge balance. The terminal voltage obeys

\[ C\,\frac{dV}{dt} = I - I_{beam} - I_{col}(V) - I_{cor}(V), \]

with \(I_{beam}\) the beam current leaving the terminal, \(I_{col}\) the current down the column’s divider (section 6) and \(I_{cor}\) corona current from the terminal. Unloaded, about 1 mA into one Round Hill sphere’s 256 pF would raise its voltage at about 4 MV per second (8.2 MV per second if all 2.1 mA went to one sphere). Corona current rises very steeply once the surface field reaches onset, so a terminal settles where corona eats the charging current: a crude natural regulator, whose controlled form is the stabiliser of section 8. The capacitance also fixes the energy a spark releases, \(U = \tfrac{1}{2}CV^2\), which is what finally ended the growth of these machines (section 12).

Pre-requisite (continued): Laplace and Poisson equations, uniqueness, boundary-value problems

Pre-requisite (continued). Laplace’s equation, boundary conditions on conductors and the uniqueness of the solution were taught in chapter 2 and, in three dimensions, in chapter 4, where Gauss’s law also gave the fields between nested spheres and cylinders and the best terminal radius. This section solves Laplace’s equation itself between nested spheres and nested cylinders, finds the terminal size that makes the peak field least, extends that to shields at intermediate potentials, and shows how a computer solves it when the shapes have no formula. Section 4 uses all of it. Taught from Tong’s Electromagnetism, sections 2.2.1 (spherical and cylindrical symmetry) and 2.4.2 (boundary value problems), with Humphries, section 9.4, for the shields.

Laplace’s equation with spherical symmetry. Between a spherical terminal and a spherical tank there is no charge, so \(\nabla^2\phi = 0\), and by symmetry \(\phi\) depends only on the distance \(r\) from the centre (spherical coordinates, F2, section 13). The quickest way to the radial form is Gauss’s law. Any sphere of radius \(r\) in the gap encloses the terminal’s charge \(Q\) and nothing else, so the flux \(4\pi r^2 \mathcal{E}_r\) is the same for every \(r\). With \(\mathcal{E}_r = -d\phi/dr\),

\[ r^2\,\frac{d\phi}{dr} = \text{constant}, \qquad \text{equivalently} \qquad \nabla^2\phi = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\frac{d\phi}{dr}\right) = 0 . \]

Call the constant \(-A\). Then \(d\phi/dr = -A/r^2\), and integrating again (the antiderivative of \(r^{-2}\) is \(-r^{-1}\)):

\[ \phi(r) = \frac{A}{r} + B . \]

With cylindrical symmetry. For a long cylindrical terminal on the axis of a cylindrical tank, a coaxial cylinder of radius \(r\) and length \(\ell\) encloses the same charge for every \(r\), so \(2\pi r\ell \mathcal{E}_r\) is constant and \(r\,d\phi/dr\) is constant, say \(A\). Then \(d\phi/dr = A/r\), and since the antiderivative of \(1/r\) is \(\ln r\),

\[ \phi(r) = A\ln r + B . \]

Fitting the boundaries. The conductors fix \(\phi(a) = V\) on the terminal and \(\phi(b) = 0\) on the tank. Two conditions fix the two constants. For spheres, \(A/a + B = V\) and \(A/b + B = 0\); subtracting the second from the first, \(A(1/a - 1/b) = A(b - a)/(ab) = V\), so \(A = Vab/(b - a)\) and \(B = -A/b = -Va/(b - a)\), which is the potential of section 4; differentiating gives its field \(\mathcal{E}(r) = -d\phi/dr = A/r^2 = Vab/[(b-a)r^2]\). For cylinders, \(A\ln a + B = V\) and \(A\ln b + B = 0\) give \(A = -V/\ln(b/a)\), and then

\[ \phi(r) = V\,\frac{\ln(b/r)}{\ln(b/a)}, \qquad \mathcal{E}(r) = \frac{V}{r\ln(b/a)} . \]

By uniqueness these are the fields, not merely possible ones.

The best terminal. The peak field is at the terminal, \(\mathcal{E}(a)\). For spheres it is \(Vb/[a(b-a)]\), least where \(f(a) = a(b - a)\) is greatest. A maximum is where the slope is zero (F1, section 5): \(f'(a) = b - 2a = 0\) gives \(a = b/2\), and \(f'' = -2 < 0\) confirms a maximum of \(f\). For cylinders, \(f(a) = a\ln(b/a) = a\ln b - a\ln a\) has, by the product rule, \(f'(a) = \ln b - \ln a - 1 = \ln(b/a) - 1\), zero where \(\ln(b/a) = 1\), at \(a = b/e\). Both have the same cause: shrink the terminal and its curvature concentrates the field; enlarge it and the gap closes. Figure 6.3 shows both curves.

Figure 6.3. The field at the surface of a terminal of radius \(a\) in a grounded tank of radius \(b\), in units of \(V/b\), from \(\mathcal{E}(a) = Vb/[a(b - a)]\) for spheres and \(V/[a\ln(b/a)]\) for coaxial cylinders. A small terminal is too sharply curved, a large one leaves too narrow a gap; between, each curve has a broad minimum, \(4V/b\) at \(a = b/2\) for spheres and \(eV/b = 2.72\,V/b\) at \(a = b/e\) for cylinders. The minimum is flat: a sphere 20 per cent off the best size raises the field by only 4 per cent.

A tank of radius 1 m at 2.5 MV. The best sphere, 0.5 m, has 10 MV/m at its surface; the field at the tank wall, \(Va/[b(b-a)]\), is only 2.5 MV/m. The gas near the wall works at a quarter of the stress of the gas near the terminal. Shields put that idle gas to work.

Nested electrodes. Put a shield of radius \(b_1\) between terminal and tank and hold it at \(V_1\) (in practice by tying it to the column’s divider at that height). Each gap is now its own boundary-value problem with the solution above: terminal to shield, between \(a\) and \(b_1\) with potentials \(V\) and \(V_1\); shield to tank, between \(b_1\) and \(b\) with \(V_1\) and 0. Each gap’s field is largest at its inner surface:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{spheres:} &\quad \mathcal{E}_a = \frac{(V - V_1)\,b_1}{a(b_1 - a)}, \qquad \mathcal{E}_{b_1} = \frac{V_1\,b}{b_1(b - b_1)}, \\ \text{cylinders:} &\quad \mathcal{E}_a = \frac{V - V_1}{a\ln(b_1/a)}, \qquad \mathcal{E}_{b_1} = \frac{V_1}{b_1\ln(b/b_1)} . \end{aligned} \]

There are three free choices, \(a\), \(b_1\) and \(V_1\), and the task is to make the larger of the two peaks as small as possible. At the best choice the two peaks are equal, for if one were larger, a small change in \(V_1\) would lower it. The minimum can be found in three steps, each a one-variable problem of the kind just solved.

  1. The inner gap. For a given shield radius \(b_1\), the terminal is the best terminal for a “tank” of radius \(b_1\): \(a = b_1/2\) for spheres, so that \(\mathcal{E}_a = 4(V - V_1)/b_1\); \(a = b_1/e\) for cylinders, so that \(\mathcal{E}_a = e(V - V_1)/b_1\).
  2. The shield voltage. Make the two peaks equal. For spheres, \(4(V - V_1)/b_1 = V_1b/[b_1(b - b_1)]\), which solves to \(V - V_1 = Vb/(5b - 4b_1)\), so the common peak is \(\mathcal{E} = 4Vb/[b_1(5b - 4b_1)]\).
  3. The shield radius. Make \(b_1(5b - 4b_1)\) greatest: its derivative \(5b - 8b_1\) vanishes at \(b_1 = 5b/8\), so \(a = 5b/16\), \(V_1 = 0.6\,V\) and \(\mathcal{E} = 4Vb/(25b^2/16) = 64V/(25b) = 2.56\,V/b\). For cylinders the same steps give \(\mathcal{E} = eV/[b_1(1 + eL)]\) with \(L = \ln(b/b_1)\); making \(b_1(1 + eL)\) greatest needs \(1 + eL - e = 0\), so \(L = 1 - 1/e = 0.632\), \(b_1 = b\,e^{-0.632} = 0.5315\,b\), \(V_1 = eLV/(1 + eL) = 0.632\,V\), and the peak \(\mathcal{E} = V/b_1 = 1.882\,V/b\).

In short:

  • spheres: \(a = 0.3125\,b = 5b/16\), \(b_1 = 0.625\,b = 5b/8\), \(V_1 = 0.6\,V\), peak \(2.56\,V/b\) (Humphries’s result, against \(4V/b\) without the shield);
  • cylinders: \(a = 0.1955\,b\), \(b_1 = 0.5315\,b\), \(V_1 = 0.632\,V\), peak \(1.882\,V/b\) (against \(2.718\,V/b\)).

In both, the inner gap is itself an optimum terminal for its own outer radius: \(b_1 = 2a\) for spheres, \(b_1 = e\,a\) for cylinders.

Figure 6.4. The field between a spherical terminal and its tank, in units of \(V/b\), against the distance \(r\) from the centre. Without a shield (red), the best terminal, \(a = b/2\), has \(4V/b\) at its surface but only \(V/b\) at the wall: the outer gas does little. With the best shield (blue), at \(5b/8\) and \(0.6\,V\), the field falls from \(2.56\,V/b\) across the inner gap, jumps back up at the shield and falls again; beyond the shield the two curves coincide. When there is no formula. A real tank holds a column of hoops, a terminal with ends, a belt or chains and support posts, and no formula describes it. The way out is to solve Laplace’s equation on a grid. Expand \(\phi\) about a grid point in a Taylor series, with grid spacing \(h\):

\[ \phi(x + h) + \phi(x - h) = 2\phi(x) + h^2\,\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2} + O(h^4) . \]

Adding the two series cancels the odd terms (\(\pm h\,\partial\phi/\partial x\) and the \(h^3\) terms); \(O(h^4)\), read “of order \(h^4\)”, stands for the terms left over, which shrink like \(h^4\) as the spacing is made smaller.

Do the same in \(y\) and add. The four neighbours of a point sum to \(4\phi + h^2\nabla^2\phi\), so where \(\nabla^2\phi = 0\),

\[ \phi_{i,j} = \tfrac{1}{4}\left(\phi_{i+1,j} + \phi_{i-1,j} + \phi_{i,j+1} + \phi_{i,j-1}\right) : \]

each point is the average of its neighbours. Fix the points on conductors at their potentials, start the rest anywhere, and sweep repeatedly, replacing each value by the average of its neighbours, until nothing changes. This is relaxation; by uniqueness it can only converge to the one solution. Overshooting each update a little (over-relaxation) speeds it greatly.

A check against the formula. A coaxial terminal of radius \(b/e\) in a tank of radius \(b\), solved this way on a square grid (computed for this course), gave the field at \(r = 0.5\,b\) as \(1.954\,V/b\) on a 101 by 101 grid, \(1.961\,V/b\) on 201 by 201 and \(1.986\,V/b\) on 401 by 401, against the exact \(2.000\,V/b\). The error comes mostly from drawing round conductors with square steps. Finite-element codes avoid it by covering the space with triangles (tetrahedra in three dimensions) that follow curved surfaces and are made small where the field is strong. They find the potential that makes the field energy \(\int\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0|\nabla\phi|^2\,dV\) least, which is the same problem: changing \(\phi\) by a small \(\delta\phi\) that vanishes on the conductors changes the energy by \(-\int\varepsilon_0\,\delta\phi\,\nabla^2\phi\,dV\) (integrate by parts), and that is zero for every \(\delta\phi\) only if \(\nabla^2\phi = 0\). The user’s books do not teach these numerical methods; this is the standard approach.

4. A terminal inside a tank

Put a spherical terminal of radius \(a\) inside a grounded spherical tank of radius \(b\). Between them the electrostatic potential \(\phi(r)\) (in V; here \(\phi\) is not an RF phase) solves Laplace’s equation with \(\phi(a) = V\) and \(\phi(b) = 0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \phi(r) &= V\,\frac{a}{b-a}\left(\frac{b}{r} - 1\right), \\ \mathcal{E}(r) &= \frac{V a b}{(b-a)\,r^2}, \qquad \mathcal{E}_{max} = \mathcal{E}(a) = \frac{V b}{a\,(b-a)} . \end{aligned} \]

A small terminal has a high field because it is sharply curved; a large one because the gap is small. For fixed \(b\) and \(V\) the product \(a(b-a)\) is largest at

\[ a = \frac{b}{2}, \qquad \mathcal{E}_{max} = \frac{4V}{b}. \]

For coaxial cylinders \(\mathcal{E}(a) = V/[a\ln(b/a)]\), least at \(a = b/e\), where \(\mathcal{E}_{max} = eV/b \approx 2.72\,V/b\) (Humphries, section 9.4).

Herb’s 1936 tank was 1.7 m in diameter and about 6.1 m (20 feet) long, nearer a cylinder than a sphere, and ran with beam up to 2.6 MV (Herb, 1971). With \(b = 0.85\) m the coaxial optimum needs \(eV/b = 8.3\) MV/m at the terminal, almost three times what air at one atmosphere holds.

Intermediate shields. The field is highest near the terminal because the potential falls fastest there. Add an electrode between terminal and tank held at an intermediate voltage \(V_1\). Humphries solves three nested spheres: terminal \(5b/16\), shield \(5b/8\) at \(V_1 = 3V/5\), peak field \(2.56\,V/b\) instead of \(4\,V/b\). The coaxial version, minimised numerically for this chapter, puts the terminal at \(0.196\,b\) and the shield at \(0.531\,b\) held at \(0.632\,V\), with peak field \(1.88\,V/b\), 69 per cent of the unshielded value.

Daresbury. Its vessel was 45 m high and 8.2 m across (Ball, 1981), with an intermediate shield so that the design could rise from 20 MV to 30 MV. As a coaxial geometry with \(b = 4.1\) m, at 30 MV the best field is 19.9 MV/m unshielded and 13.8 MV/m with one shield.

These are smooth-shape results. Columns, chains and spark gaps add local enhancements, so real tanks are designed by computing the field in the actual geometry.

5. Why pressure helps

Gas breaks down by avalanche: a free electron gains energy from the field over its mean free path, ionises a molecule, and the two electrons repeat it. Paschen’s law (1889; chapter 4, section 2) says that between parallel plates the breakdown voltage depends only on the product of pressure and gap, \(V_b = f(pd)\). On the high-\(pd\) side of the minimum, where tanks work, \(f\) is nearly linear, so

\[ \mathcal{E}_b = \frac{V_b}{d} \approx \left(\frac{\mathcal{E}}{p}\right)_{crit} p . \]

Doubling the gas density (at fixed temperature, doubling the pressure: \(p = nk_BT\), F6, section 10) halves the mean free path, so an electron needs twice the field to gain the same energy between collisions. This holds for uniform fields and clean electrodes at moderate pressure. At high pressure breakdown starts from surface roughness and dust instead; above a few bar the strength of SF\(_6\) rises more slowly than in proportion to the pressure.

Chemistry helps too. Electronegative molecules capture free electrons and take them out of the avalanche. Herb found by accident that a little carbon tetrachloride in air raised its strength greatly; later tanks used nitrogen with carbon dioxide, Freon, and finally sulphur hexafluoride, which at one atmosphere holds about 7.5 to 8.9 MV/m against 3 MV/m for air. The upper figure is the uniform-field limit \((\mathcal{E}/p)_{crit} = 88.5\) kV/(cm bar), about three times air’s 27 kV/(cm bar) (Kuffel, Zaengl and Kuffel, section 5.10). In a whole generator the gain is smaller: in MIT’s test generator SF\(_6\) up to 200 psi (pounds per square inch; 1 psi = 6895 Pa, so 200 psi is 13.8 bar) almost doubled the voltage reached with air at the same pressure (Livingston and Blewett, section 3-3).

A measured comparison. At Chalk River (Ashbaugh, McAdam and James, 1965) a 3 MV Van de Graaff and an EN tandem ran as well with SF\(_6\) at 60 psig as with nitrogen-carbon dioxide at 225 psig, that is 5.15 bar against 16.5 bar absolute (psig is pressure in psi above the atmosphere, gauge pressure; add 14.7 psi for the absolute pressure: \((60 + 14.7) \times 6895\) Pa = 5.15 bar), a factor of 3.2. The tandem typically ran at 40 psig up to 5 MV and 60 psig up to 7 MV, and did not spark below 7.5 MV in a test. A modern NEC tank is rated for 125 psig and filled with about 80 psig of SF\(_6\) (6.5 bar absolute).

6. The column

The terminal must be held up, and the potential of the space below it fixed so that the field is uniform along everything that spans it. Herb, Parkinson and Kerst’s 1937 machine introduced the answer used ever since, the column: the insulating supports, the belt and the accelerating tube enclosed in a stack of metal hoops from ground to terminal, each hoop at its share of the voltage. Inside the stack the field is nearly uniform and axial, so no insulator sees a concentration.

The hoops are held at their potentials by a divider, a chain of elements in series from terminal to ground that shares the voltage among the gaps, as resistors in series share a voltage in proportion to their resistances (F4, section 9). It is of one of two kinds:

  • Resistors, one per gap. The divider current flows all the time and must be much larger than stray currents to the hoops.
  • Corona points, needles on each hoop facing the next. Corona current rises steeply with the gap’s voltage, so each gap settles near the voltage at which its needles carry the common current. Nothing burns out in a spark, but the division drifts with gas condition.

Herb’s biographical memoir lists what came from Wisconsin and stayed: aluminium hoops around the tube, the gradient fixed by corona points or resistors, a rotating-vane generating voltmeter, and compressed electronegative gas.

Column load. At 15 MV the Vivitron needed 45 µA of down-charge current while a corona current of 22 µA was drawn from the terminal (Foehrenbacher and others, 1998). Every microampere drawn from a terminal at 15 MV costs 15 W, so those 22 µA took 330 W of the 675 W (\(15\ \text{MV} \times 45\ \text{µA}\)) that the down-charge current carried up; the rest went to the column’s resistor divider and the beam.

Pre-requisite (continued): Relativistic motion in electric and magnetic fields

Pre-requisite (continued). The Lorentz force law \(d\mathbf{p}/dt = q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})\) (F4, section 10) with \(\mathbf{p} = \gamma m\mathbf{v}\), and motion in a magnetic field, were taught in chapter 1. This section adds three small results the accelerating tube depends on: a particle released at rest in a uniform field, the sideways kick that a short transverse field gives a fast particle, and why a weak magnetic field sorts electrons from ions. Section 7 uses them. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz volume 2, sections 20 and 21 (motion in constant uniform electric and magnetic fields), and Tong’s Electromagnetism, section 5.4.2 (motion in constant fields).

Released at rest in a uniform field. With \(\mathbf{B} = 0\) and \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) constant, \(d\mathbf{p}/dt = q\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) integrates to \(\mathbf{p}(t) = q\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\,t\) for a particle that starts at rest. The momentum always points along \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\), and so does the velocity, since \(\mathbf{p} = \gamma m\mathbf{v}\) at any speed. The particle moves in a straight line along the field: along a field line, which in a uniform field is straight.

In an inclined-field tube. Suppose a section of length \(L\) along the axis has its field tilted at \(\theta\) to the axis. An electron set free at rest in it runs along the tilt, so by the time it has advanced \(L\) along the axis it has moved \(L\tan\theta\) sideways: 25 mm for \(L = 0.1\) m and \(\theta = 14^\circ\). Over that path it gains only \(e\mathcal{E}L/\cos\theta\), about 200 keV in 2 MV/m, not the megavolts of the whole tube, and the electrodes catch it.

The kick of a short transverse field. Now a fast particle of charge \(q\), momentum \(p\) and speed \(v\) crosses the same section along the axis. The field’s component across the axis is \(\mathcal{E}_\perp = \mathcal{E}\sin\theta\). Two approximations make the result simple: the deflection is small, so the particle stays near the axis and crosses the section in time \(\Delta t = L/v\); and its energy changes little in the section (\(W \gg q\mathcal{E}L\)). Then the sideways momentum it picks up is force times time,

\[ \Delta p_\perp = q\mathcal{E}\sin\theta\,\frac{L}{v}, \]

and its direction turns through the angle

\[ \Delta x' = \frac{\Delta p_\perp}{p} = \frac{q\mathcal{E}L\sin\theta}{pv} . \]

The product \(pv\) has the units of energy. At low speed \(pv = mv^2 = 2W\), which gives the result of section 7,

\[ \Delta x' \approx \frac{q\mathcal{E}L\sin\theta}{2W} . \]

At any speed, with \(\beta = v/c\), \(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}\) (so \(\beta^2 = (\gamma^2 - 1)/\gamma^2\)) and \(W = (\gamma - 1)mc^2\), \(pv = \gamma mv^2 = \gamma mc^2\beta^2 = mc^2(\gamma^2 - 1)/\gamma = mc^2(\gamma - 1)(\gamma + 1)/\gamma = W(1 + 1/\gamma)\), which tends to \(2W\) when \(\gamma \to 1\).

Numbers. For a 1 MeV proton in 2 MV/m over 0.1 m at \(14^\circ\), \(q\mathcal{E}L\sin\theta = 48.4\) keV and \(\Delta x' = 24.2\) mrad (the relativistic form changes it by less than 0.1 per cent). At 5 MeV it is 4.8 mrad: the kick falls as the ion gains energy down the tube. The next section, tilted the other way, gives an equal and opposite kick, so the ion leaves each pair of sections nearly on its old line.

Magnetic fields sort by momentum. In a magnetic field a particle bends on radius \(\rho = p/(qB)\), from the rigidity of chapter 1. At the same kinetic energy and low speed, \(p = \sqrt{2mW}\), so

\[ \frac{p_{ion}}{p_e} = \sqrt{\frac{m_{ion}}{m_e}} , \]

43 for a proton and 148 for a carbon-12 ion. The electron bends that many times more sharply.

In a tube. Take an illustrative transverse field of 5 mT over a 0.1 m section. A 10 keV electron has \(B\rho = 3.4 \times 10^{-4}\) T m and a radius of 6.8 cm: it turns round inside the section. A 100 keV electron has a radius of 22 cm and is turned through about 0.45 rad. A 1 MeV proton has a radius of 29 m and is turned through 3.5 mrad; a 5 MeV iodine ion with charge 5+, 0.7 mrad. Electrons born in the tube start slow, so a weak field drives them into the electrodes before they gain much energy, while the ions hardly notice it, and alternating its direction from section to section cancels even that.

Exercises

E1. A carbon ion with charge \(2+\) and 3 MeV crosses a 0.05 m section where a field of 3 MV/m is tilted at \(10^\circ\). Estimate its angular kick.

Answer

\(\Delta x' \approx q\mathcal{E}L\sin\theta/(2W)\) with \(q\mathcal{E}L\sin\theta = 2 \times 3\ \text{MV/m} \times 0.05\ \text{m} \times 0.1736 = 52.1\) keV, so \(\Delta x' = 52.1/(2 \times 3000) = 8.7\) mrad. The doubled charge doubles the kick for the same energy.

E2. An electron and a proton each have 50 keV in a field of 10 mT. Find their radii of curvature.

Answer

\(\rho = p/(eB)\) with \(pc = \sqrt{W(W + 2mc^2)}\). Electron: \(pc = 231\) keV, \(\rho = 7.7\) cm. Proton: \(pc = 9.69\) MeV, \(\rho = 3.2\) m. The ratio, 42, is close to \(\sqrt{m_p/m_e} = 43\); it is a little smaller because the 50 keV electron is already mildly relativistic.

7. The accelerating tube

The tube runs down the centre of the column: insulating rings (glass, later alumina) bonded to metal electrodes, each electrode tied to a column hoop, so each vacuum gap sees only its share. Herb’s 1934 tube was 6 cm Pyrex sections sealed to brass with red sealing wax; NEC’s standard tubes in 1971 were alumina bonded to titanium, in sections of 1.27 cm modulus, three sections per 1 MV column module (Herb, 1971).

Two effects make a long tube hold less per metre than a short one.

The total-voltage effect. Vacuum gaps break down by field emission, electrons pulled out of microscopic points on the metal by the strong local field (chapter 4, section 5), but the voltage a vacuum gap holds grows more slowly than its length. Cranberg (1952) proposed that a loose particle of electrode material, accelerated across the gap, strikes the far electrode hard enough to start a discharge, which gives a breakdown voltage rising as the square root of the gap: a gap that holds 100 kV at 1 cm would hold 1 MV at 1 m. Anything that crosses many sections of a tube (a particle, an electron, an ion) gains the total voltage, so the holding of a tube depends on its total voltage, not only on the gradient per gap.

Electron loading. In a tube carrying positive ions down from a positive terminal, any free electron (from residual gas ionised by the beam, or from an ion striking an electrode) is accelerated up toward the terminal, arrives with megavolts, makes X-rays as it stops in the metal (F7, section 5), and knocks out positive ions that travel down and free more electrons. At 10 MV, each 100 µA of such electrons takes 1 kW from the charging system. The terminal stalls however hard it is charged.

Inclined fields. The cure, developed by Van de Graaff’s group (Van de Graaff, Rose and Wittkower, 1962; Purser, Galejs, Rose, Van de Graaff and Wittkower, 1965), tilts the electrodes so that the field in each section is inclined at angle \(\theta\) to the axis, alternating from section to section. An electron born at rest moves along the field line and drifts sideways by \(L\tan\theta\) over a section of length \(L\): with \(\theta = 14^\circ\) (the Vivitron’s tube electrodes alternated at \(\pm 14^\circ\)) and an illustrative \(L = 0.1\) m, 25 mm, enough to hit an electrode before it gains much energy. A beam ion entering with energy \(W \gg q\mathcal{E}L\) is deflected only through

\[ \Delta x' \approx \frac{q\mathcal{E}L\sin\theta}{2W}, \]

24 mrad for a 1 MeV ion in an illustrative 2 MV/m, and the next section turns it back (figure 6.5). Later tandems added weak transverse magnetic fields, which bend light electrons strongly and heavy ions hardly at all. NEC’s very clean metal-ceramic tubes held 1 MV per module without inclined fields (Herb, 1971).

In each section the electrodes are tilted by \(\theta = 14^\circ\) (drawn true), and the field, at right angles to them, is tilted by \(\theta\) from the axis: downwards in section A, upwards in B. An electron set free at rest is pulled against the field, up the tilted field line, drifts sideways by \(L\tan\theta\) over a length \(L\), and strikes an electrode outside the central hole before it has gained much energy. Pre-requisite: Vacuum tubes and amplifiers

Pre-requisite. This section explains the two pieces of electronics at the heart of a terminal stabiliser: the vacuum triode, whose grid voltage sets the current through the corona needles, and the logarithmic amplifier, which turns two slit currents into a signal that depends on where the beam is and not on how intense it is. Section 8 uses both, and the feedback section that follows builds the loop from them. Taught from Chao and others, Handbook, section 7.3.3 (Brennan, tetrode amplifiers: amplification factor, anode resistance, transconductance), Livingston and Blewett, section 3-8 (corona control), Landau and Lifshitz volume 5, section 37 (the Boltzmann distribution), and the Child–Langmuir law of chapter 2, section 7.

The idea. A vacuum diode is a hot cathode facing an anode in vacuum. Electrons boil off the cathode, and the anode, if positive, draws them across. Current flows one way only, and how much flows is set not by the cathode’s temperature but by space charge: the electrons in the gap push back on those leaving the cathode. That is the Child–Langmuir law of chapter 2, a current density

\[ J = \frac{4}{9}\,\varepsilon_0\sqrt{\frac{2e}{m_e}}\;\frac{V^{3/2}}{d^2} \]

for a gap \(d\) (m) at voltage \(V\) (V). A triode puts a third electrode, the grid, between them: a mesh of fine wires close to the cathode. Electrons pass between the wires. Because the grid is close to the cathode, a small change in its voltage changes the field at the cathode far more than the same change on the distant anode. So a few volts on the grid control a current that the anode, at hundreds or thousands of volts, collects. Kept negative with respect to the cathode, the grid collects no electrons itself, so it controls the current while drawing almost none.

The triode law. What draws electrons off the cathode is the field at its surface, which is set by the charge induced on it. Laplace’s equation is linear, so with the cathode at zero that charge is a weighted sum of the grid and anode voltages:

\[ q_k = -(C_{gk}V_g + C_{ak}V_a), \]

with \(C_{gk}\) and \(C_{ak}\) the mutual capacitances (F) of grid and anode to the cathode. Factor out \(C_{gk}\): the cathode responds to the combination

\[ V_g + \frac{V_a}{\mu}, \qquad \mu = \frac{C_{gk}}{C_{ak}} . \]

The amplification factor \(\mu\) is large, tens to thousands, because the grid is near the cathode and screens it from the anode. The triode then behaves like a diode whose voltage is this effective voltage, and Child–Langmuir gives the anode current

\[ I_a = k\left(V_g + \frac{V_a}{\mu}\right)^{3/2}, \]

with \(k\) (A/V\(^{3/2}\)) the perveance, fixed by the geometry. When the bracket is zero or negative the tube is cut off and no current flows. The Handbook’s tetrode formula is the same with a further term for the screen grid. The approximations: the cathode emits more than space charge allows (space-charge limited), the grid is negative so it draws no current, and the electrons’ small initial speeds are ignored.

Three small-signal numbers. Around a working point, small changes are related by three partial derivatives (each taken with one voltage or current held fixed, written as a subscript; F1, section 9):

\[ g_m = \left(\frac{\partial I_a}{\partial V_g}\right)_{V_a}, \qquad r_a = \left(\frac{\partial V_a}{\partial I_a}\right)_{V_g}, \qquad \mu = -\left(\frac{\partial V_a}{\partial V_g}\right)_{I_a} . \]

The transconductance \(g_m\) (A/V) says how much current a grid volt buys; the anode resistance \(r_a\) (Ω) how little the current cares about the anode voltage. A small change is \(\delta I_a = g_m\,\delta V_g + \delta V_a/r_a\); holding \(I_a\) fixed gives \(\delta V_a/\delta V_g = -g_m r_a\), so

\[ g_m = \frac{\mu}{r_a} . \]

From the triode law, \(g_m = \tfrac{3}{2}k(V_g + V_a/\mu)^{1/2}\), and since the bracket is \((I_a/k)^{2/3}\), its square root is \((I_a/k)^{1/3}\) and \(g_m = \tfrac{3}{2}k^{2/3}I_a^{1/3}\).

The triode as a valve for corona current. In a stabiliser, a group of corona needles in the tank wall faces the terminal. They are wired to the anode of a high-voltage triode whose cathode is at ground (the ANU used a 6BK4). Corona current from the terminal to the needles must pass through the triode to reach ground, and no more can flow than the triode passes. Open the grid a little: the triode carries more current, the needles’ potential falls towards ground, the field at their tips rises, and the corona current grows until it equals what the triode carries. Close the grid and the needles float up until the corona weakens to match. The triode sets the current; the needles’ voltage follows. Because \(r_a\) is large, the current hardly depends on the needles’ voltage, so the needles behave as a current source controlled by the grid. Livingston and Blewett give control currents of a few hundred microamperes, up to 1 mA.

An illustration, not the data of any particular tube. Take \(k = 10^{-6}\) A/V\(^{3/2}\) and a standing current of 20 µA. The effective voltage is \((20)^{2/3} = 7.4\) V and \(g_m = \tfrac{3}{2} \times 20\ \text{µA}/7.4\ \text{V} = 4.1\) µA/V. One volt on the grid changes the corona current by 4.1 µA, which on a 300 pF terminal changes the voltage at \(4.1 \times 10^{-6}/(3 \times 10^{-10}) = 13.6\) kV/s.

Why a logarithmic amplifier. After the analysing magnet, two slit jaws stand at \(\pm g\) from the beam’s ideal position, and each catches the edge of the beam. If the beam’s energy is high, it moves towards one jaw (section 8). The obvious error signal is the difference of the two currents, \(I_1 - I_2\). But that is proportional to the beam’s intensity: if the ion source doubles its output, the difference doubles, and the stabiliser moves the terminal although the energy has not changed. The ratio \(I_1/I_2\) does not have this fault, and the difference of logarithms is the logarithm of the ratio:

\[ \ln I_1 - \ln I_2 = \ln\frac{I_1}{I_2} . \]

How the ratio depends on position. Let the beam’s profile be Gaussian (F6, section 5) with width \(\sigma\) (m) and centre \(x_0\), and the jaws at \(\pm g\) with \(g\) several times \(\sigma\). A jaw catches the tail beyond it, and far out a Gaussian tail falls as \(\exp[-(g \mp x_0)^2/2\sigma^2]\) times a slowly varying factor. Taking the ratio, the intensity cancels, and since \((g + x_0)^2 - (g - x_0)^2 = 4gx_0\),

\[ \ln\frac{I_1}{I_2} \approx \frac{(g + x_0)^2 - (g - x_0)^2}{2\sigma^2} = \frac{2g\,x_0}{\sigma^2}, \]

plus a smaller term from the slowly varying factors, about \(2x_0/g\). The signal is nearly linear in the displacement and independent of intensity.

At the Vivitron’s slits. Jaws at \(\pm 1.3\) mm passed 97 per cent of the beam; if all the loss were on these two jaws, the profile would be a Gaussian of \(\sigma = 0.6\) mm. A shift of 0.1 mm (an energy change of \(1.9 \times 10^{-5}\) with \(D_x = 10.6\) m) changes \(\ln(I_1/I_2)\) by 0.84, computed exactly (the simple formula gives 0.72), whatever the beam current.

How a circuit takes a logarithm. It uses a semiconductor junction, a diode or a transistor. A junction is the boundary between two layers of silicon, one laced with atoms that give up spare free electrons and the other with atoms that take electrons in; where they meet, a step of potential forms that the carriers of charge must climb to cross (the silicon diodes of chapter 5 are such junctions). Its current rises exponentially with the voltage across it:

\[ I = I_s\left(e^{eV/k_BT} - 1\right), \]

with \(I_s\) (A) a small saturation current, \(k_B\) Boltzmann’s constant and \(T\) the temperature (K). The exponential comes from the Boltzmann factor (F6, section 13): carriers must climb a potential step to cross the junction, the fraction with enough energy to do so is proportional to \(e^{-\varepsilon/k_BT}\) for a step of height \(\varepsilon\), and a forward voltage \(V\) lowers the step by \(eV\). Placed in an amplifier’s feedback path (an amplifier of very high gain whose output is connected back to its input through the junction, so that it drives its output to whatever voltage lets all the input current flow through the junction), the junction develops whatever voltage makes it carry the input current. For \(I_{in} \gg I_s\) the \(-1\) is negligible, \(e^{eV/k_BT} = I_{in}/I_s\), and taking logarithms the output is

\[ V_{out} = \frac{k_BT}{e}\ln\frac{I_{in}}{I_s} . \]

At 300 K, \(k_BT/e = 25.9\) mV, or 59.5 mV per factor of ten in current. The ANU’s slit pre-amplifiers covered 1 nA to 100 µA, five decades, in about 0.30 V of junction voltage before further gain. Two matched junctions at the same temperature have the same \(I_s\), and their difference, \((k_BT/e)\ln(I_1/I_2)\), does not depend on it. The price is speed at low current: the ANU’s pre-amplifier bandwidth was about 250 Hz at 100 nA and 5 kHz at 1 µA (Lobanov and others, 2012), because the junction’s small-signal resistance, \(k_BT/(eI)\), is large when the current is small.

Pre-requisite: Laplace transform, feedback and stability

Pre-requisite. This section teaches the language of a regulating loop: the Laplace transform, the transfer function, the loop gain, and the stability margin that a delay eats into. Section 8 uses it to explain why a terminal stabiliser divides errors by a factor of hundreds yet cannot act faster than about 1 to 10 Hz, and what the capacitive pickoff adds. The user’s books do not teach the Laplace transform itself; the method here is the standard one. The accelerator side is from Wangler, section 5.8 (feedback on cavity fields and the bandwidth that loop delays allow), Chao and others, Handbook, section 5.14 (Montag, vibration control: a magnet’s response to forces written as a transfer function in the Laplace domain), and the measurements of Lobanov and others (2012) on the ANU 14UD.

The idea. Left alone, a terminal settles where its charging current and its load current balance, and the previous sections showed how far it drifts: 1 µA of change in the chain’s current moves the 14UD by 34 kV. Feedback measures the voltage, compares it with the wanted value, and corrects: the error drives the triode, the triode sets the corona current, the corona current pulls the terminal back. A loop like this has parts that each take time (the terminal integrates current, the corona charge takes time to arrive, filters smooth the signals), and in the time domain combining them means solving coupled differential equations. The Laplace transform turns each part into a factor and the loop into a product.

The Laplace transform. For a function \(f(t)\) that starts at \(t = 0\), define

\[ F(s) = \int_0^\infty f(t)\,e^{-st}\,dt , \]

where \(s\) is a complex number (F5, sections 1 and 2) with units of inverse seconds. Multiplying by \(e^{-st}\) and integrating over all time turns a function of time into a function of \(s\); the point, as with the turning arrows of F5, is that derivatives become multiplications. Four results are all this section needs. (The integrals are worked as in F1, section 7; each assumes the real part of \(s\) is large enough that \(e^{-st}\) kills the integrand as \(t \to \infty\).)

  • A step, \(f = 1\) for \(t > 0\): \(F = \int_0^\infty e^{-st}\,dt = [-e^{-st}/s]_0^\infty = 1/s\).
  • A decay, \(f = e^{-at}\): \(F = \int_0^\infty e^{-(s+a)t}\,dt = 1/(s + a)\).
  • A derivative: integrate by parts (F1, section 7), \(\int_0^\infty f'e^{-st}\,dt = [fe^{-st}]_0^\infty + s\int_0^\infty fe^{-st}\,dt\), so the transform of \(df/dt\) is \(sF(s) - f(0)\). Differentiating becomes multiplying by \(s\).
  • A delay by \(t_d\), the same signal arriving \(t_d\) later, \(f(t - t_d)\), zero before \(t_d\): substituting \(t' = t - t_d\), \(\int_{t_d}^\infty f(t - t_d)e^{-st}\,dt = \int_0^\infty f(t')e^{-s(t' + t_d)}\,dt' = e^{-st_d}F(s)\).

The terminal as a transfer function. Work with small deviations from the settled state, so that the previous section’s linear equation applies: \(v\) is the terminal’s deviation (V) and \(i\) a change in current into it (A), both zero at \(t = 0\):

\[ C\,\frac{dv}{dt} + g\,v = i . \]

Transform it: \((Cs + g)V(s) = I(s)\), so

\[ V(s) = G(s)\,I(s), \qquad G(s) = \frac{1/g}{1 + s\tau}, \qquad \tau = \frac{C}{g} . \]

\(G(s)\), in V/A, is the terminal’s transfer function: output over input, whatever the input. As a check, a step of current \(I_0\) has \(I(s) = I_0/s\), and

\[ V(s) = \frac{I_0}{g}\,\frac{1}{s(1 + s\tau)} = \frac{I_0}{g}\left(\frac{1}{s} - \frac{1}{s + 1/\tau}\right), \]

where the fraction has been split in two (check by putting the right-hand side over a common denominator: \(1/(s + 1/\tau) = \tau/(1 + s\tau)\) and \(1/s - \tau/(1 + s\tau) = [1 + s\tau - s\tau]/[s(1 + s\tau)]\)), and that, read back through the step and decay results, is \(v(t) = (I_0/g)(1 - e^{-t/\tau})\): the answer of the previous section, reached by algebra.

Frequency response. Drive the system with a sinusoid. Once transients die away, the response is a sinusoid at the same frequency, multiplied in size by \(|G(i\omega)|\) and shifted in phase by \(\arg G(i\omega)\): put \(s = i\omega\), exactly as the driven oscillator of F5, section 5 was solved with \(d/dt \to i\omega\). For the terminal, \(G(i\omega) = (1/g)/(1 + i\omega\tau)\); the complex number \(1 + i\omega\tau\) has modulus \(\sqrt{1 + \omega^2\tau^2}\) and argument \(\arctan\omega\tau\), and dividing by it divides the modulus and subtracts the argument, so

\[ |G(i\omega)| = \frac{1/g}{\sqrt{1 + \omega^2\tau^2}}, \qquad \arg G = -\arctan\omega\tau . \]

Below the corner frequency \(f_c = 1/(2\pi\tau)\) the terminal responds fully; above it the response falls as \(1/(\omega C)\) with a lag of \(90^\circ\), since the terminal then just integrates current. For the 14UD’s \(\tau = 10.2\) s the corner is at 0.0156 Hz: everything that matters to the stabiliser happens far above it.

A delay has \(|e^{-i\omega t_d}| = 1\) but a phase lag of \(\omega t_d\) radians, which grows without limit as the frequency rises. The ANU measured 31 ms between a step on the triode’s grid and the start of the terminal’s response: the transit time of the corona charge drifting through the gas from needles to terminal. It was shorter with the needles closer and depended on the gas pressure.

Closing the loop. Let a disturbance current \(d\) act on the terminal (the chain’s fluctuation, say), and let the stabiliser feed back a correction current \(-Kv\), with \(K\) in A/V the gain from measured voltage to corona current, arriving after the delay. Figure 6.6 draws the loop. In transforms, the current into the terminal is the disturbance less the delayed correction, and the terminal turns current into voltage through \(G\); collecting the \(V\) terms, \(V(1 + KGe^{-st_d}) = GD\):

\[ V = G\,(D - Ke^{-st_d}V) \quad\Rightarrow\quad V = \frac{G}{1 + L(s)}\,D, \qquad L(s) = K\,G(s)\,e^{-st_d} . \]

\(L(s)\) is the loop gain, a pure number: the factor a signal is multiplied by on its way round the loop. Every disturbance is divided by \(1 + L\). At slow frequencies \(L(0) = K/g = L_0\), and a steady disturbance is cut \(1 + L_0\) times. The loop also quickens the response. Ignoring the delay, \(L = L_0/(1 + s\tau)\), and the closed loop’s response is

\[ \frac{G}{1 + L} = \frac{1/g}{1 + s\tau + L_0} = \frac{1/[g(1 + L_0)]}{1 + s\,\tau/(1 + L_0)} : \]

the same form as \(G\) itself, with the gain \(1/g\) divided by \(1 + L_0\) and the time constant \(\tau\) divided by \(1 + L_0\). So the closed loop’s time constant is \(\tau/(1 + L_0)\).

Figure 6.6. The terminal stabiliser as a loop. A disturbance current \(D\), such as a fluctuation of the chain’s charge, and the correction current meet at the terminal, which turns current into a voltage deviation \(V\) through its transfer function \(G(s)\). The slits and logarithmic amplifiers measure \(V\); the triode and corona needles turn it into a correction current \(KV\), which is subtracted after the time \(t_d\) the corona charge takes to drift through the gas. A signal that goes once round the loop is multiplied by \(L(s) = K\,G(s)\,e^{-st_d}\).

Why the gain cannot be endless: stability. The danger is \(L(i\omega) = -1\). Then the correction, delayed by half a period, arrives in step with the error it was meant to cancel and at the same size, and an oscillation feeds itself. The working rule (the Nyquist criterion, in the simple form that holds when the loop gain falls with frequency): at the crossover frequency \(\omega_x\), where \(|L| = 1\), the phase of \(L\) must be above \(-180^\circ\), and the excess, the phase margin, should be some 30 to 45 degrees in practice (the ANU paper quotes 30 to 40).

For the terminal well above its corner, \(\omega\tau \gg 1\), so \(1 + i\omega\tau \approx i\omega\tau\) and \(L \approx (L_0/i\omega\tau)\,e^{-i\omega t_d}\). Dividing by \(i = e^{i\pi/2}\) is a phase of \(-90^\circ\) (the terminal integrating the current), and \(e^{-i\omega t_d}\) adds \(-\omega t_d\) (the delay). The phase margin is \(180^\circ\) plus the phase of \(L\) at crossover, \(180^\circ - 90^\circ - \omega_x t_d = 90^\circ - \omega_x t_d\) (with \(\omega_x t_d\) turned from radians to degrees by multiplying by 57.3). So

\[ \omega_x t_d = \frac{\pi}{2} - \text{margin} . \]

With \(t_d = 31\) ms, a \(45^\circ\) margin allows crossover at \(f_x = 1/(8t_d) = 4.0\) Hz, and a \(30^\circ\) margin 5.4 Hz. Raising the gain until the margin is gone would make the loop oscillate at \(1/(4t_d) = 8.1\) Hz. That is the paper’s statement that the corona loop’s cut-off is below 10 Hz because of the transit time. Since \(|L| \approx L_0/(\omega\tau)\) above the corner, \(|L| = 1\) at crossover means \(L_0 = \omega_x\tau = f_x/f_c\) (because \(f_c = 1/(2\pi\tau)\)), and crossover at 4.0 Hz means \(L_0 \approx 4.0/0.0156 = 260\): slow disturbances cut 260 times, a closed-loop time constant of 40 ms.

The full fitted response. The ANU fitted the measured response at 14 MV with \(G_p(s) = (1 + 0.01s)/(22.63s^2 + 85.24s + 1)\), normalised, which has two time constants, 85 s and 0.27 s. With the 31 ms delay added, a \(45^\circ\) margin then allows crossover at only 0.52 Hz (computed for this course): the second lag eats margin. NEC quotes a bandwidth of about 1 Hz for its stabilisers, the same order.

A simulation. Computing the step response of the simple loop (10.2 s terminal, 31 ms delay) for this course: with \(L_0 = 256\) the error settles at \(1/257\) of its open-loop value after an overshoot of 28 per cent and a second of 2 per cent; with \(L_0 = 400\) (about \(20^\circ\) of margin) it overshoots by 71 per cent and rings at about 7 Hz, each swing less than half the one before; with \(L_0 = 600\), beyond the limit of 517, the oscillation grows.

What a proportional loop leaves. Dividing by \(1 + L_0\) leaves a small steady error, \(1/(1 + L_0)\) of the open-loop shift. Adding an integral term, \(K_i/s\), to the controller (dividing by \(s\) is integrating in time, the derivative rule read backwards: the controller then also pushes in proportion to the error accumulated so far, which keeps growing until the error is zero) makes the loop gain infinite at zero frequency and removes the steady error; it costs phase at low frequencies, so it is kept well below crossover.

The derivative term from a fast pickoff. A plate facing the terminal with capacitance \(C_p\) to it holds charge \(C_pV_T\), so the current to it is

\[ i_p = C_p\,\frac{dV_T}{dt} : \]

a signal proportional to the rate of change of the terminal voltage, available at once, with no rotating vane and no filtering. In transforms it is \(sC_pV\), a derivative term. A controller of the form \(K(1 + st_D)\) has a phase lead of \(\arctan\omega t_D\), which offsets some of the lags of the terminal and the delay: with \(t_D = 0.05\) s the lead at 4 Hz is \(51^\circ\). In practice the slow signals, from the generating voltmeter or the slits, rule below a crossover near 1 Hz (0.9 Hz at the ANU), and the capacitive pickoff rules above it. With the pickoff on, the ANU found fast ripple of about 52 V full width at half maximum (the width of a peaked distribution measured where it falls to half its height, F6, section 5) at 13.5 MV, and slow wandering of 3.9 kV.

8. Measuring and holding the energy

The analysing magnet. After the tube a bending magnet (usually 90°) turns a particle of momentum \(p\) and charge \(q\) on radius \(\rho\) with \(B\rho = p/q\) (the magnetic rigidity of chapter 1). A particle with a little more momentum bends on a slightly larger radius and comes out of the magnet a little to the outside. Beyond the magnet a pair of slit jaws, the image slits, stands where the magnet focuses the beam to a narrow line. A relative momentum deviation \(\delta = \Delta p/p\) moves the particle there by \(\Delta x = D_x\delta\), where the dispersion \(D_x\) (m) is a length fixed by the magnet’s radius and the distances to it: of the order of the bending radius or several times it. To turn momentum into energy, differentiate \((W + mc^2)^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4\): \(2(W + mc^2)\,dW = 2pc^2\,dp\), and since \(W + mc^2 = \gamma mc^2\), \(dW = [p/(\gamma m)]\,dp = v\,dp\). Then \(dW/W = (pv/W)\,dp/p\), with \(pv/W = \gamma m v^2/[(\gamma - 1)mc^2] = \gamma\beta^2/(\gamma - 1) = (\gamma + 1)/\gamma\) (using \(\beta^2 = (\gamma^2 - 1)/\gamma^2\) and \(\gamma^2 - 1 = (\gamma - 1)(\gamma + 1)\)). So \(dW/W = (1 + 1/\gamma)\,dp/p\), and

\[ \frac{\Delta W}{W} = \left(1 + \frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\frac{\Delta x}{D_x}, \]

which is \(2\Delta x/D_x\) for slow ions. At the Vivitron \(D_x = 10.6\) m, so 1 mm at the slits is \(\Delta W/W = 1.9 \times 10^{-4}\); with slits at \(\pm 1.3\) mm the measured peak-to-peak energy stability was \(2.0 \times 10^{-4}\).

The generating voltmeter. A grounded rotating vane alternately covers and uncovers an insulated plate in the tank wall facing the terminal. Uncovered, the plate holds induced charge \(\varepsilon_0 \mathcal{E}_n A\), with \(\mathcal{E}_n\) the field at the wall and \(A\) the exposed area. The current to the plate is

\[ i = \varepsilon_0 \mathcal{E}_n \frac{dA}{dt}, \]

an alternating signal (positive while the plate is being uncovered, negative while it is covered) which is rectified, turned one way by diodes (chapter 5), and whose rectified amplitude is proportional to \(\mathcal{E}_n\) and so to \(V\) (Kirkpatrick and Miyake, 1932; Humphries, section 9.14). As an illustration, a wall field of 2 MV/m on a 0.01 m\(^2\) plate induces 0.18 µC, and four windows at 50 revolutions a second give about 70 µA rectified. The factor between \(\mathcal{E}_n\) and \(V\) is geometric, so the meter is calibrated against the magnet and a nuclear reaction; it follows only slow changes, below about 1 Hz.

Feedback. A beam slightly too energetic lands more on one slit jaw than the other. The two slit currents, amplified logarithmically so that their difference depends on position and not intensity, give an error signal. It drives the grid of a vacuum triode in series with corona needles facing the terminal: more error, more corona current, more charge bled away. Capacitive pickoff plates sense fast ripple and add a derivative term. NEC’s stabilisers switch to slit control when the terminal is within 50 kV of its set point and beam is on a slit, and otherwise use the generating voltmeter; rated stability is \(\pm 0.01\) per cent. The corona loop is slow: NEC gives its bandwidth as about 1 Hz; at the ANU the transit time of charge from needles to terminal keeps it below 10 Hz, and the 14UD’s open-loop time constant was 10.2 s at 8 MV. That system held the beam with long-term stability equal to a 3.9 kV full-width fluctuation of the terminal (Lobanov and others, 2012).

Energy defined to about one part in \(10^4\) is ordinary for these machines (Jones, 1984); the ripple of a Pelletron chain alone is typically \(10^{-4}\) full width at half maximum, and active correction has reached \(10^{-5}\) (Sundquist, in Chao and others, section 1.6.5).

Pre-requisite (continued): Nuclear reactions and Q value

Pre-requisite (continued). Reaction equations and the centre-of-mass energy available from a fixed target were taught in chapter 1, the Q-value from tabulated masses in chapter 5, and the thick-target yield integral in chapter 5, section 3. This section adds the compact notation, why a negative Q-value means a threshold, what a resonance and its width are, and how thin and thick targets turn a resonance into a yield curve that calibrates a beam’s energy. Section 9 uses it, and section 14’s depth profiling with \(^{15}\)N rests on the thick-target curve. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz volume 3, section 145 (Breit and Wigner’s formulae), Tong’s Quantum Mechanics, sections 14.1.5 and 14.2.9 (resonances), and Landau and Lifshitz volume 1, section 16 (the centre-of-mass frame in disintegrations).

Notation. A reaction is written \(A(a,b)B\): target nucleus \(A\), projectile \(a\), light product \(b\), heavy product \(B\). So \(^7\)Li(p,n)\(^7\)Be means p + \(^7\)Li → n + \(^7\)Be, and \(^{27}\)Al(p,γ)\(^{28}\)Si is radiative capture: the proton is absorbed and the new nucleus sheds its excess energy as a gamma ray. In \(^1\)H(\(^{15}\)N,αγ)\(^{12}\)C the beam is the heavy nucleus and the target the hydrogen in a sample.

Why a negative Q-value means a threshold. \(Q_{nuc} = (m_a + m_A - m_b - m_B)c^2\) is the kinetic energy released. If it is negative, kinetic energy must supply \(-Q_{nuc}\). But momentum is conserved, so the products must still move with the centre of mass, and that motion takes its share. Only the centre-of-mass energy (F3, section 6), \(W m_2/(m_1 + m_2)\) for a projectile of mass \(m_1\) and kinetic energy \(W\) on a target \(m_2\) at rest (chapter 1), can be turned into mass. Setting it equal to \(-Q_{nuc}\) gives the threshold of section 9, \(W_{th} = -Q_{nuc}(1 + m_1/m_2)\).

Just at threshold the products have no motion relative to the centre of mass: they all move forward together at its velocity, \(v_{cm} = p/(m_1 + m_2)\). For \(^7\)Li(p,n) at 1880.4 keV this gives the neutrons \(\tfrac{1}{2}m_nv_{cm}^2 = 29.7\) keV, all forward: a source of nearly single-energy slow neutrons.

A resonance. Some reactions go through a compound nucleus: projectile and target merge into one nucleus, here \(^{28}\)Si, left in an excited state, one of the discrete energy levels that a nucleus has, just as an atom has (F7, sections 7 and 10). When the centre-of-mass energy, added to the energy released by forming the compound, matches one of its excited levels, the proton is captured into that level, lingers, and decays, here by a gamma ray. For \(^{27}\)Al + p the energy released on forming \(^{28}\)Si is, from atomic masses (the electrons balance), \(Q_{nuc} = (26.98153841 + 1.00782503 - 27.97692653)\,\text{u} \times 931\,494\ \text{keV/u} = 11\,584.9\) keV. The resonance at a laboratory proton energy of 991.86 keV is a centre-of-mass energy of \(991.86 \times 26.98/(26.98 + 1.008) = 956.1\) keV, so it feeds a level near 12 541 keV in \(^{28}\)Si; the evaluated level is at 12 541.5 keV.

The width. A level that decays has a finite lifetime \(\tau\), and so no perfectly sharp energy. In quantum mechanics (F7, section 9) a state of energy \(E\) has an amplitude that turns in time as \(e^{-iEt/\hbar}\), and the probability is the square of the amplitude’s size. A decaying level’s amplitude turns at its energy \(E_R\) and shrinks: it is \(e^{-iE_Rt/\hbar}e^{-t/2\tau}\) for \(t > 0\), so that its square falls as \(e^{-t/\tau}\), the decay law. How much of each energy \(E\) it contains comes from Fourier’s idea (F5, section 16): multiply by \(e^{iEt/\hbar}\), which undoes the turning at exactly \(E\), and add up over time. This is the integral of a decaying exponential, the “decay” result of the Laplace transform above with a complex rate:

\[ \int_0^\infty e^{-[\Gamma/2 - i(E - E_R)]t/\hbar}\,dt = \frac{\hbar}{\Gamma/2 - i(E - E_R)}, \qquad \Gamma = \frac{\hbar}{\tau} . \]

Its size squared is \(\hbar^2/[(E - E_R)^2 + (\Gamma/2)^2]\) (a complex number \(x - iy\) has \(|x - iy|^2 = x^2 + y^2\)). So near an isolated resonance the cross-section has the Breit–Wigner shape

\[ \sigma(E) = \sigma_R\,\frac{(\Gamma/2)^2}{(E - E_R)^2 + (\Gamma/2)^2}, \]

with \(E\) the centre-of-mass energy, \(E_R\) the resonance energy, \(\sigma_R\) the peak cross-section and \(\Gamma\) the full width at half maximum: \(\sigma = \sigma_R/2\) at \(E = E_R \pm \Gamma/2\). Landau and Lifshitz derive the full formula, with the factors that fix \(\sigma_R\) from the partial widths for entering and leaving; the shape is all the calibration needs. The \(^{15}\)N resonance of section 14 is 1.8 keV wide on the scale of the \(^{15}\)N beam’s laboratory energy. With hydrogen as the target the centre-of-mass energy is \(1.008/(15.000 + 1.008)\) of the beam energy, so the level’s own width is \(\Gamma = 1.8 \times 0.063 = 0.11\) keV, and its lifetime \(\hbar/\Gamma = 5.8 \times 10^{-18}\) s.

Thin-target yield. Let the target be so thin that the beam loses much less energy in it than the resonance width and the beam’s own spread. If it holds \(n_t\) atoms per unit area, a fraction \(n_t\sigma(E)\) of the beam reacts (each atom presents an area \(\sigma\)). Scanning the beam’s energy traces the cross-section, broadened by the beam’s energy spread and by the target’s energy thickness. Thin targets are quoted by mass per unit area: a film of 1 µg/cm\(^2\) of aluminium (3.7 nm thick, at 2.70 g/cm\(^3\)) holds \(10^{-6}\ \text{g/cm}^2\) divided by the mass of one atom, \(26.98 \times 1.6605 \times 10^{-24}\) g, which is \(2.2 \times 10^{16}\) atoms/cm\(^2\); protons of 992 keV, losing about 48 keV per micrometre in aluminium (the Bethe formula of chapter 1, without its small low-energy corrections), lose 0.18 keV crossing it.

Thick-target yield. Now let the beam stop in the target. A proton entering with energy \(E_0\) above the resonance slows down, and somewhere inside its energy passes through \(E_R\), where it may react. In a layer \(dx\) it meets \(n\,dx\) atoms per unit area, so the yield per proton is \(dY = n\sigma\,dx\), and \(dx = dE/S\) with \(S = dE/dx\) the stopping power. Writing \(\varepsilon = S/n\) for the stopping cross-section (energy lost per atom per unit area),

\[ Y(E_0) = \int_0^{E_0}\frac{\sigma(E)}{\varepsilon(E)}\,dE . \]

Here \(E\) is the beam’s laboratory energy, so from now on \(E_R\) and \(\Gamma\) mean the resonance’s position and width on that scale: each is the centre-of-mass value times \((m_1 + m_2)/m_2\). Across a narrow resonance \(\varepsilon\) hardly changes and comes outside the integral. What is left is \(\sigma_R(\Gamma/2)^2\int dE/[(E - E_R)^2 + (\Gamma/2)^2]\). Substitute \(u = 2(E - E_R)/\Gamma\), so \(dE = (\Gamma/2)\,du\) and the integrand becomes \((2/\Gamma)\,du/(1 + u^2)\); and \(\int du/(1 + u^2) = \arctan u\), because if \(u = \tan\theta\) then \(du/d\theta = 1/\cos^2\theta = 1 + \tan^2\theta\). The lower limit, \(E = 0\), is so many widths below the resonance that \(\arctan u\) there is \(-\pi/2\). So the Breit–Wigner shape integrates to an arctangent:

\[ Y(E_0) = \frac{\sigma_R\Gamma}{2\varepsilon}\left[\arctan\frac{2(E_0 - E_R)}{\Gamma} + \frac{\pi}{2}\right] . \]

The yield is zero well below the resonance, rises as a step, and levels off at \(\pi\sigma_R\Gamma/(2\varepsilon)\) above it. Its half height falls exactly at \(E_0 = E_R\), for any target thick compared with the width: that is what makes the step a calibration point. Its 10 to 90 per cent rise takes \(3.08\,\Gamma\) (exercise H3). If the beam’s energy spread, Gaussian with width \(\sigma_E\), is much larger than \(\Gamma\), the step takes the beam’s shape instead: the yield at \(E_0\) counts the part of the beam above the resonance, the area of the Gaussian beyond a point, which is the error function of F6, section 5 (taken further in the Pre-requisite before section 11). It rises from 10 to 90 per cent over \(2.56\,\sigma_E\) (from \(-1.28\,\sigma_E\) to \(+1.28\,\sigma_E\)): the step’s width measures the beam’s energy spread.

Figure 6.7. A resonance of full width \(\Gamma = 1.8\) keV (dashed, the Breit–Wigner cross-section as a fraction of its peak) and the yield from a thick target (solid, as a fraction of its plateau) as the beam energy \(E_0\) is stepped across it. The beam reacts wherever its energy, slowing in the target, passes through the resonance, so the yield rises as a step once \(E_0\) exceeds \(E_R\). The step’s half height falls exactly at \(E_R\), and it rises from 10 to 90 per cent over \(3.08\,\Gamma = 5.5\) keV.

Energy into depth. A beam set \(\Delta E\) above the resonance reacts at the depth where it has lost \(\Delta E\), \(x = \Delta E/S\) (taking \(S\) as constant over so short a path). For protons in aluminium at 992 keV, 1 keV above resonance is about 21 nm deep and 5 keV about 104 nm. Scanning the energy upwards scans the reacting layer inwards. That is nuclear reaction analysis: with the 1.8 keV wide \(^{15}\)N resonance, a few keV of beam energy resolve layers of nanometres in a sample’s hydrogen.

9. Calibration by nuclear reactions

A magnet and a voltmeter give relative energy; nuclear reactions of known energy give the scale.

A threshold. \(^7\)Li(p,n)\(^7\)Be has \(Q_{nuc} < 0\), where \(Q_{nuc}\) is the reaction’s Q-value, the kinetic energy it releases (written as in chapter 5, since a bare \(Q\) is the terminal charge of section 3). Part of the proton’s energy must go into motion of the centre of mass, so the reaction starts only at a threshold. Non-relativistically, for a projectile of mass \(m_1\) on a target \(m_2\) at rest, the energy available in the centre of mass is \(W m_2/(m_1 + m_2)\), which must equal \(-Q_{nuc}\):

\[ W_{th} = -Q_{nuc}\left(1 + \frac{m_1}{m_2}\right). \]

The threshold is 1880.4 keV (1880.443 ± 0.020 keV in a measurement referred to the volt standard, White, Barker and Lovelock, 1985, against an earlier accepted 1880.51 ± 0.08 keV). With nuclear masses \(m_1/m_2 = 1.00728/7.01436\) this gives \(Q_{nuc} = -1644.3\) keV; the relativistic formula changes only the fifth figure, to \(-1644.1\) keV. Below the threshold no neutrons; above it, a yield rising from zero.

A resonance. \(^{27}\)Al(p,γ)\(^{28}\)Si has a sharp resonance at a proton energy of 991.86 keV (the evaluated value; a measurement against the volt standard gave 991.756 ± 0.017 keV, Brindhaban and others, 1994), narrow enough that thick-target yield curves show structure a few hundred electronvolts above it (Walters and others, 1962). Scanning the beam across it with a thin aluminium target, the gamma yield rises and falls; its position is a second calibration point.

What one part in ten thousand means. At the lithium threshold, 188 eV; at the aluminium resonance, 99 eV. Before the war Herb’s group found resonances as narrow as 1 keV with MeV protons on light elements such as fluorine and aluminium; from 1947 they measured the energy with a 90° electrostatic analyser between concentric electrodes at a precisely measured voltage. Its scale, good to 0.1 per cent, put the lithium threshold at 1.882 MeV and the aluminium resonance at 0.9933 MeV (Herb, Snowden and Sala, as given by Livingston and Blewett, section 3-8). This made the electrostatic generator the instrument of nuclear spectroscopy: level energies and widths of light nuclei, taken point by point at known energy. Cyclotrons (chapter 10) gave more energy with a much broader spread.

10. Charging by chain

A rubberised fabric belt sheds dust and lint, carries uneven charge, and its joint passes the terminal once a revolution (at the Vivitron, a disturbance of 0.3 s every 9.76 s). Herb’s programme from 1946 sought metal carriers: paper staples on a rubberised belt in a 1951 machine; then, by Victor Fung and James Ferry, a “string of beads”, metal cylinders on twisted nylon cord, which ran in gradients of 1.5 MV per foot. At National Electrostatics Corporation it became the Pelletron chain: metal cylinders joined by solid insulating links such as nylon.

The chain is charged by induction. At the bottom pulley an inductor electrode at a few kilovolts faces the pellets while they touch the grounded pulley; its field draws charge onto each pellet, which keeps it when contact breaks. In the terminal the process reverses. The charge per pellet is set by a capacitance and a voltage, not by a corona, so it is reproducible, there is no sparking at the transfer, and charging fluctuations are very low. Herb’s 1971 chain, on 30 cm pulleys, was rated at 150 µA (a single chain still delivers about that; Sundquist, in Chao and others, section 1.6.5), and the return run can bring down charge of the other sign.

The Laddertron, developed at Daresbury for its large tandem, does the same with a flat chain of closely spaced metal cross-pieces, each insulated from the next, between insulating side members. High Voltage Engineering Corporation later marketed it, and it was fitted to tandems at Legnaro, Orsay, Beijing and Stony Brook (McKay, 2005).

Pre-requisite (continued): Energy loss in matter, range, Bragg peak

Pre-requisite (continued). Stopping power, the Bethe formula and range were taught in chapter 1, and the stopping cross-section per atom and Bethe’s formula at low energy in chapter 5. This section adds what happens to an ion’s charge as it passes through matter, which decides what a tandem’s stripper delivers, and how stopping power turns energy into depth in the analyses of section 14. Sections 11 and 14 use it. Taught from Chao and others, Handbook, section 7.1.7.1 (McMahan, charge state strippers), and Tong’s Quantum Mechanics, section 6.3 (the hydrogen atom), for the orbital speed.

Charge changing. An ion moving through a foil or a gas meets atom after atom. In each encounter it may lose one of its electrons to the collision, or capture one from the atom. Let \(\sigma_l\) and \(\sigma_c\) be the cross-sections (m\(^2\), or cm\(^2\) as tables give them) for loss and capture. (Here \(\sigma\) is a cross-section, as elsewhere in the course; the Gaussian width of the next section is a different quantity.) Measure the stripper’s thickness \(x\) in atoms per unit area.

Take the simplest case, two charge states, and let \(F_0\) and \(F_1 = 1 - F_0\) be the fractions of the beam in the lower and the higher. In a layer \(dx\), a fraction \(\sigma_l\,dx\) of the lower state loses an electron and a fraction \(\sigma_c\,dx\) of the higher captures one:

\[ \frac{dF_1}{dx} = \sigma_lF_0 - \sigma_cF_1 = \sigma_l - (\sigma_l + \sigma_c)F_1 . \]

This is the first-order equation with a constant source of chapter 3 (the pump-down equation \(V\,dP/dt = Q - SP\) in other letters), and its solution relaxes to a steady value:

\[ F_1(x) = F_{1\infty} + \left[F_1(0) - F_{1\infty}\right]e^{-(\sigma_l + \sigma_c)x}, \qquad F_{1\infty} = \frac{\sigma_l}{\sigma_l + \sigma_c} . \]

The equilibrium does not depend on the charge the ion arrived with; once the stripper is thick enough, a negative ion and a neutral atom of the same speed come out with the same distribution. With many charge states the Handbook writes one such equation for each state, coupled through the cross-sections, and the equilibrium distribution comes out roughly Gaussian about a mean charge. Capture dominates at low speeds (below about 0.5 MeV per nucleon, that is, per proton or neutron in the ion: a measure of speed, since ions with the same energy per nucleon move at the same speed) and loss at high speeds (above about 2 MeV per nucleon), so the mean charge rises with speed. A foil strips to a higher mean charge than a gas because its atoms are so close together that an ion excited in one collision is hit again before it relaxes, and an excited electron is easier to remove.

An illustration. With \(\sigma_l = 2 \times 10^{-17}\) cm\(^2\) and \(\sigma_c = 5 \times 10^{-18}\) cm\(^2\) in carbon, \(F_{1\infty} = 0.8\), and the approach to it has a \(1/e\) thickness of \(1/(\sigma_l + \sigma_c) = 4 \times 10^{16}\) atoms/cm\(^2\), or 0.8 µg/cm\(^2\) of carbon. Three such thicknesses, 2.4 µg/cm\(^2\), bring the beam within 5 per cent of equilibrium.

Bohr’s velocity criterion. Which electrons go? In the Bohr picture of hydrogen (F7, section 7), the electron’s orbit balances the Coulomb pull against its motion, \(m_ev^2/r = e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)\), with angular momentum \(m_evr = \hbar\). Dividing the first by the second,

\[ v = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar} = \alpha c = 2.19 \times 10^6\ \text{m/s} . \]

Here \(\alpha = e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c) = 1/137.036\) is the fine-structure constant, a pure number, so the hydrogen electron moves at \(c/137\).

For an electron bound to a nuclear charge \(Z\) in the innermost shell, the same argument gives \(Z\alpha c\). Bohr’s criterion says that an ion moving through matter keeps those electrons whose orbital speed is above its own speed and loses those whose speed is below it. Seen from the ion, the target’s atoms rush past at speed \(v\); an electron slower than that is too loosely bound, on the scale of those collisions, to stay, while a faster one adjusts to each collision and remains. Using the statistical (Thomas–Fermi) picture of a heavy atom, in which the typical electron speed is \(Z^{2/3}\alpha c\) and the number of electrons orbiting slower than a speed \(v\) is roughly \(Z^{1/3}v/\alpha c\), Bohr counted the electrons the ion loses and estimated the mean charge as

\[ \bar n_q \approx Z^{1/3}\,\frac{v}{\alpha c}, \qquad v < Z^{2/3}\alpha c . \]

It is a rough guide, good to tens of per cent. At a 14 MV terminal with 0.15 MeV injection (14.15 MeV at the stripper): iodine has \(v = 2.12\,\alpha c\) and the estimate gives 8.0 against the fitted 10.9 of section 11; nickel, \(3.14\,\alpha c\), gives 9.5 against 10.5; gold, \(1.70\,\alpha c\), gives 7.3 against 11.2. Carbon, at \(6.9\,\alpha c\), moves faster than its own innermost electrons (\(6\,\alpha c\)), so it can be stripped bare, which is why light ions leave a tandem fully stripped and heavy ones do not.

Foils wear out. A heavy ion deposits much of its lost energy close to its track, displaces carbon atoms and heats the foil. The foil’s structure changes, it shrinks and thickens, and in time it breaks, sooner for heavier ions. That is why foils are carried in magazines, and why a thickening foil shows as a drift of the charge states downward (section 11 and At the bench). The user’s books give no numbers for foil lifetimes.

Energy into depth. Over a thin layer the stopping power \(S = -dW/dx\) is nearly constant, so an ion that has gone a depth \(x\) has lost \(Sx\). In Rutherford backscattering an ion enters normally with \(W_0\), loses \(S_{in}x\) on the way in, keeps the fraction \(K\) (the kinematic factor of section 14) in the collision, and loses \(S_{out}x/|\cos\theta_{out}|\) on the slanting way out, where \(\theta_{out}\) is the exit angle from the normal. It arrives at the detector with

\[ W_1 = K(W_0 - S_{in}x) - \frac{S_{out}x}{|\cos\theta_{out}|}, \]

so a layer at depth \(x\) appears \([S]x\) below the surface signal, with \([S] = KS_{in} + S_{out}/|\cos\theta_{out}|\) the energy-loss factor.

2 MeV helium in silicon at \(170^\circ\). The Bethe formula gives about 245 keV/µm going in at 2 MeV and about 300 keV/µm coming out at 1.13 MeV (estimates; near the stopping maximum, tabulated values are preferred). With \(K = 0.566\) and an exit \(10^\circ\) from the normal, \([S] = 0.566 \times 245 + 300/0.985 = 443\) keV/µm. A layer 100 nm deep shows 44 keV below the surface edge, and a detector resolving 15 keV resolves about 34 nm of depth. In nuclear reaction analysis the depth is simpler, \(x = (E_0 - E_R)/S\) (the Pre-requisite section before section 9).

Pre-requisite (continued): Special functions and integrals (Bessel, Gaussian, error function)

Pre-requisite (continued). Gaussian integrals such as \(\int e^{-p^2/2mk_BT}\,dp = \sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}\) were taught in chapter 2. This section adds, as a probability distribution, the Gaussian of F6, section 5: its mean and width, its full width at half maximum, and the fraction of it in a given interval through the error function. Section 11 uses it for the charge states out of a stripper, and section 8’s stability figures are quoted as full widths. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz volume 5, section 110 (the Gaussian distribution), and Chao and others, Handbook, section 7.1.7.1 (the Gaussian charge-state distribution).

Why the Gaussian appears. Take any distribution with a single sharp peak and expand the logarithm of the probability about the peak. The first derivative is zero there, so the first term that matters is quadratic, and the probability near the peak falls as the exponential of minus a square. Landau and Lifshitz use this to show that small fluctuations are Gaussian. The charge of an ion leaving a stripper is the net result of many captures and losses, and the Handbook gives the equilibrium distribution in this form.

The distribution. A quantity \(x\) with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\) (both in the units of \(x\)) has the Gaussian probability density

\[ p(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\exp\!\left[-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right] . \]

It is normalised: with \(u = x - \mu\), \(\int e^{-u^2/2\sigma^2}du = \sigma\sqrt{2\pi}\) is the Gaussian integral of chapter 2. The mean is \(\mu\) by symmetry. The variance comes from differentiating the Gaussian integral with respect to its parameter: \(\int e^{-au^2}du = \sqrt{\pi/a}\), so \(\int u^2e^{-au^2}du = -\frac{d}{da}\sqrt{\pi/a} = \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\pi}\,a^{-3/2}\). With \(a = 1/2\sigma^2\) this is \(\sigma^3\sqrt{2\pi}\), and dividing by the normalisation leaves

\[ \int (x - \mu)^2\,p(x)\,dx = \sigma^2 . \]

Full width at half maximum. The density falls to half its peak where \((x - \mu)^2/2\sigma^2 = \ln 2\), so

\[ \text{FWHM} = 2\sigma\sqrt{2\ln 2} = 2.355\,\sigma . \]

The ANU’s slow terminal wandering of 3.9 kV full width (section 8) is a standard deviation of 1.66 kV.

The fraction in an interval. The fraction of a Gaussian within \(h\) of its mean is

\[ P(|x - \mu| < h) = \operatorname{erf}\!\left(\frac{h}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\right), \qquad \operatorname{erf}(z) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_0^z e^{-t^2}\,dt . \]

The error function has no expression in elementary functions; it is tabulated or computed. Within \(\pm\sigma\) lie 68.3 per cent, within \(\pm 2\sigma\) 95.4 per cent.

Charge states as a Gaussian. Charges come in whole numbers, but when the width is more than about one charge, the fraction in charge state \(q\) is close to the area of the Gaussian over the unit-wide bin around it:

\[ F(q) \approx \int_{q - 1/2}^{q + 1/2}p(x)\,dx = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[\operatorname{erf}\!\left(\frac{q + \tfrac{1}{2} - \bar n_q}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\right) - \operatorname{erf}\!\left(\frac{q - \tfrac{1}{2} - \bar n_q}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\right)\right], \]

with \(\bar n_q\) the mean charge and \(\sigma\) the width (written \(\Delta n_q\) in section 11). For a bin centred on the mean with \(\sigma = 1.6\), \(F = \operatorname{erf}(0.5/(1.6\sqrt{2})) = \operatorname{erf}(0.221) = 0.245\); almost the same as the peak density times the bin width, \(1/(1.6\sqrt{2\pi}) = 0.249\).

Iodine at 14 MV. With \(\bar n_q = 10.9\) and \(\sigma = 1.6\): 9+ 12.4 per cent, 10+ 21.1, 11+ 24.5, 12+ 19.5, 13+ 10.7. A quarter of the beam is in the most likely charge, and the magnet after the tandem takes only one.

11. The tandem

The idea. Willard Bennett proposed in a patent filed in 1937 (granted 1940), and Luis Alvarez again in 1951 (“Energy doubling in dc accelerators”), to use a positive terminal twice. Make negative ions at ground. They are drawn up to the terminal at \(+V_T\). Inside it they pass through a thin foil or a short tube of gas, the stripper, which removes two or more electrons. Now positive, they are repelled down a second tube to ground. Ion source and target both sit at ground.

The energy. For a charge in a static potential, kinetic energy plus charge times potential is constant while the charge is fixed (energy conservation with potential energy \(q\phi\), F4, section 5). Going up, the charge is \(-e\) and the potential rises from 0 to \(V_T\): the kinetic energy rises by \(eV_T\). Going down, the charge is \(+n_q e\) and the potential falls to 0: it rises by \(n_q eV_T\). So

\[ W = W_{inj} + (1 + n_q)\,eV_T . \]

If a molecular ion of mass \(M\) breaks up in the stripper, a fragment of mass \(m\) keeps the fraction \(m/M\) of the energy it arrived with: \(W = (m/M)(W_{inj} + eV_T) + n_q eV_T\). Accelerator mass spectrometry relies on this break-up (section 14).

Figure 6.8. A tandem, laid out straight and not to scale, with the potential along the beam’s path beneath it. Negative ions from a source at ground are drawn up the first tube to the positive terminal, gaining \(eV_T\); inside the terminal there is no field and no energy change; the stripper turns them into positive ions of charge \(n_qe\), which are pushed down the second tube to ground, gaining \(n_qeV_T\). Source and target both stay at ground potential, where they can be reached and adjusted.

At 14 MV, the terminal voltage guaranteed for the ANU 14UD Pelletron (Herb, 1971): protons 28 MeV, carbon stripped to 6+ 98 MeV, oxygen stripped to 8+ 126 MeV. The protons have \(B\rho = 0.770\) T m and need 0.51 T on a 1.5 m radius.

Charge-state distributions. A stripper gives a spread of charges. Passing through matter, an ion loses and captures electrons until the two balance, at a mean charge that rises with its speed. Bohr’s criterion gives the picture: electrons whose orbital speed is below the ion’s speed are stripped, faster ones are kept; for hydrogen the orbital speed is \(\alpha c = 2.19 \times 10^6\) m/s. A widely used semi-empirical fit for the mean charge state after a carbon foil (Nikolaev and Dmitriev, 1968) is

\[ \bar n_q = Z\left[1 + \left(\frac{v}{Z^{0.45}\,v'}\right)^{-1/0.6}\right]^{-0.6}, \qquad v' = 3.6 \times 10^6\ \text{m/s}, \]

with \(Z\) the atomic number and \(v\) the ion’s speed, and the distribution roughly Gaussian with width \(\Delta n_q = 0.5\,[\bar n_q(1 - (\bar n_q/Z)^{1/0.6})]^{1/2}\). It is designed for \(Z \gtrsim 20\) at 5 to 200 MeV and is good to about 5 per cent (Gastis and others, 2016). It is quoted, not derived.

A worked spectrum. At 14 MV with 0.15 MeV injection, ions reach the terminal with 14.15 MeV. For \(^{127}\)I, \(\beta = 0.0155\), \(\bar n_q = 10.9\), \(\Delta n_q = 1.6\): charge 11+ holds 25 per cent of the beam, 10+ and 12+ about 21 and 20 per cent, and the 11+ ions leave with \(0.15 + 12 \times 14 = 168\) MeV. For \(^{58}\)Ni, \(\bar n_q = 10.5\); for \(^{197}\)Au, 11.2. Heavy ions are slow at the terminal and keep most of their electrons, so they gain far less than full stripping would give (for iodine, 168 MeV against \(0.15 + 54 \times 14 = 756\) MeV), and only a quarter of the beam is in the chosen charge.

A foil strips to a higher mean charge than gas but is damaged by heavy ions, so foils thicken and break and are carried in magazines; gas never wears out. Large tandems added a second foil about two-thirds of the way down (Jones, 1984; the Handbook puts it about one-third of the way down the second tube, Sundquist, in Chao and others, section 1.6.5). In a long tandem both tubes can share one column, with a 180° magnet in the terminal: the folded tandem, with about 30 per cent less capacitance than a straight one at Oak Ridge’s size.

12. The largest machines, and what limited them

  • Oak Ridge, Holifield Heavy Ion Research Facility (completed 1982). An NEC 25URC Pelletron: folded, vertical, 25 MV design, corona grading. The column without tubes held 30.9 MV (Jones, 1984; Herb’s memoir gives 32 MV). With tubes it ran experiments at 19 MV in 1984 and reached its 25 MV design only in late 1988, after a sparking tube was redesigned and replaced in 1987. It cost about 8 million US dollars.
  • Daresbury Nuclear Structure Facility (completed 1982). Built by Daresbury: straight, vertical, 20 MV design upgradable to 30 MV, intermediate shield, Laddertron charging, resistor grading. The column alone held 29.7 MV; with beam it reached 20.1 MV by 1984, but it never ran much above 20 MV in operation, and it closed in 1992 or 1993 (accounts differ).
  • The Vivitron, Strasbourg (first beam August 1993). Designed for 35 MV by Michel Letournel’s group, with a seven-layer “portico” of discrete electrodes in place of a solid shield, giving a nearly uniform radial field in a tank of 1,300 m\(^3\), about 60 per cent of the Oak Ridge or Daresbury volume. It used a belt and inclined-field glass tubes, and ran experiments at 8 to 19 MV until it closed in December 2003.

None reached its first ambition. Jones (1984) gave three reasons: immaturity (every large machine improved over years of conditioning, the slow raising of voltage that burns off field emitters one by one, described in At the bench); statistics (a machine fails at its weakest element, and more elements mean more weak ones); and stored energy. When the terminal sparks, most of its energy goes into the gas, but some couples into the tube and causes overvoltages, vacuum arcs and flashover. Oak Ridge stored about 120 kJ at 20 MV: from \(U = \tfrac{1}{2}CV^2\), that is 600 pF and a terminal charge of 12 mC, and 188 kJ at 25 MV. The column’s capacitance grows roughly with its length, which grows with the design voltage, so stored energy grows roughly as the design voltage times the square of the operating voltage, and a conventional tank’s volume as the cube of the design voltage.

13. Electron machines for X-ray therapy

Reverse the polarity, put a hot cathode in the terminal, and the generator accelerates electrons down onto a heavy-metal target to make megavolt X-rays. Megavolt X-rays penetrate further and build their dose (the energy absorbed per kilogram of tissue) up below the skin rather than at it. The X-rays deposit their energy through the fast electrons they knock forward out of atoms, and those electrons travel some millimetres before stopping; at the surface few have yet been set moving, so the dose there is low, and it grows with depth until as many electrons stop in each layer as start in it (the secondary electrons are then said to be in equilibrium with the X-rays) (Trump, 1948; Livingston and Blewett, section 7-12). In 1937 Van de Graaff and John Trump installed a 1 MV air-insulated machine at the Collis P. Huntington Memorial Hospital in Boston; in 1939 they described a compact pressure-insulated X-ray generator; in the war five 2 MeV units were built for the US Navy for radiography, made workable by a uniform-field tube of Van de Graaff and Buechner. High Voltage Engineering Corporation made 2 to 2.5 MV therapy machines from 1946 to 1959, after which electron linear accelerators (chapter 14) took over.

Pre-requisite (continued): Rutherford scattering

Pre-requisite (continued). The Rutherford cross-section, impact parameter and solid angle were taught in chapter 1, for a target heavy enough not to recoil. Backscattering analysis needs the recoil, worked here with the conservation laws of F3, section 6: this section works out the energy an ion keeps after an elastic collision with a nucleus at rest, the kinematic factor \(K\) of section 14. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz volume 1, section 17 (elastic collisions).

The collision. An ion of mass \(M_1\) and speed \(v_0\) strikes a nucleus of mass \(M_2\) at rest. Afterwards the ion moves at speed \(v_1\) at angle \(\theta\) to its original direction, and the nucleus recoils at speed \(v_2\) at angle \(\varphi\) on the other side. The collision is elastic: no energy goes into exciting either nucleus. At a few MeV the ion is slow (\(\beta = 0.033\) for 2 MeV helium), so non-relativistic mechanics is enough.

Momentum along and across the original direction, and kinetic energy, are conserved:

\[ \begin{aligned} M_1v_0 &= M_1v_1\cos\theta + M_2v_2\cos\varphi, \\ 0 &= M_1v_1\sin\theta - M_2v_2\sin\varphi, \\ \tfrac{1}{2}M_1v_0^2 &= \tfrac{1}{2}M_1v_1^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}M_2v_2^2 . \end{aligned} \]

We do not observe the recoil, so eliminate \(\varphi\). Move the ion’s terms to one side in the two momentum equations, square and add; \(\cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\varphi = 1\) gives

\[ M_2^2v_2^2 = M_1^2\left(v_0^2 - 2v_0v_1\cos\theta + v_1^2\right) . \]

The energy equation gives \(M_2v_2^2 = M_1(v_0^2 - v_1^2)\); multiply it by \(M_2\) and set the two expressions for \(M_2^2v_2^2\) equal: \(M_1^2(v_0^2 - 2v_0v_1\cos\theta + v_1^2) = M_1M_2(v_0^2 - v_1^2)\). Divide by \(M_1v_0^2\) and write \(u = v_1/v_0\): \(M_1(1 - 2u\cos\theta + u^2) = M_2(1 - u^2)\), which gathered by powers of \(u\) is a quadratic,

\[ (M_1 + M_2)\,u^2 - 2M_1\cos\theta\,u - (M_2 - M_1) = 0, \]

By the quadratic formula the roots are

\[ u = \frac{M_1\cos\theta \pm \sqrt{M_1^2\cos^2\theta + (M_1 + M_2)(M_2 - M_1)}}{M_1 + M_2}, \]

and the quantity under the root simplifies, since \(M_1^2\cos^2\theta + M_2^2 - M_1^2 = M_2^2 - M_1^2\sin^2\theta\). For \(M_2 > M_1\) only the plus sign gives a positive speed, so

\[ u = \frac{M_1\cos\theta + \sqrt{M_2^2 - M_1^2\sin^2\theta}}{M_1 + M_2} . \]

The kinetic energy goes as the square of the speed, so the fraction kept is \(K = u^2\), the formula of section 14. Landau and Lifshitz reach it more elegantly, by drawing the collision in the centre-of-mass frame, where both particles keep their speeds and only turn, and adding back the centre-of-mass velocity.

Two checks. Backscattering needs \(M_2 > M_1\): if \(M_1 > M_2\) the square root turns imaginary beyond \(\sin\theta = M_2/M_1\), and the ion cannot be turned back. With equal masses \(K = \cos^2\theta\), zero at \(90^\circ\): a billiard ball cannot rebound off an equal ball at rest.

Heavy targets. For \(M_2 \gg M_1\), expand to first order in \(M_1/M_2\) with \((1 + x)^n \approx 1 + nx\) (F1, section 8): the root is \(M_2[1 - \tfrac{1}{2}(M_1/M_2)^2\sin^2\theta] \approx M_2\), so \(u \approx (M_2 + M_1\cos\theta)/(M_1 + M_2) = [1 + (M_1/M_2)\cos\theta]/[1 + M_1/M_2] \approx 1 - (M_1/M_2)(1 - \cos\theta)\), and squaring,

\[ K \approx 1 - \frac{2M_1}{M_2}(1 - \cos\theta), \]

which at \(180^\circ\) is \(1 - 4M_1/M_2\). The energy lost to recoil shrinks as \(1/M_2\), and its change with mass shrinks as \(1/M_2^2\). Two heavy elements a mass unit apart therefore come out at almost the same energy.

2 MeV helium at \(170^\circ\). \(K\) is 0.253 off carbon, 0.362 off oxygen, 0.566 off silicon and 0.922 off gold (natural atomic masses). Near silicon, one mass unit shifts the scattered energy by 23 keV, so a detector resolving 15 keV separates masses about 0.6 u apart; near gold, one mass unit shifts it by only 0.76 keV, and the same detector cannot separate masses closer than about 20 u.

Pre-requisite (continued): Larmor formula and relativistic radiated power

Pre-requisite (continued). Chapter 1 derived Larmor’s formula for a charge moving slowly compared with light, noted that under the same force the power goes as \(q^2F^2/m^2\), and carried it to high speed. This section recalls the derivation briefly and works out the consequence that section 14 uses: under the same force a proton radiates millions of times less than an electron, so in PIXE (particle-induced X-ray emission, section 14) the background comes from the electrons the protons knock out. Taught from Tong’s Electromagnetism, section 7.2.2 (power radiated: the Larmor formula), and Landau and Lifshitz volume 2, section 67 (dipole radiation).

The radiation field. A charge \(q\) sits at rest, its field lines straight and radial. At \(t = 0\) it is given a brief acceleration \(a\) lasting \(\Delta t\), which leaves it moving slowly at \(\Delta v = a\Delta t\). News of the change travels outwards at \(c\). At a later time \(t\), beyond radius \(ct\) the field is still the old one, centred on where the charge would have been; inside \(c(t - \Delta t)\) it is the field of the moving charge. In the thin shell between, of thickness \(c\Delta t\), each field line must join its outer part to its inner part, and since the inner pattern has shifted sideways by \(\Delta v\,t\sin\Theta\) (with \(\Theta\) the angle from the direction of acceleration), the line runs a long way sideways across a thin shell. The ratio of the sideways to the radial field in the shell is the ratio of those lengths:

\[ \frac{\mathcal{E}_\perp}{\mathcal{E}_r} = \frac{\Delta v\,t\sin\Theta}{c\,\Delta t} = \frac{a\,t\sin\Theta}{c} . \]

With \(\mathcal{E}_r = q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)\) and \(r = ct\),

\[ \mathcal{E}_\perp = \frac{q\,a\sin\Theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c^2r} . \]

This falls only as \(1/r\), so it carries energy to any distance: it is the radiation.

The power. A radiation field carries energy flux \(\varepsilon_0c\mathcal{E}_\perp^2\) (W/m\(^2\)), with half of it in the magnetic field \(B = \mathcal{E}_\perp/c\) (the Poynting vector, F4, section 18). Over a sphere of radius \(r\), the area element is \(r^2\,d\Omega\) with \(d\Omega = \sin\Theta\,d\Theta\,d\varphi\) the element of solid angle (F2, section 13), and \(\int\sin^2\Theta\,d\Omega = 2\pi\int_0^\pi\sin^3\Theta\,d\Theta = 2\pi \times \tfrac{4}{3} = 8\pi/3\) (put \(u = \cos\Theta\): \(\int_{-1}^{1}(1 - u^2)\,du = 4/3\)); the factor \(r^2\) cancels the \(1/r^2\) of \(\mathcal{E}_\perp^2\), so

\[ P = \varepsilon_0c\left(\frac{qa}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c^2}\right)^2\frac{8\pi}{3} = \frac{q^2a^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0c^3} . \]

That is the Larmor formula, in watts for \(q\) in coulombs and \(a\) in m/s\(^2\). It assumes \(v \ll c\); at high speed the power rises by powers of \(\gamma\), which is the synchrotron radiation of chapter 20.

Same force, different mass. For a given force \(F\) the acceleration is \(a = F/m\), so

\[ P = \frac{q^2F^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0c^3m^2} . \]

An electron and a proton in the same field feel the same force and radiate in the ratio \((m_p/m_e)^2 = 3.37 \times 10^6\). At \(10^{-11}\) m from a silicon nucleus the Coulomb force on either is \(3.2 \times 10^{-5}\) N; the electron would radiate at 7.2 mW and the proton at 2.1 nW.

In PIXE. So the proton beam itself makes almost no bremsstrahlung (“braking radiation”: the X-rays a charge gives off when it is deflected and slowed by the nuclei it passes). What background remains comes mostly from the electrons the protons knock out of the target. A proton of kinetic energy \(W\) can give a free electron at most about \(4(m_e/m_p)W\) (a head-on elastic collision of a heavy body with a light one at rest sends the light one off at twice the heavy one’s speed, F3, section 6; its energy is then \(\tfrac{1}{2}m_e(2v)^2 = 4(m_e/m_p)\,\tfrac{1}{2}m_pv^2\)): 6.5 keV for 3 MeV protons. Their bremsstrahlung therefore dies away above a few keV, leaving the characteristic X-rays of heavier elements on a low background.

Exercises

L1. Under the same force, how much less does an alpha particle radiate than an electron?

Answer

\(P \propto q^2/m^2\) at fixed force. The alpha has twice the charge and \(m_\alpha/m_e = 7294\), so the electron radiates \(7294^2/4 = 1.3 \times 10^7\) times more.

L2. Find the largest energy a 2 MeV proton can give a free electron, and what that means for the bremsstrahlung background in PIXE at 2 MeV.

Answer

\(4(m_e/m_p)W = 4 \times 0.511/938.3 \times 2000\ \text{keV} = 4.4\) keV. The secondary electrons’ bremsstrahlung ends near 4 keV, so X-ray lines above that sit on a low background.

14. Uses, at the level of the physics

The applications are taken up in chapter 28; here are the physical reasons the tandem suits them.

Accelerator mass spectrometry (from 1977). Living carbon holds \(^{14}\)C at about \(1.2 \times 10^{-12}\) of \(^{12}\)C. Counting decays (half-life 5,730 years, F7, section 13; Sundquist, in Chao and others, section 1.6.5, table 1) is slow; counting atoms is fast if they can be separated from everything else of mass 14. Two facts make the tandem do it. \(^{14}\)N does not form a stable negative ion, so this isobar (a nucleus of the same mass number, here 14, which a magnet alone cannot tell apart) never leaves the source as a negative ion. And the stripper destroys molecular ions such as \(^{13}\)CH\(^-\) and \(^{12}\)CH\(_2^-\): stripped to 3+ they fly apart. In November 1977 two groups published in the same issue of Science: Nelson, Korteling and Stott with the McMaster tandem, and Bennett, Beukens, Clover, Gove and colleagues with the Rochester tandem; Muller had proposed the method with a cyclotron earlier that year.

Counting rates. A 30 µA beam of \(^{12}\)C\(^-\) is \(1.9 \times 10^{14}\) ions a second, so a modern sample sends 225 atoms of \(^{14}\)C a second toward the detector, before losses. At 50,000 years the fraction left is \(2^{-50000/5730} = 0.24\) per cent: about 1,900 an hour. At 2.5 MV, 3+ ions leave with 10 MeV; \(^{14}\)C\(^{3+}\) has \(B\rho = 0.568\) T m. A \(^{13}\)C\(^{3+}\) fragment of \(^{13}\)CH\(^-\) keeps \(13/14\) of the 2.5 MeV it arrived with, so it leaves with 9.82 MeV and \(B\rho = 0.542\) T m, 5 per cent lower.

Rutherford backscattering. A light ion of mass \(M_1\) and energy \(W_0\) scattered elastically through \(\theta\) by a nucleus \(M_2\) at rest leaves with \(W_1 = KW_0\); energy and momentum conservation give

\[ K = \left[\frac{\sqrt{M_2^2 - M_1^2\sin^2\theta} + M_1\cos\theta}{M_1 + M_2}\right]^2 . \]

For 2 MeV helium-4 at 170°, \(K\) is 0.566 off silicon (1.13 MeV) and 0.922 off gold (1.84 MeV). The scattered energy names the element; the energy lost on the way in and out gives depth. Rubin, Passell and Bailey used the elastic scattering of accelerated ions for chemical analysis of surfaces in 1957.

Particle-induced X-ray emission. Protons of a few MeV knock out inner-shell electrons, and the characteristic X-rays name the element. Johansson, Akselsson and Johansson reported trace analysis at the \(10^{-12}\) g level in 1970. Protons make far less bremsstrahlung background than electrons, because radiated power from a given force scales as the inverse square of the mass.

Nuclear reaction analysis. \(^1\)H(\(^{15}\)N,αγ)\(^{12}\)C has a resonance at 6.385 MeV only about 1.8 keV wide, signalled by 4.43 MeV gamma rays (Lanford and others, 1976). Raise the \(^{15}\)N energy a little above resonance and the ions come into resonance at the depth where they have lost the excess: the gamma yield against beam energy is the hydrogen profile. It works only because the beam’s energy is defined well inside the resonance width.

How it was researched and built

Princeton, 1929 to 1931. Van de Graaff worked largely alone, supported by Compton. He found pure silk for the first belt by burning samples to check that they were not loaded with tin salts; the 1931 pair cost under USD 100, obtained from Henry Smyth. In 1931 he followed Compton to MIT. His patent (US 1,991,236), written with help from Vannevar Bush, was issued in February 1935.

Round Hill, 1932 to 1937. On Colonel Edward H. R. Green’s estate at South Dartmouth, Massachusetts, an MIT group built the first giant in a former airship hangar: two aluminium spheres 4.6 m across on hollow insulating columns 7.6 m high and 1.8 m across, the spheres 13 m above the ground, each unit on a truck on a railway track. Designed for 10 MV, with paper belts charged at about 20 kV, it reached about 5.1 MV between its spheres and was first shown publicly on 28 November 1933 (Van de Graaff, Compton and Van Atta, 1933; Van Atta and others, 1936). After Green’s death it was moved in 1937 to MIT, enclosed, and run up to about 2.4 MV (Livingston and Blewett give beams of 2.75 MeV by 1940); in 1955 it went to the Museum of Science in Boston, where it still runs.

The Carnegie Institution. Tuve, Hafstad and Dahl fitted a Van de Graaff generator with an accelerating tube and by late 1932 were doing nuclear physics with it; their 1935 paper set out the high-voltage technique of the day.

Wisconsin, 1933 to 1940. After the vacuum machine, Herb turned to compressed gas in 1933. The first tank (1934), about 1 m across and 2 m long, reached about 750 kV in compressed air, and its 50 cm tube limited experiments to 400 keV (Herb, Parkinson and Kerst, 1935); with carbon tetrachloride added it reached 1 MV (Herb, 1971). The second, built in 1936 and 1937, 1.7 m across and 6.1 m long, introduced the column; the 1937 paper gives about 2.5 MV at most, and Herb later gave 2.6 MV with beam (Herb, Parkinson and Kerst, 1937; Herb, 1971). With graduate students, especially J. L. McKibben and C. M. Turner, a new machine with concentric intermediate electrodes was built in the same tank and gave 4.5 MV in 1940 (Herb and others, 1940; McKay gives 4.3 MV for steady running, and Livingston and Blewett, section 3-1, 4.0 MV), an energy that, according to Herb’s memoir, only cyclotrons surpassed until 1955. From 1943 to 1946 two Wisconsin machines ran around the clock at Los Alamos. At Princeton, Barton, Mueller and Van Atta had been the first to try pressure insulation, with a cylindrical terminal in a horizontal tank; it reached 1 MV in air at 7 atmospheres (Barton, Mueller and Van Atta, 1932, as given by Livingston and Blewett, section 3-1).

Industry, from 1946. Van de Graaff, Trump and Denis Robinson founded High Voltage Engineering Corporation (HVEC) in Cambridge, Massachusetts, in December 1946; production moved to Burlington in 1957. By 1967 more than 500 Van de Graaff machines were in use in more than 30 countries (Burrill, 1967). Chalk River in Canada bought HVEC’s first tandem, the EN, in 1956; it began operating in February 1959. The Atomic Energy Commission placed the first American tandem, an HVEC EN running from 1960, at Wisconsin, where Herb’s group had built a negative hydrogen ion source giving 20 µA in 1956, so that a 5 MV terminal could give 10 MeV protons. Larger HVEC tandems (FN, and the MP “Emperor” of the 1960s) followed, with inclined-field tubes.

Herb’s second programme, 1946 to 1971. Herb set out to remove the three sources of dirt and failure in a tank: oil vapour from diffusion pumps (he made the first successful getter-ion pump), organic seals in the tube (high-temperature bonding of alumina to metal), and the belt (metal carriers). A 1951 Wisconsin machine combining them went to 7 MV without its tube, but discharges damaged the tube at 5 MV and use was limited to 3 MV. In 1965 Herb, Ferry and T. Pauly founded National Electrostatics Corporation (NEC) in Middleton, Wisconsin. Its standard became the 1 MV column module (four interchangeable alumina-titanium posts) with a matching tube section and the Pelletron chain. Oscar Sala at the University of São Paulo placed the first order for a Pelletron for nuclear research. NEC had built about 130 Pelletrons by Herb’s death.

How one is designed today. The choices come from this history: a steel vessel of SF\(_6\) at about 6 to 7 bar; a column of identical modules of about 1 MV each; resistor or corona grading; baked metal-ceramic tubes in short sections; chain charging of a few hundred microamperes; a generating voltmeter, capacitive pickoff and slit control acting on corona needles; a 90° analysing magnet with image slits. A tandem adds a sputter source of negative ions (chapter 7) on an injection platform, a gas or foil stripper, and a high-energy magnet. The tank field is computed with finite-element electrostatic codes in the real geometry, keeping every surface well below breakdown and shielding the junctions where metal, insulator and gas meet. Most machines built now are 1 to 6 MV, for analysis, dating and implantation.

At the bench

This is drawn from working laboratories: Chalk River (1965), Oak Ridge and Daresbury (Jones, 1984), the Vivitron (1998), the ANU 14UD (2012 and its tank-opening reports), and NEC’s documentation.

The control desk. Terminal voltage from the generating voltmeter, in MV; charging current up and down the chain, and column current, in microamperes; corona probe current and triode grid voltage; the two slit currents on logarithmic amplifiers, about 1 nA to 100 µA; tank pressure, tube vacuum, X-ray monitors; the stabiliser’s mode (voltmeter, slit or automatic).

Bringing a machine up. Start the chain and raise the charge; the voltmeter climbs and the stabiliser holds the set point. Inject the beam, set the analysing magnet’s field (read with an NMR probe) for the wanted particle and energy, and centre the beam on the slits. Within 50 kV of the set point, with current on a slit, the stabiliser switches to slit control, and the terminal now follows the beam. The corona loop answers in seconds; faster ripple is handled through the capacitive pickoff.

Sparks. A full terminal spark shows everywhere at once: the voltmeter drops to zero, the beam vanishes, the tube vacuum gauges jump, and the stored energy (120 kJ at 20 MV at Oak Ridge) goes into the gas in microseconds, a little of it into the tube. Afterwards the machine may not return to its voltage: it has deconditioned. Damage accumulates as pitted tube electrodes, tracks on insulators, failed resistors, broken chain links. At Daresbury spark damage required replacing tube modules on several occasions. Spark gaps across every column gap and tube section, and metal toroids around the support posts, carry spark current around the delicate parts.

Conditioning. After assembly or a tank opening the voltage is raised slowly, held when microdischarges appear (X-ray bursts, flickers of column current, small voltage dips), and raised again when they die away; each removes a field emitter, but raised too fast the discharges make new damage. At the ANU in 1979, after closing up, the 14UD went at once to 13 MV and was conditioned in a couple of days to 14.28 MV. Hydrogen arc cleaning of the tube (about 50 mTorr, a 4 A arc, 1 to 2 hours) raised conditioning thresholds in small tests from under 22 to over 40 kV per centimetre of insulator.

Belt humidity and charging. A damp belt charges badly: its surface conducts, the sprayed charge leaks away, and the terminal stalls. Drying the tank gas is part of keeping a belt machine running, and the Vivitron fitted a better gas-drying system for reliability. The belt joint shows as a periodic disturbance, 0.3 s in every 9.76 s at the Vivitron, until rebalancing the charging supplies cut the standard deviation of the analysed current from 23 to 8 per cent.

Calibrating with a resonance. Put a thin aluminium target in the line with a gamma detector beside it. Set the magnet a little below 991.86 keV protons and step the field upward, counting gammas for a fixed charge on target at each step. Plot yield against field; the resonance fixes the magnet constant between \(B\) and \(p/q\). Check a second point, such as the 1880.4 keV lithium threshold with a neutron counter. Laboratories repeat this from time to time and after changes to the beam line.

The analysing magnet and its slits. At the Vivitron the slits were set at \(\pm 1.3\) mm by \(\pm 2.5\) mm and passed 97 per cent of the beam. Narrow slits define the energy better and pass less; too narrow, and the beam is lost whenever the voltage wanders.

A charge-state spectrum. Scan the high-energy magnet with a Faraday cup after it. Each charge state appears as a peak at the field for its \(B\rho\), with energy from \(W = W_{inj} + (1 + n_q)eV_T\). For iodine at 14 MV, peaks from 9+ to 13+, the largest at 11+ with about a quarter of the beam. A thinning foil shows as a drift of the peaks toward lower charge; a broken one as a jump in transmission. Then the foil changer is turned to a new foil.

Tank openings. When the foil magazine is spent, a chain breaks, a resistor fails or a spark has done damage, the tank is opened. At Chalk River the gas system for the MP tandem was designed to pump the SF\(_6\) back to storage, taking the tank from 90 psig to 5 torr in 6 hours; the tank is then filled with air, and after the work evacuated and refilled: about 14 hours of gas handling per cycle, against an estimated 16.5 for nitrogen-carbon dioxide. The Vivitron needed eight openings in 1996 and four in 1997, aiming at two a year; at Oak Ridge several months between openings was usual by 1984.

Handling sulphur hexafluoride. SF\(_6\) is non-toxic and stable to about 500 °C, but sparks and corona break it into products such as SOF\(_2\) and SO\(_2\)F\(_2\), which are toxic and corrosive; activated alumina in the gas circuit removes them (Ashbaugh and others, 1965). It is about five times as dense as air, so leaks collect at low points, where Chalk River put detectors, and its density loads the drive motor. It is stored as liquid: the storage tank for Chalk River’s MP tandem was designed to hold about 23 tonnes. It is also a very strong greenhouse gas, so it is recovered rather than vented.

Failure signatures. No rise with charging on: a damp belt or worn spray points. A terminal stuck at one voltage while the X-rays rise: electron loading. Ripple at the belt’s period: the joint. Sudden collapse and slow recovery: a spark and deconditioning. Beam steering that wanders with voltage: a shorted column gap, so the tube field is no longer uniform. Charge state lower than expected: a thick or damaged foil, or too little stripper gas.

Where it stops

The limit is the field at the surfaces, and pushing it costs volume and stored energy. At fixed breakdown field the voltage grows with the tank’s size, the tank’s volume with the cube of the design voltage, and the energy of a spark roughly with the design voltage times the operating voltage squared. The largest machines reached 20 to 25 MV; the Vivitron, designed for 35 MV, ran below 20 MV. A 25 MV tandem gives 50 MeV protons, but for heavy ions the energy needed to reach the Coulomb barrier of uranium, about 6 MeV per nucleon, is out of reach above medium masses.

The way past is to stop holding the whole voltage at once. If a particle crosses a modest voltage many times, in step with an alternating field, no part of the machine holds more than that modest voltage while the energy keeps growing. Ising proposed it in 1924 and Wideroe built it in 1927 and 1928: chapter 9, and its circular form, the cyclotron, in chapter 10. The large tandems lived on as precise heavy-ion injectors for cyclotron and linear-accelerator boosters (chapter 14); Oak Ridge’s fed a cyclotron.

Whatever the accelerator, two things come before and after it and set what it can do: the source that makes the ions or electrons, which sets the current and the beam’s quality for good (chapter 7), and the instruments that show where the beam is and what it is (chapter 8).

Pre-requisite (continued): Counting statistics (Poisson)

Pre-requisite (continued). The Poisson distribution, the \(\sqrt N\) uncertainty of \(N\) counts and the counting time \(1/(\epsilon^2r)\) for a relative precision \(\epsilon\) at a rate \(r\) were taught in chapter 1, and the decay law with a half-life in chapter 1’s section on nuclear reactions. Both are also in Part 0: the Poisson distribution in F6, section 6 and the decay law in F7, section 13. This section recalls the decay law and the Poisson distribution briefly and adds what a background does to the time a measurement takes. Exercise 7 and the counting rates of section 14 use them. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz volume 5, section 114 (Poisson’s formula), and Chao and others, Handbook, section 1.6.5 (backgrounds in accelerator mass spectrometry).

The decay law with a half-life. Each radioactive nucleus decays with the same chance \(\lambda\,dt\) in a time \(dt\), independently of its age, so \(dN/dt = -\lambda N\) and \(N = N_0e^{-\lambda t}\). The half-life \(t_{1/2}\) is the time in which half go: \(e^{-\lambda t_{1/2}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\), so \(\lambda = \ln 2/t_{1/2}\) and

\[ \frac{N}{N_0} = e^{-t\ln 2/t_{1/2}} = 2^{-t/t_{1/2}} . \]

The second form reads at a glance: after \(n\) half-lives, \(2^{-n}\) remains. For carbon-14 (\(t_{1/2} = 5730\) years), 50,000 years is 8.73 half-lives and leaves \(2.36 \times 10^{-3}\).

The Poisson distribution. Suppose events occur independently, each with a small chance in any short interval, at an average of \(\bar N\) in the counting time. The chance of counting exactly \(k\) is

\[ P(k) = \frac{\bar N^k e^{-\bar N}}{k!} . \]

Landau and Lifshitz derive it as the chance of finding \(k\) molecules of a dilute gas in a small part of its volume; atoms of \(^{14}\)C arriving at a detector are the same problem. Summing, the mean is \(\sum kP(k) = \bar N\) and the variance \(\sum(k - \bar N)^2P(k) = \bar N\), which gives the \(\sqrt N\) of chapter 1. For a mean of 2: \(P(0) = 13.5\) per cent, \(P(1) = P(2) = 27.1\) per cent, \(P(3) = 18.0\) per cent.

Signal and background. A measurement counts \(G\) events in time \(t\): the wanted signal at rate \(s\), plus background at rate \(b\) from everything else that reaches the detector looking like the signal. The background rate is measured separately, on a blank, and subtracted: the signal is \(S = G - bt\). If \(b\) is known well, the uncertainty of \(S\) is that of \(G\), \(\sqrt{G} = \sqrt{(s + b)t}\), and the relative error is

\[ \frac{\sqrt{(s + b)t}}{st} . \]

To reach a relative precision \(\epsilon\) takes

\[ t = \frac{s + b}{\epsilon^2s^2} , \]

which is \(1/(\epsilon^2 s)\) without background and grows by the factor \(1 + b/s\) with it. Measuring the background itself also takes time, and its uncertainty adds; this formula is the best case.

Accelerator mass spectrometry. Exercise 7’s sample, 50,000 years old, gives \(s = 0.53\) counts a second from 30 µA of \(^{12}\)C\(^-\). The Handbook gives backgrounds near \(5 \times 10^{-16}\) in \(^{14}\)C/\(^{12}\)C, which with \(1.87 \times 10^{14}\) carbon ions a second is \(b = 0.094\) a second: 18 per cent of the signal. A 1 per cent measurement then takes 6.2 hours instead of 5.2. The background also sets the oldest age that can be measured: the signal falls to the background when \(1.2 \times 10^{-12} \times 2^{-t/5730} = 5 \times 10^{-16}\), at about 64,000 years.

Exercises

M1. If the background rate equals the signal rate, how much longer does a measurement of given precision take than with no background?

Answer

\(t = (s + b)/(\epsilon^2s^2)\) with \(b = s\) is \(2/(\epsilon^2s)\): twice as long, and that assumes the background is known exactly.

M2. A sample is expected to give a mean of 2 counts in a run. What is the chance of seeing none? Of seeing at least one?

Answer

\(P(0) = e^{-2} = 0.135\), so the chance of at least one is 0.865. A single empty run says little when the expected count is that small.

Exercises

1. A belt 0.5 m wide runs at 20 m/s in air at one atmosphere. What is the largest charging current it can carry?

Answer

\(\sigma_s \le \varepsilon_0 \mathcal{E}_b = 8.854 \times 10^{-12} \times 3 \times 10^6 = 26.6\) µC/m\(^2\), so \(I = \sigma_s w v_b = 26.6 \times 10^{-6} \times 0.5 \times 20 = 266\) µA. Real belts run well below this, because of field concentration at edges and pulleys.

2. Show that a spherical terminal in a spherical tank of radius \(b\) has the least surface field at radius \(b/2\), and find the tank radius needed for 10 MV if the surface field must stay below 15 MV/m. Compare a coaxial geometry.

Answer

\(\mathcal{E}(a) = Vb/[a(b-a)]\); \(a(b-a)\) is largest where \(b - 2a = 0\), so \(a = b/2\) and \(\mathcal{E} = 4V/b\). Then \(b = 4V/\mathcal{E} = 4 \times 10/15 = 2.67\) m, a tank 5.3 m across. For cylinders \(b = eV/\mathcal{E} = 1.81\) m: the field of a cylinder falls as \(1/r\) rather than \(1/r^2\), so the voltage is spread more evenly across the gap.

3. A tandem has 14 MV on its terminal. Neglecting injection energy, find the energies and magnetic rigidities of protons, of \(^{12}\)C\(^{6+}\) and of \(^{16}\)O\(^{8+}\).

Answer

\(W = (1 + n_q)eV_T\): 28, 98 and 126 MeV. With \(pc = \sqrt{W(W + 2mc^2)}\) and \(B\rho = p/(n_q e)\): 0.770, 0.825 and 0.810 T m. Non-relativistically \((B\rho)^2 = 2mW/q^2 = 2m(1 + n_q)V_T/(n_q^2 e)\); a fully stripped light ion has \(m \approx 2n_q m_p\), so \((B\rho)^2 \approx 4m_p(1 + 1/n_q)V_T/e\), against \(4m_pV_T/e\) for protons. The rigidities differ only by the factor \(\sqrt{1 + 1/n_q}\): 8 per cent more for carbon and 6 per cent more for oxygen. The charge-to-mass ratio is not the reason (the proton’s is twice carbon’s); the heavier ion’s larger momentum is offset by its larger charge.

4. The 28 MeV protons are analysed with a dispersion of 10.6 m at the slits. What slit half-width corresponds to an energy band of \(\pm 1 \times 10^{-4}\)?

Answer

\(\gamma = 1 + W/m_pc^2 = 1.0298\), so \(\Delta W/W = (1 + 1/\gamma)\delta = 1.971\,\delta\). For \(10^{-4}\), \(\delta = 5.07 \times 10^{-5}\) and \(\Delta x = D_x\delta = 0.54\) mm.

5. From the \(^7\)Li(p,n) threshold of 1880.4 keV, find \(Q_{nuc}\) with the non-relativistic threshold formula, and the energy that one part in \(10^4\) represents there.

Answer

\(W_{th} = -Q_{nuc}(1 + m_p/m_{Li})\) with nuclear masses 1.00728 u and 7.01436 u: \(1 + m_p/m_{Li} = 1.1436\), so \(Q_{nuc} = -1880.4/1.1436 = -1644.3\) keV (relativistically \(-1644.1\) keV). One part in \(10^4\) is 188 eV.

6. Oak Ridge’s tandem stored about 120 kJ at 20 MV. Find its capacitance and terminal charge, the stored energy at 25 MV, and the time for 100 µA to charge it from zero to 20 MV with no load.

Answer

\(C = 2U/V^2 = 2 \times 1.2 \times 10^5/(2 \times 10^7)^2 = 600\) pF; \(Q = CV = 12\) mC; at 25 MV, \(U = 188\) kJ; \(t = Q/I = 0.012/10^{-4} = 120\) s. The column current also draws on the charging current, so in practice it takes longer.

7. In an AMS measurement 30 µA of \(^{12}\)C\(^-\) leaves the source. With perfect transmission, how many \(^{14}\)C atoms an hour come from a sample 50,000 years old, and how long does a 1 per cent measurement take?

Answer

\(30\ \text{µA}/e = 1.87 \times 10^{14}\) ions/s; modern carbon gives \(225\) \(^{14}\)C/s. The surviving fraction is \(2^{-50000/5730} = 2.36 \times 10^{-3}\), so 0.53/s or 1,910 an hour. One per cent needs \(10^4\) counts (\(1/\sqrt N\)), about 5.2 hours, and a background far below 0.5 counts a second.

8. For a coaxial terminal of radius \(a\) in a grounded cylinder of radius \(b\), show that the surface field is least at \(a = b/e\). For the Daresbury vessel (\(b = 4.1\) m), compare the best fields at 30 MV without a shield and with one optimised shield (peak field \(1.88\,V/b\)), and the unshielded field at 20 MV.

Answer

\(\mathcal{E}(a) = V/[a\ln(b/a)]\); \(a\ln(b/a)\) has derivative \(\ln(b/a) - 1\), zero at \(a = b/e\), where \(\mathcal{E} = eV/b\). At 30 MV: 19.9 MV/m unshielded, 13.8 MV/m shielded. At 20 MV unshielded: 13.3 MV/m. The shield lets 30 MV run at about the field that 20 MV needs without it.

9. 2 MeV helium-4 ions backscatter through 170°. Find the scattered energies from gold and platinum, and explain why RBS separates them poorly.

Answer

With \(M_1 = 4.003\): gold (196.97) \(K = 0.9225\), 1.845 MeV; platinum (195.08) \(K = 0.9218\), 1.844 MeV, only 1.4 keV apart, below the 10 to 20 keV resolution of a typical silicon detector. As \(M_2 \gg M_1\), \(K \to 1\) and \(dK/dM_2 \to 0\): a heavy nucleus barely recoils, whatever its exact mass.
