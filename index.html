The physics of particle accelerators: course notes

These are course notes on the physics of particle accelerators, from the simplest machine to the most complex: a glass tube with a hot wire in it, then the multiplier and the belt generator, the cyclotron and the linac, the synchrotron and the collider, the free-electron laser and the plasma wake. Each chapter explains how one kind of machine works, derives the mathematics that matters, puts numbers from a real machine through every important formula, and tells how the machine was researched and built: who did it, where, when, with what, and what went wrong.

They are reference material to study from. They are not a plan and not a design. The plan is the decisions in docs/decisions/, and nothing here decides anything.

How to read it

To read the notes as a PDF book, on a computer, tablet or phone, run ./docs/course/build.sh from the repository root. It makes build/course/course.pdf, with a table of contents, an outline for jumping around, and links between chapters. The answers to the exercises are at the end of each chapter. To build one chapter, give its number: ./docs/course/build.sh 02. To read them as a website instead, with each chapter on its own page, the sections beside the text and the answers folded under each exercise, run ./docs/course/build.sh site: it makes build/course/site/index.html. The website is published at https://vfaizin.github.io/accelerator-course/; ./docs/course/publish-pages.sh rebuilds it and publishes the new version there. The Markdown here stays the only source: edit it and build again.

Start with Part 0, Foundations, for any tool you have not met: each of its chapters assumes only high-school mathematics and physics and the Foundations chapters before it. Then read the In one page section at the top of every chapter, in order, from chapter 1 to chapter 29. That takes an evening and gives the whole map: each machine, the limit that stopped it, the idea that got past the limit, the equations to remember and the numbers worth knowing by heart.

Then read the parts in order. The course is built so that each chapter begins at the limit where the one before it stopped, and later chapters use earlier ones without repeating them: the phase stability of chapter 12 is assumed by every ring after it, and the optics of chapter 16 by every chapter from 17 on. Parts V to VII rest on Part IV; after it, chapters 23 to 25 are best read in order, and chapters 26 to 29 in any order. Chapter 1 holds the relativity, rigidity, centre-of-mass energy and cross-sections that the rest of the course uses; read all of it, not only its first page.

Do the exercises. Most take a few lines and a calculator; the answers are folded underneath, to be opened after trying.

What you need first

High-school mathematics and physics, at the level of SPM Additional Mathematics or A-level: algebra, graphs, powers and logarithms, trigonometry, and the ideas of force, speed, acceleration and energy. Nothing more is assumed. Everything after that, from calculus and vectors to Maxwell’s equations, special relativity, and Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics, is taught in the notes: first in Part 0, Foundations, which gives the first-year toolkit, and then in the “Pre-requisite” sections inside each chapter, placed just before the topic that first needs them.

The books in references/ (local only, not tracked by git) are further reading, for going deeper than the notes, not something to read first:

  • Landau and Lifshitz. Mechanics (volume 1) for small oscillations, parametric resonance, nonlinear oscillations, adiabatic invariants and Liouville’s theorem: these are the betatron oscillation, the resonances of chapter 18, the shrinking of a beam as it accelerates, and the conservation of emittance. The Classical Theory of Fields (volume 2) for relativistic mechanics, the motion of a charge in electric and magnetic fields, and the radiation of a moving charge, which is the subject of chapter 20. Statistical Physics (volume 5) for the gas kinetics behind chapter 3 and the temperature of a beam in chapter 24.
  • David Tong, Lectures on Theoretical Physics. Classical Mechanics (volume 1) for the Hamiltonian view and phase space; Electromagnetism (volume 2) for fields in conductors, waves in guides and cavities, and radiation.

Appendix C gives, for each part, the sections of these books and the standard accelerator texts that go deeper. Several of those are also in references/accelerators/: Livingston and Blewett’s Particle Accelerators (1962), S. Y. Lee’s Accelerator Physics, Chao and Tigner’s Handbook of Accelerator Physics and Engineering (third edition), Wangler’s RF Linear Accelerators, two books on RF superconductivity, Martin N. Wilson’s Superconducting Magnets, and books on ion sources, vacuum technology and high-voltage engineering.

Nothing about accelerators is assumed. Every term is explained at its first use.

Contents

The years after each chapter are the span of history it covers. Most chapters also carry the physics forward to what is used today.

The family tree of machines

Every accelerator gives energy to a charged particle with an electric field. There are only a few ways to make that field, and each branch below is one of them. Direct voltage uses one potential difference once, and is limited by breakdown. Resonance uses a modest alternating voltage many times, on a particle that arrives in step. Induction makes the electric field from a changing magnetic flux. Rings combine resonance with a magnetic field that rises with the momentum, so the particle comes back to the same gap. The new methods look for fields that metal cannot hold, or for particles and geometries the older machines could not use.

Notes on the years, all taken from the chapters, where the sources are given:

  • 1897 is Braun’s tube and Thomson’s measurement; the tube was an instrument only by the 1920s (chapter 2).
  • 1929 is Van de Graaff’s first model, 80 kV; his pair of spheres reached about 1.5 MV in 1931 (chapter 6). 1959 is the first HVEC tandem, at Chalk River, in operation.
  • 1928 is Wideroe’s paper on the machine he built at Aachen for his doctorate of 1927 (chapter 9). 1931 is Livingston’s 4-inch cyclotron, 80 keV (chapter 10).
  • 1958 is the Delft isochronous cyclotron, the first in the open; classified prototypes came earlier, and some sources date Delft 1956 to 1958 (chapter 12).
  • 1995 is CEBAF at its 4 GeV design energy; superconducting cavities had driven a smaller linac at Stanford in the 1970s (chapter 13, chapter 14).
  • 1946 is Goward and Barnes’s betatron converted to an 8 MeV synchrotron at Woolwich (chapter 15). 1959 is the CERN PS at 24 GeV; the idea is of 1950 to 1952, and Cornell’s electron ring may have run earlier, a date the chapter could not confirm (chapter 16).
  • 1961 is AdA, the first stored beam meant for collisions; the ISR, the first hadron collider, followed in 1971 (chapter 23). 1980 is the first ring built as a light source, at Daresbury (chapter 20).
  • 1977 is the first FEL oscillator, at Stanford; 1999 is the Jefferson Lab FEL recovering its beam’s energy, an idea of Tigner’s from 1965 (chapter 26).
  • 1963 is the Astron injector at Livermore; 1956 the first MURA electron model; 1979 Tajima and Dawson’s proposal; 2013 the first dielectric laser acceleration above 250 MeV/m; 2020 the MICE result on ionisation cooling. No muon collider has been built (chapter 27).

How every chapter is built

Each of the 29 chapters has the same sections, in the same order. The Foundations chapters of Part 0 follow the same plan with two changes: The wall becomes The problem it solved, a short history of the mathematics or physics, and How it was researched and built, At the bench and Where it stops become a table of where the course uses each tool.

  • The title and a line in italics. The years the chapter covers, the machines and people in it, and the wall it gets past.
  • In one page. A summary to read first and come back to: the wall (what stopped the machines before it), the idea (what got past), the equations to remember, with every symbol and unit given, and the numbers worth knowing, from real machines.
  • The wall. The history of the limit: what was tried, what failed and why, told with dates and the people involved.
  • The physics. The core of the chapter, in numbered sections. The physical picture comes before each equation; every symbol is defined at its first use with its unit; the results that matter are derived step by step; a result quoted without derivation says so and says where the derivation is; every approximation is stated with where it fails. After each important formula, a worked number from a real machine.
  • How it was researched and built. The laboratories, the people, the years, the parts and the money: how the idea was found, tested, built at full size and made to work, and what went wrong on the way.
  • At the bench. What working at such a machine was like in a real laboratory: what there was to read and set, how it was tuned and commissioned, the ways it failed and the signature of each failure.
  • Where it stops. The physical or practical limit the machine reaches, with numbers, and the idea that the next chapter brings to get past it.
  • Figures. Throughout, a figure wherever a picture does the work of a paragraph: the machine’s layout, the geometry of a derivation, a field, a phase-space picture, or a plot computed from the chapter’s own formula and numbers. Each is drawn as code in figures/ and has its caption in italics under it.
  • Exercises. Seven to ten problems, mostly numerical, with worked answers folded underneath each one.
  • Sources. The original papers, the standard texts with sections, and secondary sources, each with what it was used for. Sources free to read online are marked (free).

How the notes were made and checked

The notes were written and checked by AI agents on 2026-10-22, to an agreed outline and a shared notation. Each chapter’s numbers were then recomputed in Python with the CODATA 2018 constants of scipy.constants, and its facts, dates, names, machine parameters and citations were checked against sources: the original papers where they could be reached, the standard texts, and the proceedings of the CERN Accelerator School and the accelerator conferences. Where a figure could not be confirmed, the chapter gives a range or says so.

Errors may remain. A chapter can be wrong where its sources disagree, where a source could not be reached, or where an agent misread one. Treat a surprising claim as something to verify, and go to the sources listed at the end of the chapter before relying on a number. Corrections are welcome in the files themselves.

Notation in brief

The whole course shares one notation, set out in full in Appendix A. The essentials:

  • The particle. Charge \(q\) (the elementary charge \(e\) is positive), rest mass \(m\); \(\beta = v/c\), \(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-\beta^2}\); total energy \(E = \gamma m c^2\), kinetic energy \(W = (\gamma - 1) m c^2\), momentum \(p = \gamma m v\). \(W\) is always kinetic energy and \(T\) always temperature.
  • Units. SI throughout, never Gaussian. Energies in eV with prefixes, momenta in eV/\(c\). Magnetic rigidity \(B\rho = p/q\) in T m; for a singly charged particle \(B\rho\,[\mathrm{T\,m}] = 3.3356\,p\,[\mathrm{GeV}/c]\).
  • Coordinates. \(s\) along the reference orbit, \(x\) horizontal (outward on a ring), \(y\) vertical, \(x' = dx/ds\); bending radius \(\rho\); average radius of a ring \(R = C/2\pi\).
  • Optics. The Courant-Snyder functions \(\beta_x\), \(\alpha_x\), \(\gamma_x\) always carry a subscript \(x\) or \(y\), to keep them apart from the relativistic \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\). Tunes \(Q_x\), \(Q_y\) and the synchrotron tune \(Q_s\); emittance \(\varepsilon\) and normalised emittance \(\varepsilon_n = \beta\gamma\varepsilon\); dispersion \(D_x\); \(\delta = \Delta p/p\); momentum compaction \(\alpha_c\) and phase-slip factor \(\eta = \alpha_c - 1/\gamma^2\); quadrupole gradient \(g\) in T/m and strength \(k = g/(B\rho)\) in m\(^{-2}\); field index \(n\).
  • Longitudinal motion. The energy gain at a gap is \(\Delta W = q\hat{V}\sin\phi\), with the phase \(\phi\) increasing for later arrival, so a stable phase lies between \(0\) and \(\pi/2\) below transition and between \(\pi/2\) and \(\pi\) above it. Linac texts often use a cosine with negative phases; the chapters convert where it matters.
  • Cavities. \(Q_0\), \(Q_L\) and \(Q_{ext}\), never a bare \(Q\); shunt impedance \(R_{sh} = \hat{V}^2/P\) (the linac definition; circuit texts use half of it); transit-time factor \(T_t\).
  • Colliders. Luminosity \(\mathcal{L}\) in cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\); beam sizes \(\sigma_x\), \(\sigma_y\), \(\sigma_z\), \(\sigma_\delta\), always subscripted; beam-beam parameter \(\xi\); \(N\) particles per bunch, \(n_b\) bunches.

Where a chapter must use a symbol for something else (a pressure \(p\), a skin depth \(\delta\), a gap length \(g\)), it says so where the symbol first appears.
