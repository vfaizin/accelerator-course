F1 Calculus

1665 to 1821: Newton’s fluxions, Leibniz’s differentials, Cauchy’s limits. The mathematics of anything that changes: how fast it changes, how much it adds up to, and how to approximate it. Every chapter of the course after this one uses it.

What this chapter assumes. High-school mathematics and physics, at the level of SPM Additional Mathematics or A-level: algebra, graphs of functions, powers and roots, logarithms, trigonometry (sine, cosine and tangent, and the identity \(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1\)), and the ideas of force, speed, acceleration and energy. Nothing else. Section 0 lists exactly what is used, with a short check.

What comes next. F2 Vectors and vector calculus uses everything here in three dimensions. The pre-requisite sections inside the chapters (for example Ordinary differential equations in chapter 2) take the last sections of this chapter further.

In one page

The problem. Physics is about things that change: the position of an electron between two plates, the pressure in a chamber being pumped, the number of atoms left in a radioactive source. Algebra handles quantities that are fixed or change at a steady rate. It cannot say how fast something is changing at one instant when the rate itself is changing, and it cannot add up a quantity whose rate keeps changing. Calculus does both.

The idea. Look closely enough at any smooth curve and it looks straight. Over a small enough step, every changing quantity changes at a steady rate, and algebra works again. The derivative is the rate over a step so small that the curve is straight. The integral adds up many such small steps. The two are inverse operations: this is the fundamental theorem of calculus. Once it is understood, differential equations (laws that say how a quantity changes) can be solved, and functions can be approximated by polynomials (Taylor series), which is how most formulas in the course are simplified.

Equations to remember.

  • Derivative: \(\dfrac{df}{dx} = \lim_{h\to 0}\dfrac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}\), the slope of the curve \(y = f(x)\) at \(x\).
  • Rules: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}x^n = nx^{n-1}\); \((fg)' = f'g + fg'\); \(\dfrac{d}{dx}f(g(x)) = f'(g(x))\,g'(x)\).
  • \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x\) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos x = -\sin x\), with \(x\) in radians; \(\dfrac{d}{dx}e^x = e^x\); \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac{1}{x}\).
  • Small changes: \(\Delta f \approx f'(x)\,\Delta x\); for \(f = x^n\), \(\dfrac{\Delta f}{f} \approx n\dfrac{\Delta x}{x}\).
  • Fundamental theorem: \(\displaystyle\int_a^b f'(x)\,dx = f(b) - f(a)\).
  • Taylor series: \(f(a + h) = f(a) + f'(a)\,h + \tfrac{1}{2}f''(a)\,h^2 + \tfrac{1}{6}f'''(a)\,h^3 + \dots\); in particular \((1+x)^n \approx 1 + nx\) for small \(x\).
  • Decay: \(\dfrac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N\) has the solution \(N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}\), with time constant \(\tau = 1/\lambda\).
  • Oscillation: \(\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -\omega^2 x\) has the solution \(x = A\cos\omega t + B\sin\omega t\).

Numbers worth knowing.

  • \(e = 2.71828\); \(\ln 2 = 0.6931\), \(\ln 10 = 2.3026\).
  • An electron in a field of 200 kV/m (2 kV across 1 cm) accelerates at \(3.52 \times 10^{16}\) m/s\(^2\) and crosses the centimetre in 0.754 ns.
  • A 100-litre chamber pumped at 50 litres per second loses pressure with a time constant of 2 s, and falls from atmospheric pressure to 1 Pa in 23 s, if nothing else feeds gas in.
  • \(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - \beta^2} \approx 1 + \beta^2/2\) for small \(\beta\); at \(\beta = 0.5\) this gives 1.125 against the true 1.1547.
  • At 2500 K, a 1 per cent rise in a tungsten cathode’s temperature raises its current by 23 per cent on the small-change rule and by 26 per cent exactly: small-change rules fail when the change is not small.

The problem it solved

Motion that will not hold still

Galileo, around 1604, found that a ball rolling down a ramp covers distances in proportion to the square of the time. He could state that, but he had no general way to say how fast the ball was going at a given instant, since its speed was different at every instant. Kepler, in 1609, found that a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times. Adding up an area bounded by a curve was a problem the Greeks had solved only for special shapes, by long and clever arguments.

Newton and Leibniz

Isaac Newton, in 1665 and 1666, with Cambridge closed by plague, worked out what he called the method of fluxions: a “fluent” quantity flows in time, and its “fluxion” is its rate of flow. He used it for tangents, areas, and later for the motion of the planets, but published little of it for decades. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz developed the same mathematics independently and published first, in 1684, in a paper titled Nova Methodus pro Maximis et Minimis (A new method for maxima and minima). His notation, \(dy/dx\) for a rate and \(\int y\,dx\) for a sum (the long S of summa), is the one used today. The two men and their followers fought for decades over who had priority. Both had it: Newton first, Leibniz independently and in print.

Making it sound

Neither Newton nor Leibniz could say exactly what a quantity “infinitely small but not zero” was, and the bishop George Berkeley mocked them for it in 1734. The answer came in 1821, when Augustin-Louis Cauchy’s Cours d’analyse built calculus on the limit: not a number that is infinitely small, but what a ratio approaches as a step is made as small as one likes. That is the version taught here.

The mathematics

0. What this chapter assumes, and a quick check

Everything here is built from high-school mathematics. These are the facts used, in the form they are used.

  • Powers. \(x^a x^b = x^{a+b}\), \((x^a)^b = x^{ab}\), \(x^{-a} = 1/x^a\), \(x^{1/2} = \sqrt{x}\), \(x^0 = 1\).
  • Logarithms. \(\log_{10} 1000 = 3\) because \(10^3 = 1000\). The rules: \(\log(xy) = \log x + \log y\), \(\log(x^n) = n\log x\). Section 4 introduces the natural logarithm \(\ln\), the logarithm to base \(e\).
  • Trigonometry. In a right-angled triangle with hypotenuse 1 and angle \(\theta\), the sides are \(\sin\theta\) and \(\cos\theta\). Then \(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1\), and the addition formulas are \(\sin(a + b) = \sin a\cos b + \cos a\sin b\) and \(\cos(a + b) = \cos a\cos b - \sin a\sin b\).
  • Straight lines. The line through \((x_1, y_1)\) and \((x_2, y_2)\) has slope \((y_2 - y_1)/(x_2 - x_1)\). A line with slope \(m\) rises \(m\) units for every unit along.
  • Speed and acceleration. At a steady speed \(v\), distance is \(vt\). With a steady acceleration \(a\) from rest, the speed is \(at\) and the distance \(\tfrac12 at^2\).

Check yourself. (a) Simplify \(x^3/x^{-2}\). (b) Solve \(10^x = 50\). (c) If \(\sin\theta = 0.6\) and \(\theta\) is acute, find \(\cos\theta\). (d) Find the slope of the line through \((1, 2)\) and \((4, 11)\). Answers: (a) \(x^5\); (b) \(x = \log_{10}50 = 1.699\); (c) \(0.8\); (d) \(3\). If any of these is unclear, revise it first; the rest of the chapter builds on all four.

1. Rate of change: the derivative

The picture. A car’s speedometer shows the speed at this instant, not the average over the trip. How can a speed belong to an instant, when in an instant the car goes nowhere? The answer is to take the average speed over a short interval, then a shorter one, then a shorter one, and see what number the averages settle on.

An example. An electron starts at rest between two parallel plates 1 cm apart, with 2 kV between them. The field, the voltage per metre, is \(\mathcal{E} = 2000/0.01 = 2 \times 10^5\) V/m. (The script letter \(\mathcal{E}\) is the course’s symbol for electric field, keeping \(E\) for energy.) The force on the electron is \(e\mathcal{E}\), and Newton’s second law gives its acceleration \(a = e\mathcal{E}/m_e\). With \(e/m_e = 1.7588 \times 10^{11}\) C/kg,

\[ a = 1.7588 \times 10^{11} \times 2 \times 10^5 = 3.518 \times 10^{16}\ \text{m/s}^2 . \]

That is \(3.6 \times 10^{15}\) times the acceleration of gravity, so gravity can be forgotten. From high-school physics, its distance from the starting plate after time \(t\) is \(x(t) = \tfrac12 at^2\).

What is its speed at \(t = 0.5\) ns? Take the average speed over a short interval from \(t\) to \(t + h\):

\[ \frac{x(t+h) - x(t)}{h} = \frac{\tfrac12 a (t+h)^2 - \tfrac12 a t^2}{h} = \frac{\tfrac12 a (2th + h^2)}{h} = at + \tfrac12 ah . \]

The average depends on the interval \(h\). But as \(h\) is made smaller, the term \(\tfrac12 ah\) shrinks towards zero and the average settles on \(at\). That number is the speed at the instant \(t\): \(v = at = 3.518 \times 10^{16} \times 0.5 \times 10^{-9} = 1.76 \times 10^7\) m/s.

The definition. For any function \(f(x)\), the derivative at \(x\) is

\[ f'(x) = \frac{df}{dx} = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} . \]

The symbol \(\lim_{h \to 0}\) means: the number the fraction approaches as \(h\) is made as small as one likes, without \(h\) ever being zero. (At \(h = 0\) the fraction would be \(0/0\), which means nothing; the limit asks what happens on the way there.) Geometrically, \((f(x+h) - f(x))/h\) is the slope of the straight line through two points of the curve, and as \(h\) shrinks the line turns into the tangent, the straight line that just touches the curve at \(x\). The derivative is the slope of the tangent.

Notation. Three notations are in common use, and the course uses all of them:

  • \(f'(x)\), Lagrange’s, for a function of one variable.
  • \(\dfrac{df}{dx}\), Leibniz’s. It reads as “a small change in \(f\) divided by the small change in \(x\) that caused it”, which is exactly what it is in the limit. Its units are the units of \(f\) divided by the units of \(x\): a derivative of metres with respect to seconds is in m/s.
  • \(\dot x\), Newton’s dot, for a derivative with respect to time only: \(\dot x = dx/dt\) is a speed, and two dots, \(\ddot x = d^2x/dt^2\), the derivative of the derivative, is an acceleration.

The second derivative. Differentiate twice and the result is the rate at which the slope changes, written \(f''(x)\) or \(d^2f/dx^2\). For the electron, \(\dot x = at\) and \(\ddot x = a\): the speed grows at the steady rate \(a\), as it should. Where \(f'' > 0\) the curve bends upwards (the slope is increasing); where \(f'' < 0\) it bends downwards.

When there is no derivative. The limit has to exist. At a sharp corner, like \(|x|\) at \(x = 0\), the slope from the left is \(-1\) and from the right is \(+1\), and no single tangent exists. Physical quantities are almost always smooth enough; where they are not (a voltage switched on at an instant, the edge of a magnet’s field in a simple model), the course says so.

2. The rules for derivatives

Working out every derivative from the limit would be slow. A few rules, each proved once from the definition, handle every function in the course.

Constants and sums. A constant does not change, so its derivative is 0. Multiplying a function by a constant multiplies its slope by the same constant: \((cf)' = cf'\). The slope of a sum is the sum of the slopes: \((f + g)' = f' + g'\).

Powers. For \(f(x) = x^n\) with \(n\) a whole number, expand \((x + h)^n\) by the binomial theorem: \((x + h)^n = x^n + nx^{n-1}h + (\text{terms with } h^2, h^3, \dots)\). Then

\[ \frac{(x+h)^n - x^n}{h} = nx^{n-1} + (\text{terms with } h, h^2, \dots) \ \longrightarrow\ nx^{n-1} . \]

So \(\dfrac{d}{dx}x^n = nx^{n-1}\). The same rule holds for any real power \(n\), including negative and fractional ones (section 4 shows why): \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\dfrac{1}{x} = -\dfrac{1}{x^2}\) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sqrt{x} = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\).

Products. For \(f(x)g(x)\), the change over a small step is

\[ f(x+h)g(x+h) - f(x)g(x) = \big[f(x+h) - f(x)\big]g(x+h) + f(x)\big[g(x+h) - g(x)\big] . \]

Divide by \(h\) and let \(h \to 0\):

\[ (fg)' = f'g + fg' . \]

Picture a rectangle of sides \(f\) and \(g\). When both sides grow a little, the area grows by a strip along each side, \(g\,\Delta f\) and \(f\,\Delta g\), plus a tiny corner \(\Delta f\,\Delta g\) that vanishes faster than the strips.

Quotients. From the product rule applied to \(f = (f/g)\,g\):

\[ \left(\frac{f}{g}\right)' = \frac{f'g - fg'}{g^2} . \]

The chain rule. Most functions in physics are functions of functions. The Lorentz factor \(\gamma = (1 - \beta^2)^{-1/2}\) is a power of \(u = 1 - \beta^2\), which is itself a function of \(\beta\). If \(y\) depends on \(u\) and \(u\) depends on \(x\), a small change \(\Delta x\) causes \(\Delta u \approx \dfrac{du}{dx}\Delta x\), which causes \(\Delta y \approx \dfrac{dy}{du}\Delta u\). So

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\,\frac{du}{dx} . \]

Rates multiply along the chain: if \(u\) changes 3 times as fast as \(x\), and \(y\) changes 2 times as fast as \(u\), then \(y\) changes 6 times as fast as \(x\). Leibniz’s notation makes it look like cancelling the \(du\), which is a good way to remember it.

Example: how fast \(\gamma\) grows with speed. With \(u = 1 - \beta^2\), \(\gamma = u^{-1/2}\):

\[ \frac{d\gamma}{d\beta} = -\tfrac12 u^{-3/2} \times (-2\beta) = \beta\,(1 - \beta^2)^{-3/2} = \beta\gamma^3 . \]

At \(\beta = 0.9\), \(\gamma = 2.294\) and \(d\gamma/d\beta = 10.9\). At \(\beta = 0.99\), \(\gamma = 7.089\) and \(d\gamma/d\beta = 353\). Near the speed of light, a tiny gain in speed needs a huge gain in energy (energy is \(\gamma m c^2\), chapter 1). This is the first sign of why accelerators stop measuring progress in speed and measure it in energy.

3. Sine and cosine, in radians

Radians. An angle in radians is the length of arc it cuts from a circle of radius 1. A full turn is \(2\pi\) radians, so \(180°\) is \(\pi\) radians and 1 radian is \(57.30°\). Calculus uses radians because then, for a small angle \(\theta\), the arc \(\theta\) and the half-chord \(\sin\theta\) are almost the same length:

\[ \lim_{\theta \to 0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = 1 . \]

Numerically, \(\sin 0.1 = 0.09983\), within 0.17 per cent of 0.1. In degrees the ratio would tend to \(\pi/180\) instead, and every formula below would carry that factor.

The second limit needed is for the cosine. Since \(1 - \cos\theta = \dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{1 + \cos\theta}\) (multiply top and bottom by \(1 + \cos\theta\) and use \(\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1\)),

\[ \frac{1 - \cos\theta}{\theta} = \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\cdot\frac{\sin\theta}{1 + \cos\theta} \ \longrightarrow\ 1 \times \frac{0}{2} = 0 . \]

The derivatives. With the addition formula,

\[ \frac{\sin(x + h) - \sin x}{h} = \sin x\,\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} + \cos x\,\frac{\sin h}{h} \ \longrightarrow\ \cos x . \]

The same steps with the cosine’s addition formula give \((\cos x)' = -\sin x\). So:

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\cos x = -\sin x . \]

Differentiate the sine twice and it comes back as minus itself: \((\sin x)'' = -\sin x\). That one fact is behind every oscillation in the course (section 10).

With a rate inside. An RF voltage (a voltage that oscillates at radio frequency, chapter 9 onwards) is written \(V(t) = V_0\sin(\omega t)\), with \(\omega\) the angular frequency in radians per second, \(\omega = 2\pi f\) for a frequency \(f\) in hertz. By the chain rule, \(\dfrac{dV}{dt} = V_0\,\omega\cos(\omega t)\). The voltage changes fastest as it crosses zero, at the rate \(V_0\omega\). For \(V_0 = 100\) kV at \(f = 200\) MHz, that is \(10^5 \times 2\pi \times 2 \times 10^8 = 1.26 \times 10^{14}\) volts per second.

4. The exponential and the logarithm

A function that is its own rate. Many quantities change at a rate proportional to themselves: the number of radioactive atoms that decay each second is proportional to the number present, and the gas a pump removes each second is proportional to the gas there is. The simplest such function is the one whose rate equals itself:

\[ \frac{d}{dx}f(x) = f(x), \qquad f(0) = 1 . \]

This function is called the exponential, \(e^x\) or \(\exp(x)\). Why is it a power of a number? Any power \(a^x\) has the derivative

\[ \frac{a^{x+h} - a^x}{h} = a^x\,\frac{a^h - 1}{h}, \]

which is \(a^x\) times a constant that depends only on \(a\). For \(a = 2\) the constant is \(0.693\); for \(a = 3\) it is \(1.099\). In between there is one number for which the constant is exactly 1. That number is \(e = 2.71828\ldots\), and it can be computed as the limit of \((1 + 1/n)^n\) as \(n\) grows: \((1.01)^{100} = 2.7048\), \((1.0001)^{10000} = 2.7181\).

The natural logarithm. The inverse of \(e^x\) is the natural logarithm, \(\ln x\): \(\ln(e^x) = x\) and \(e^{\ln x} = x\). It obeys the usual rules of logarithms. Its derivative follows from the chain rule applied to \(e^{\ln x} = x\): differentiating both sides, \(e^{\ln x}\,(\ln x)' = 1\), so

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln x = \frac{1}{x} . \]

Two consequences used throughout the course:

  • Any power can be written with base \(e\): \(a^x = e^{x\ln a}\), so \((a^x)' = a^x\ln a\). The constant 0.693 above was \(\ln 2\).
  • For any real power \(n\), \(x^n = e^{n\ln x}\), so \((x^n)' = x^n \cdot n/x = nx^{n-1}\). This proves the power rule for every \(n\), not only whole numbers.

Exponential decay. The function \(e^{-x/\tau}\) falls by a factor \(e\) for every step \(\tau\) in \(x\), and its slope at any point is \(-1/\tau\) times its value. After one \(\tau\) it is at \(1/e = 36.8\) per cent; after two, 13.5 per cent; after \(\tau\ln 2 = 0.693\tau\), at one half. Section 10 shows where it comes from in physics.

The logarithmic derivative. Since \((\ln f)' = f'/f\), the derivative of a logarithm measures relative change: the fractional change of \(f\) per unit change of \(x\). Chapter 2 uses this to find how sensitive a hot cathode’s current is to its temperature.

5. Using derivatives: small changes, errors, maxima

Small changes. Close to a point, a curve is nearly its tangent. So a small change \(\Delta x\) causes a change

\[ \Delta f \approx f'(x)\,\Delta x . \]

For a power \(f = Cx^n\), dividing by \(f\) gives a rule worth remembering:

\[ \frac{\Delta f}{f} \approx n\,\frac{\Delta x}{x} . \]

A quantity that goes as the square doubles any small relative error; one that goes as the square root halves it. The Child–Langmuir law of chapter 2 says the current through a gap goes as \(V^{3/2}/d^2\), so a 1 per cent error in the gap \(d\) becomes a 2 per cent error in the current, and a 1 per cent error in the voltage a 1.5 per cent error.

When “small” stops being small. A hot tungsten cathode emits a current density \(J = A T^2 e^{-W/(k_B T)}\) (chapter 2), where \(T\) is the temperature, \(W = 4.53\) eV the work function and \(k_B = 8.617 \times 10^{-5}\) eV/K Boltzmann’s constant. Its logarithmic derivative is

\[ \frac{d\ln J}{d\ln T} = 2 + \frac{W}{k_B T} . \]

At 2500 K this is \(2 + 21.03 = 23.03\). A 1 per cent rise in temperature should raise the current by about 23 per cent. The exact calculation gives \(1.01^2 \times e^{21.03 \times (1 - 1/1.01)} = 1.256\), a 26 per cent rise. The small-change rule is only the first term of an approximation (section 8 gives the rest); it is good when \(f'\Delta x\) is small compared with \(f\), and here 23 per cent is not small.

Maxima and minima. At the top of a hill or the bottom of a valley the tangent is level, so \(f'(x) = 0\). The second derivative tells which: \(f'' < 0\) for a maximum, \(f'' > 0\) for a minimum.

Example: matching a load. A source of voltage \(V\) with internal resistance \(r\) drives a load resistance \(R\). The current is \(I = V/(R + r)\) and the power into the load is

\[ P(R) = I^2R = \frac{V^2 R}{(R + r)^2} . \]

By the quotient rule, \(P'(R) = V^2\,\dfrac{(R + r)^2 - 2R(R + r)}{(R + r)^4} = V^2\,\dfrac{r - R}{(R + r)^3}\), which is zero at \(R = r\). The power is largest when the load matches the source, and is then \(V^2/(4r)\): 50 W from a 100 V source with \(r = 50\ \Omega\). Chapter 13 meets the same idea when RF power has to pass from a klystron into a cavity without being reflected.

6. Accumulation: the integral

The picture. If a car’s speed is steady at \(v\), the distance after time \(t\) is \(vt\): the area of a rectangle of height \(v\) and width \(t\) on a graph of speed against time. If the speed changes, cut the time into many short intervals \(\Delta t\). In each, the speed is nearly steady, and the distance is nearly \(v\,\Delta t\), the area of a thin strip. Add the strips:

\[ \text{distance} \approx \sum_i v(t_i)\,\Delta t . \]

As the strips are made thinner, the sum settles on the area under the curve of \(v(t)\). That limit is the integral:

\[ \int_{t_1}^{t_2} v(t)\,dt = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0}\sum_i v(t_i)\,\Delta t . \]

The \(\int\) is a stretched S for sum, \(dt\) is the width of a strip that has become infinitely thin, and \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) are the limits of the integral, where the adding starts and stops. The units are those of \(v\) times those of \(t\): m/s times s gives metres. Where \(v\) is negative the strips count as negative area: going backwards subtracts distance.

Integrals in physics. The same pattern appears every time a rate is added up over a range:

  • Distance is the integral of speed over time; speed is the integral of acceleration over time.
  • Charge is the integral of current over time: \(Q = \int I\,dt\).
  • Work is the integral of force over distance: \(W = \int F\,dx\). When a charge \(q\) crosses a region with electric field \(\mathcal{E}(x)\), the force is \(q\mathcal{E}\) and the energy it gains is \(q\int\mathcal{E}\,dx = qV\), the charge times the voltage. That is why accelerators are rated in volts.
  • Mass is the integral of density over volume; the number of particles in a beam is the integral of the beam’s density.

7. The fundamental theorem, and working integrals out

Why integrating undoes differentiating. Let \(F(t)\) be the distance travelled by time \(t\). Over a small extra interval \(h\) the distance grows by about \(v(t)\,h\), so \(F'(t) = v(t)\): the rate at which the area under a curve grows is the height of the curve. Then, for any function \(F\) whose derivative is \(f\),

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a) . \]

This is the fundamental theorem of calculus. It turns the hard problem (adding up infinitely many strips) into an easier one (finding a function whose derivative is \(f\)). Such a function \(F\) is an antiderivative of \(f\), also written \(\int f\,dx\) without limits. It is fixed only up to an added constant, since a constant has no slope; the constant cancels in \(F(b) - F(a)\). The bracket notation \(\big[F(x)\big]_a^b\) means \(F(b) - F(a)\).

A table to start from. Each line is a derivative from sections 2 to 4, read backwards:

\(f(x)\) \(\int f\,dx\)
\(x^n\) (any \(n \neq -1\)) \(\dfrac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}\)
\(1/x\) \(\ln\lvert x\rvert\)
\(e^{kx}\) \(\dfrac{e^{kx}}{k}\)
\(\sin kx\) \(-\dfrac{\cos kx}{k}\)
\(\cos kx\) \(\dfrac{\sin kx}{k}\)

Example: the electron again. With \(v(t) = at\), the distance after time \(t\) is \(\int_0^t at'\,dt' = \big[\tfrac12 at'^2\big]_0^t = \tfrac12 at^2\), the high-school formula, now derived. (The variable inside the integral is renamed \(t'\) because \(t\) is being used as the upper limit.) The electron crosses \(d = 1\) cm when \(\tfrac12 at^2 = d\):

\[ t = \sqrt{\frac{2d}{a}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.02}{3.518 \times 10^{16}}} = 7.54 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{s}, \]

arriving at \(v = at = 2.65 \times 10^7\) m/s, \(0.088c\). Energy gives the same: \(\tfrac12 m_e v^2 = eV\), so \(v = \sqrt{2eV/m_e} = 2.65 \times 10^7\) m/s for \(V = 2000\) V, as chapter 2 finds.

Example: an integral to infinity. How close does an alpha particle of kinetic energy \(K = 5\) MeV get to a gold nucleus, head on? The force between charges \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) at distance \(r\) is \(q_1q_2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)\). The work needed to bring the alpha from very far away to distance \(d\) against it is

\[ W = \int_d^{\infty}\frac{q_1 q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\,dr = \frac{q_1q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[-\frac{1}{r}\right]_d^{\infty} = \frac{q_1q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,d} . \]

An upper limit of infinity means: take the limit as the upper end grows without bound. Here \(-1/r \to 0\), so the integral is finite even though the range is not. Setting \(W = K\), with \(q_1 = 2e\), \(q_2 = 79e\) and the handy combination \(e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) = 1.440\) MeV fm (1 fm \(= 10^{-15}\) m),

\[ d = \frac{2 \times 79 \times 1.440\ \text{MeV fm}}{5\ \text{MeV}} = 45.5\ \text{fm} . \]

That is the distance of closest approach of chapter 1, and the reason Rutherford’s alphas could not reach a heavy nucleus.

Example: the charge in a pulse. A current that dies away as \(I(t) = I_0 e^{-t/\tau}\) carries the charge

\[ Q = \int_0^{\infty} I_0 e^{-t/\tau}\,dt = I_0\big[-\tau e^{-t/\tau}\big]_0^{\infty} = I_0\tau . \]

The total is the same as a steady current \(I_0\) flowing for one time constant.

Substitution. The chain rule read backwards. If the integrand contains a function \(u(x)\) and its derivative \(u'(x)\), change variable to \(u\), with \(du = u'(x)\,dx\). For example \(\int 2x\,e^{x^2}\,dx\): with \(u = x^2\), \(du = 2x\,dx\), and the integral is \(\int e^u\,du = e^{x^2}\) plus a constant. (Check: differentiate \(e^{x^2}\) by the chain rule.)

Integration by parts. The product rule read backwards. Integrating \((fg)' = f'g + fg'\) gives

\[ \int_a^b f\,g'\,dx = \big[fg\big]_a^b - \int_a^b f'\,g\,dx . \]

It helps when \(f\) becomes simpler when differentiated. Example: \(\int_0^{\infty} x e^{-x}\,dx\). Take \(f = x\), \(g' = e^{-x}\), so \(g = -e^{-x}\): the result is \(\big[-xe^{-x}\big]_0^{\infty} + \int_0^{\infty}e^{-x}\,dx = 0 + 1 = 1\).

Not every integral has a formula. \(\int e^{-x^2}\,dx\), the area under the bell curve that describes the spread of particles in a beam, cannot be written with the functions above. The integral from \(-\infty\) to \(\infty\) is known exactly, \(\sqrt{\pi}\), by a trick taught in chapter 2; integrals over a part of the range are given new names (the error function) and tabulated, or computed numerically (section 11).

8. Approximating a function: Taylor series

The idea. The small-change rule of section 5 replaces a curve by its tangent, a straight line. A better approximation also matches the curvature, and better still the rate at which the curvature changes. Choose a polynomial \(f(a + h) \approx c_0 + c_1h + c_2h^2 + c_3h^3 + \dots\) whose value and first few derivatives at \(h = 0\) equal those of \(f\) at \(a\). Differentiating the polynomial term by term and setting \(h = 0\) gives \(c_0 = f(a)\), \(c_1 = f'(a)\), \(2c_2 = f''(a)\), \(6c_3 = f'''(a)\), and in general \(n!\,c_n = f^{(n)}(a)\), the \(n\)th derivative (\(n! = 1 \times 2 \times \dots \times n\)). So

\[ f(a + h) = f(a) + f'(a)\,h + \frac{f''(a)}{2!}h^2 + \frac{f'''(a)}{3!}h^3 + \dots \]

This is the Taylor series (Brook Taylor, 1715). For small \(h\) each term is much smaller than the one before, and a few terms are enough.

The series used most.

  • \(e^x = 1 + x + \dfrac{x^2}{2!} + \dfrac{x^3}{3!} + \dots\) (every derivative of \(e^x\) is 1 at \(x = 0\)). This series converges for every \(x\).
  • \(\sin x = x - \dfrac{x^3}{3!} + \dots\) and \(\cos x = 1 - \dfrac{x^2}{2!} + \dots\) For \(x = 0.1\): \(\cos 0.1 = 0.995004\) against \(1 - 0.005 = 0.995\).
  • \((1 + x)^n = 1 + nx + \dfrac{n(n-1)}{2}x^2 + \dots\) for any real \(n\), for \(\lvert x\rvert < 1\). For instance \(\sqrt{1.1} \approx 1 + 0.05 - 0.00125 = 1.04875\), against the true 1.048809.
  • \(\ln(1 + x) = x - \dfrac{x^2}{2} + \dfrac{x^3}{3} - \dots\) for \(\lvert x\rvert < 1\).

Example: kinetic energy at low speed. A particle’s kinetic energy is \((\gamma - 1)mc^2\) (chapter 1), with \(\gamma = (1 - \beta^2)^{-1/2}\). The binomial series with \(x = -\beta^2\) and \(n = -\tfrac12\) gives

\[ \gamma = 1 + \frac{\beta^2}{2} + \frac{3\beta^4}{8} + \dots, \qquad (\gamma - 1)mc^2 = \tfrac12 mv^2\left(1 + \frac{3}{4}\beta^2 + \dots\right) . \]

At low speed, relativity’s kinetic energy is Newton’s \(\tfrac12 mv^2\), and the first correction is \(\tfrac34\beta^2\) of it. For the 2 kV electron, \(\beta = 0.088\) and the correction is 0.59 per cent, which is why chapter 2 can use Newton’s formula there. At \(\beta = 0.5\), two terms give \(\gamma \approx 1.125\) and three give 1.1484, against the true 1.1547: the series still works, but needs more terms. At \(\beta = 0.99\) it is useless, and the exact formula must be used.

How good is the approximation? The error of stopping after the \(h^n\) term is roughly the size of the first term left out. That is the rule the course uses whenever it writes “to first order” (keeping terms in \(h\)) or “to second order” (keeping \(h^2\)): it means the next term was checked to be small.

9. Several variables: partial derivatives

The picture. Most quantities depend on more than one thing. The radius of a charged particle’s path in a magnetic field is \(\rho = p/(qB)\): it depends on the momentum \(p\), the charge \(q\) and the field \(B\). How does it change when only \(B\) changes?

The definition. The partial derivative \(\partial f/\partial x\) is the derivative with respect to \(x\) with every other variable held fixed. The curly \(\partial\) is a reminder that the others are being held. For \(\rho = p/(qB)\),

\[ \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial p} = \frac{1}{qB}, \qquad \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial B} = -\frac{p}{qB^2} . \]

Changing several at once. If every variable changes by a small amount, the changes add, each through its own slope:

\[ \Delta f \approx \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\Delta x + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\Delta y + \dots \]

For \(\rho = p/(qB)\), dividing by \(\rho\):

\[ \frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho} \approx \frac{\Delta p}{p} - \frac{\Delta B}{B} . \]

Example. A proton of momentum 1 GeV/\(c\) in a field of 1 T moves on a circle of radius \(\rho = 3.336\) m (chapter 1 shows \(B\rho\,[\text{T m}] = 3.3356\,p\,[\text{GeV}/c]\)). A proton with 0.1 per cent more momentum moves on a radius 3.3 mm larger. Rings are built around this fact: chapter 16 calls it dispersion.

Second partial derivatives. One can differentiate twice with respect to the same variable, \(\partial^2 f/\partial x^2\), or once with respect to each, \(\partial^2 f/\partial x\,\partial y\). For smooth functions the order does not matter: \(\dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y} = \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x}\). F2 combines the partial derivatives with respect to \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) into the gradient, the divergence and the curl, the language of electric and magnetic fields.

10. Equations of change: first differential equations

What they are. Most laws of physics do not say what a quantity is; they say how it changes. Newton’s second law says that a body’s acceleration is the force divided by the mass. A differential equation is an equation relating a function to its derivatives. Solving it means finding the function. Most of the course’s physics comes as differential equations, so this section shows the three kinds that recur most.

Kind 1: the rate is given. \(\dfrac{dx}{dt} = g(t)\). The solution is an integral: \(x(t) = x(0) + \int_0^t g(t')\,dt'\). The electron of section 7 was this kind, twice: \(\dot v = a\), then \(\dot x = v\).

Kind 2: the rate is proportional to the amount. \(\dfrac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N\), with \(\lambda > 0\) a constant. Section 4 already knows a function whose derivative is a constant times itself: try \(N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}\). Then \(\dot N = -\lambda N_0 e^{-\lambda t} = -\lambda N\), and at \(t = 0\), \(N = N_0\). The solution is

\[ N(t) = N_0\,e^{-\lambda t} = N_0\,e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \tau = \frac{1}{\lambda} . \]

A general method: separating the variables. Treat \(dN\) and \(dt\) as small amounts, put everything with \(N\) on one side and with \(t\) on the other, and integrate both: \(\dfrac{dN}{N} = -\lambda\,dt\), so \(\ln N = -\lambda t + \text{const}\), which gives the same solution. This works whenever the rate is a function of \(N\) times a function of \(t\).

Three places the course meets it:

  • Radioactive decay. Each atom has the same chance per second, \(\lambda\), of decaying. Polonium-210, the alpha source of the early experiments, has a half-life of 138.4 days, so \(\lambda = \ln 2/138.4 = 0.00501\) per day, the mean life is \(\tau = 199.6\) days, and after a year 16.0 per cent of it is left.
  • Pumping a chamber. A pump with speed \(S\) (volume of gas removed per second) on a chamber of volume \(V\) removes the fraction \(S/V\) of the gas each second, so \(\dot p = -(S/V)\,p\) and the pressure falls as \(e^{-t/\tau}\) with \(\tau = V/S\). For \(V = 0.1\) m\(^3\) and \(S = 0.05\) m\(^3\)/s, \(\tau = 2\) s, and the pressure falls from \(10^5\) Pa to 1 Pa in \(2\ln(10^5) = 23\) s. Real chambers stop falling long before this predicts, because gas keeps coming off the walls; chapter 3 adds that term.
  • A capacitor discharging. A capacitor \(C\) discharging through a resistor \(R\) loses charge at the rate of the current, \(V/R\), so \(C\dot V = -V/R\) and \(\tau = RC\). A 1 nF capacitor through 1 MΩ has \(\tau = 1\) ms.

Kind 3: oscillation. A body pulled back towards a centre with a force proportional to its distance from it, \(F = -kx\) (a spring, or, in chapter 10 onwards, a particle in a focusing field), obeys

\[ m\ddot x = -kx, \qquad \text{or} \qquad \ddot x = -\omega^2 x, \quad \omega = \sqrt{k/m} . \]

Section 3 found functions that come back as minus themselves after two derivatives. Try \(x = A\cos\omega t + B\sin\omega t\): then \(\ddot x = -\omega^2(A\cos\omega t + B\sin\omega t) = -\omega^2 x\). It works for any constants \(A\) and \(B\), which are fixed by where the body starts (\(x(0) = A\)) and how fast (\(\dot x(0) = B\omega\)). The motion repeats every \(2\pi/\omega\) seconds, whatever the size of the swing. The particles in every ring of the course oscillate about their ideal path this way; chapter 2’s pre-requisite on ordinary differential equations and chapter 16 go on from here.

Why two constants? An equation with a second derivative needs two facts to pin down a solution (a position and a speed); one with a first derivative needs one (a starting amount). Physically: Newton’s law says what the acceleration is, but where the body is and how fast it moves now are up to how it was set going.

11. When there is no formula: numbers on a computer

Many integrals and differential equations in accelerator physics have no solution in formulas. They are done numerically, and the methods are the definitions of this chapter with a finite step.

Integrals. The trapezoid rule joins neighbouring points of the curve by straight lines and adds the areas of the trapezoids: with \(n\) strips of width \(h\),

\[ \int_a^b f\,dx \approx h\left[\tfrac12 f(x_0) + f(x_1) + \dots + f(x_{n-1}) + \tfrac12 f(x_n)\right] . \]

Simpson’s rule fits parabolas through the points instead, weighting them \(1, 4, 2, 4, \dots, 4, 1\) times \(h/3\). For \(\int_0^1 e^{-x^2}dx = 0.746824\), four strips give 0.742984 by the trapezoid rule (0.5 per cent low) and 0.746855 by Simpson’s (0.004 per cent high). Halving the strip width cuts the trapezoid’s error by about 4 and Simpson’s by about 16.

Differential equations. Euler’s method steps the equation forward: from \(\dot x = g(x, t)\), take \(x(t + h) \approx x(t) + g(x, t)\,h\), the small-change rule, and repeat. It is easy and inaccurate; better methods (Runge–Kutta, and for orbits the symplectic methods of chapter 16) take a few evaluations per step. Every simulation code that tracks particles round a ring is, at heart, a careful version of this step.

Exercises

  1. The electron of section 1 accelerates at \(3.518 \times 10^{16}\) m/s\(^2\) from rest. Starting from \(x(t) = \tfrac12 at^2\) and the definition of the derivative, find its speed at \(t = 0.5\) ns, and its acceleration.
Answer

The average speed over \([t, t+h]\) is \(at + \tfrac12 ah\), which tends to \(v = at\) as \(h \to 0\): \(v = 3.518 \times 10^{16} \times 0.5 \times 10^{-9} = 1.76 \times 10^7\) m/s. Differentiating again, \(\ddot x = a = 3.518 \times 10^{16}\) m/s\(^2\), the same at every instant.

  1. Differentiate: (a) \(x^3e^{-2x}\); (b) \(V_0\sin(\omega t + \phi)\) with respect to \(t\); (c) \(\ln(1 + x^2)\); (d) \(1/\sqrt{1 - x^2}\).
Answer
  1. Product rule and chain rule: \(3x^2e^{-2x} - 2x^3e^{-2x} = x^2(3 - 2x)e^{-2x}\). (b) \(V_0\,\omega\cos(\omega t + \phi)\). (c) \(\dfrac{2x}{1 + x^2}\). (d) Write it as \((1 - x^2)^{-1/2}\): \(-\tfrac12(1 - x^2)^{-3/2}(-2x) = x(1 - x^2)^{-3/2}\). With \(x = \beta\) this is section 2’s \(\beta\gamma^3\).
  1. At \(\beta = 0.99\), \(\gamma = 7.089\). Use \(d\gamma/d\beta = \beta\gamma^3\) to estimate \(\gamma\) at \(\beta = 0.991\). Compare with the exact value, 7.470. Why is the estimate low?
Answer

\(d\gamma/d\beta = 0.99 \times 7.089^3 = 352.7\), so \(\Delta\gamma \approx 352.7 \times 0.001 = 0.353\) and \(\gamma \approx 7.442\). The true rise is 0.382. The estimate is low because the curve bends upwards (\(\gamma'' > 0\)): its slope at 0.991 is larger than at 0.99, and the tangent lies below the curve. Near \(\beta = 1\) the curvature is large, and even a step of 0.001 is not small.

  1. A chamber of 0.2 m\(^3\) is pumped by a pump of speed 0.05 m\(^3\)/s. Ignoring gas from the walls, how long does it take to fall from \(10^5\) Pa to 10 Pa?
Answer

\(\tau = V/S = 4\) s. The pressure falls as \(e^{-t/\tau}\), so \(t = \tau\ln(10^5/10) = 4 \times \ln 10^4 = 4 \times 9.21 = 36.8\) s.

  1. Show that the average of \(\sin^2(\omega t)\) over one period \(T = 2\pi/\omega\) is \(\tfrac12\). (Hint: \(\sin^2\theta = \tfrac12(1 - \cos 2\theta)\).) An RF cavity’s voltage is \(V_0\sin\omega t\) across a resistance \(R\); what is the average power?
Answer

\(\dfrac{1}{T}\displaystyle\int_0^T \tfrac12(1 - \cos 2\omega t)\,dt = \dfrac{1}{T}\left[\tfrac12 t - \dfrac{\sin 2\omega t}{4\omega}\right]_0^T = \tfrac12\), since \(\sin(2\omega T) = \sin 4\pi = 0\). The power is \(V^2/R = (V_0^2/R)\sin^2\omega t\), whose average is \(V_0^2/(2R)\). That factor of \(\tfrac12\) appears in every RF power formula in chapter 13.

  1. Rutherford’s alphas from radium C′ had a kinetic energy of 7.69 MeV. How close could one come to a gold nucleus (\(Z = 79\)), head on?
Answer

\(d = 2 \times 79 \times 1.440/7.69 = 29.6\) fm. (A gold nucleus has a radius of about 7 fm, so the alpha stays well outside it, which is why Rutherford’s scattering law held.)

  1. Use the binomial series to two and three terms to estimate \(\sqrt{1.1}\). How big is the error of each, compared with the first term left out?
Answer

\((1 + x)^{1/2} = 1 + \tfrac12 x - \tfrac18 x^2 + \tfrac1{16}x^3 - \dots\) With \(x = 0.1\): two terms give 1.05 and three give 1.04875. The true value is 1.048809. The errors are \(1.2 \times 10^{-3}\) and \(5.9 \times 10^{-5}\); the first terms left out are \(-1.25 \times 10^{-3}\) and \(+6.25 \times 10^{-5}\), close to the errors as the rule says.

  1. For a hot cathode, \(J = AT^2e^{-W/(k_BT)}\). Find \(d\ln J/d\ln T\) at 2000 K for \(W = 4.53\) eV. How steady must the temperature be held to keep the current steady to 1 per cent?
Answer

\(\ln J = \ln A + 2\ln T - W/(k_BT)\). With \(u = \ln T\), \(T = e^u\) and \(-W/(k_BT) = -(W/k_B)e^{-u}\), so \(d\ln J/du = 2 + W/(k_BT) = 2 + 4.53/(8.617 \times 10^{-5} \times 2000) = 2 + 26.3 = 28.3\). To hold \(J\) to 1 per cent, hold \(T\) to \(1/28.3\) per cent: 0.035 per cent, or 0.71 K at 2000 K. (Chapter 2, exercises on errors, does the same.)

  1. A 10 nF capacitor charged to 10 kV discharges through a 100 MΩ resistor (a voltage divider of the kind in chapter 4). What is the time constant, and how long until the voltage is below 1 kV?
Answer

\(\tau = RC = 10^8 \times 10^{-8} = 1\) s. \(V = V_0e^{-t/\tau}\) falls to \(V_0/10\) at \(t = \tau\ln 10 = 2.30\) s.

  1. Estimate \(\int_1^2 dx/x\) with the trapezoid rule and four strips. Compare with the exact value.
Answer

\(h = 0.25\): \(0.25 \times (\tfrac12 \times 1 + 0.8 + 0.6667 + 0.5714 + \tfrac12 \times 0.5) = 0.6970\). The exact value is \(\ln 2 = 0.6931\); the error is 0.56 per cent, high because \(1/x\) bends upwards and the straight chords lie above it.
