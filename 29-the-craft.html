29 How an accelerator is researched, designed, built, commissioned and run

The 1930s to today: Lawrence’s Radiation Laboratory, Cockcroft and Walton at the Cavendish and Kerst’s betatron, then ISABELLE, the SSC, the LHC and the High-Luminosity LHC; past the wall between a machine that works on paper and one that works in a tunnel, on time, for the people who need its beam.

In one page

The wall. The physics of a beam is the smaller part of an accelerator. The rest is parts that must all work at once (the LHC has over 1700 power converters and over 7000 quench detectors), money that must be found and defended, and years of building. Projects have died at this wall: ISABELLE in July 1983 after more than 200 million US dollars, the SSC in October 1993 after about 2 billion US dollars and 22.5 km of tunnel. The LHC worked for nine days in September 2008 before one soldered joint of 220 nΩ ended its first run for fourteen months.

The idea. A machine has a life, and each part of it has its own method: a physics case turned into a parameter list; a conceptual design, then a technical design; prototypes and test stands; approval; civil engineering, survey and installation; hardware commissioning without beam; beam commissioning; operation with machine-development time; upgrades; and decommissioning. Research runs alongside: theory and maps, simulation codes, beam experiments and test facilities, an open literature and the schools.

The equations to remember.

\[ N_{ev} = \sigma\int\mathcal{L}\,dt, \qquad C \geq \frac{2\pi p}{qBF}, \qquad P = I^2R \]

\[ x_j = \sqrt{\beta_{x,i}\beta_{x,j}}\,\sin(\psi_j - \psi_i)\,\theta_i, \qquad \frac{\Delta f_{RF}}{f_{RF}} = -\eta\,\delta, \qquad \sigma_{\text{var}}^2 = \sum_i\sigma_i^2 + 2\sum_{i<j}\rho_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j \]

The first counts events from integrated luminosity; the second sets a ring’s circumference from momentum \(p\), field \(B\) and the fraction \(F\) of the ring filled with dipoles; the third is the heat in a resistive joint. The fourth is how a kick \(\theta_i\) (rad) at one point moves the beam at a later point on its first pass, which is how a beam is threaded. The fifth turns an RF frequency trim into a momentum offset, which is how chromaticity is measured. The last is the variance of a sum of uncertain costs, which is what contingency is for.

Numbers worth knowing. LHC: 3756 million CHF to CERN’s budget plus 1224 million CHF of labour (components built 1995 to 2007); 26.7 km; first beam 10 September 2008; beam with a lifetime over an hour after three days. Splice: 220 nΩ against a nominal of about 0.3 to 0.35 nΩ (sources differ), 16.7 W at 8.7 kA. HL-LHC: 3000 fb\(^{-1}\) by the early 2040s, 250 fb\(^{-1}\) a year, levelled at \(5\times10^{34}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\), which is 58 days of levelled collisions a year. In Run 2 the LHC was in collision almost half of its scheduled proton time. A flat ring of the LHC’s radius, level at its centre, stands 1.41 m above the level surface at its edge; FCC’s would stand 16.3 m above it. CERN’s annual dose limits: 1 mSv (non-designated areas), 6 mSv (supervised), 20 mSv (controlled).

The wall

The first accelerators were built by the people who used them, in a room, with borrowed parts. In January 1931 Ernest Lawrence specified a cyclotron with 10 cm radius; Kenneth Bainbridge estimated the materials at about USD 700, and the National Research Council gave USD 500 (chapter 10). John Cockcroft and Ernest Walton began at the Cavendish after Gamow’s visit in 1928 (Cockcroft’s Nobel lecture; some accounts give January 1929) and split lithium on 14 April 1932 (chapter 5). Donald Kerst came to Illinois in 1938 and had his betatron working on 15 July 1940 (chapter 11). Each machine was a few years’ work by a handful of people.

By the 1970s a frontier machine took a decade, thousands of people and a national budget line. The difficulties changed. The beam physics was usually understood; what failed was industrial production, money and politics.

ISABELLE. Brookhaven’s 200 + 200 GeV superconducting proton collider was recommended by the US High Energy Physics Advisory Panel in 1974 and construction began in 1978. A prototype magnet worked in 1979. In 1981 production magnets failed at fields below what operation needed. Funding had been pushed through before the magnet development was complete. The Department of Energy cancelled the project in July 1983, after spending more than 200 million US dollars (chapter 17). Its tunnel and halls later housed RHIC.

The SSC. The Superconducting Super Collider was to collide 20 TeV protons in a ring of 87.1 km near Waxahachie, Texas. In 1987 Congress was told it would cost 4.4 billion US dollars; by March 1993 the estimate had grown to 8.4 billion. Construction began in 1991. Seventeen shafts were sunk and about 22.5 km of tunnel bored (one account gives 23.5 km) before the House of Representatives rejected its funding on 19 October 1993; the bill ending it was signed on 30 October. About 2 billion US dollars had been spent, some 400 million of it by Texas. In the same years the 6 TeV UNK proton complex at Protvino in Russia was also terminated (Shiltsev and Zimmermann).

The LHC. The project was approved at the end of 1994, conditional on extra resources, after the first full-scale dipole prototype had been tested (Lebrun). The technical components were built and installed from 1995 to 2007, at a cost to CERN of 3756 million CHF plus 1224 million CHF of labour (Evans, quoted by Shiltsev and Zimmermann). Beam commissioning began on 10 September 2008. On 19 September, during a powering test without beam, a soldered joint in a busbar between two magnets failed, an electric arc punctured the helium enclosure, and 53 magnets had to be removed (Wenninger 2009). Beam returned on 20 November 2009.

What stood in front of these projects is a wall of a different kind from those in earlier chapters. No equation of motion forbids anything here. The wall is the number of things that must be right at once, the size of the sums at risk, and the time over which they are spent. This chapter is about how the field learned to get past it, and how research in accelerators is done.

The physics

The machine physics is in the earlier chapters. This section collects the quantitative tools of the practice: how a parameter list follows from a physics case, how cost scales and how contingency is set, how tolerances are budgeted, what the first tests measure, and how a beam is commissioned and a machine run. Each is a short derivation with a real number.

1. From a physics case to a parameter list

A physics case says what is to be measured and how well. For a collider it fixes the centre-of-mass energy and the number of events. If a process has cross-section \(\sigma\) (cm\(^2\)), the number of events is

\[ N_{ev} = \sigma\int\mathcal{L}\,dt , \]

with \(\mathcal{L}\) the luminosity (cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\); \(1\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\) \(= 10^4\) m\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\)). An integrated luminosity of 1 fb\(^{-1}\) is \(10^{39}\) cm\(^{-2}\). (The barn is the unit of cross-section, \(10^{-24}\) cm\(^2\) (chapter 3); a picobarn, pb, is \(10^{-12}\) of it, \(10^{-36}\) cm\(^2\), and a femtobarn, fb, \(10^{-15}\) of it, \(10^{-39}\) cm\(^2\). An “inverse femtobarn” is therefore \(10^{39}\) cm\(^{-2}\), and a cross-section in fb times an integrated luminosity in fb\(^{-1}\) is a plain number of events.) The integral is there because the luminosity changes during a fill; \(\int\mathcal{L}\,dt\) is the area under the curve of \(\mathcal{L}\) against time.

Worked number. The Higgs boson’s gluon-fusion cross-section at 13 TeV is about 48.6 pb (Anastasiou and others, as used in chapter 23), that is \(4.86\times10^{-35}\) cm\(^2\). The HL-LHC’s goal of 3000 fb\(^{-1}\), \(3\times10^{42}\) cm\(^{-2}\), then means \(4.86\times10^{-35}\times3\times10^{42} = 1.46\times10^{8}\) Higgs bosons per experiment, produced at \(4.86\times10^{-35}\times5\times10^{34} = 2.4\) per second at the levelled \(5\times10^{34}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\) (“levelled”: held constant through the fill on purpose, chapter 23). The goal sets the luminosity, and the luminosity formula of chapter 23 sets the bunch population \(N\), the number of bunches \(n_b\), the emittance \(\varepsilon_n\) and \(\beta^*\).

The energy sets the size. A particle of momentum \(p\) and charge \(q\) bends on radius \(\rho = p/(qB)\) in a field \(B\) (the magnetic force \(qvB\) supplies the centripetal force, F4, section 10, and the relativistic form is in chapter 1). The dipoles together must turn the beam through one full circle, \(2\pi\) radians, and each metre of dipole turns it by \(1/\rho\) radians, so the total length of dipole is \(2\pi\rho\), whatever the shape of the ring. If dipoles fill a fraction \(F\) of the circumference \(C\), then \(2\pi\rho = FC\) and

\[ C = \frac{2\pi p}{qBF} . \]

Worked number. The product \(B\rho = p/q\) (T m) is the rigidity, the field-times-radius a beam needs; for a singly charged particle with \(p\) in GeV/\(c\) it is \(3.3356\,p\) T m (chapter 1). At 7 TeV/\(c\), \(B\rho = 3.3356\times7000 = 23\,349\) T m. At 8.33 T, \(\rho = 23\,349/8.33 = 2803\) m, so \(2\pi\times2803\) m \(= 17.6\) km of the LHC’s 26.66 km is bending: \(F = 0.66\). The rest is focusing, correctors, interconnects and eight straight sections for experiments, RF, collimation and the beam dump.

For a machine that delivers beam to a target, the parameter list is energy, current and duty factor. A pulsed linac giving a peak current \(\hat{I}\) in pulses of length \(\tau_p\) at a repetition rate \(f_{rep}\) has duty factor \(D_f = \tau_p f_{rep}\), the fraction of the time the beam is on, and average current \(\hat{I}D_f\). If each particle leaves with kinetic energy \(W\) (J), the beam carries \(\hat{I}D_f/q\) particles a second, so its average power is \(P_b = (W/q)\,\hat{I}D_f\) (power is energy per second, F3, section 4; \(W/q\) is the energy in volts, 2 GV for a 2 GeV proton). ESS: 62.5 mA, 2.86 ms, 14 Hz give \(D_f = 0.040\), 2.5 mA and 5.0 MW at 2 GeV (chapter 14). For a light source the list is energy, current and emittance (chapter 20).

Pre-requisite (continued): Errors and their propagation

Pre-requisite. This section adds four things to the error rules of earlier chapters: what happens to a sum when its errors are correlated, how to read a percentile from a Gaussian, how to handle skewed uncertainties by Monte Carlo, and the spread of a quantity known only to lie somewhere in an interval. Section 2 uses the first three to set a contingency on a cost estimate; section 6 uses the last for the precision of a tune read from one FFT bin. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 5, §110 (the Gaussian distribution) and §111 (several variables, and the mean square fluctuation of a function of them).

Reminder: chapter 1 showed that variances add when errors are independent, because the cross terms \(\langle\delta x\,\delta y\rangle\) average to zero; chapter 18 applied this to many random kicks. Here the cross terms are kept. The angle brackets \(\langle\ \rangle\) mean an average over many imagined repetitions (here, many possible ways the project could turn out), and a variance is the average of the squared deviation from the mean (F6, section 3).

Correlated errors. A cost estimate is a sum \(K = \sum_i K_i\) of items (CHF). Each item’s true cost differs from its estimate by an error \(\delta K_i\), with \(\langle\delta K_i\rangle = 0\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_i\). The error of the total is \(\delta K = \sum_i\delta K_i\). Square it and average. For two items, \((\delta K_1 + \delta K_2)^2 = \delta K_1^2 + \delta K_2^2 + 2\,\delta K_1\delta K_2\); for any number, the square of a sum is the sum of every product of one term with another, so

\[ \sigma_K^2 = \Big\langle\Big(\sum_i\delta K_i\Big)^2\Big\rangle = \sum_i\sum_j\langle\delta K_i\,\delta K_j\rangle . \]

The terms with \(i = j\) are the variances \(\sigma_i^2\). The terms with \(i \neq j\) are the covariances \(\langle\delta K_i\,\delta K_j\rangle\). Divide each by \(\sigma_i\sigma_j\) to get a pure number, the correlation coefficient

\[ \rho_{ij} = \frac{\langle\delta K_i\,\delta K_j\rangle}{\sigma_i\sigma_j} , \]

which lies between \(-1\) and \(+1\). (Why: the average of a square cannot be negative, and \(\langle(\delta K_i/\sigma_i \pm \delta K_j/\sigma_j)^2\rangle = 1 + 1 \pm 2\rho_{ij}\), which is \(\geq 0\) for both signs only if \(|\rho_{ij}| \leq 1\).) Each pair \(i \neq j\) appears twice in the double sum, as \((i, j)\) and \((j, i)\), so

\[ \sigma_K^2 = \sum_i\sigma_i^2 + 2\sum_{i<j}\rho_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j . \]

This is the special case, for a plain sum, of Landau’s rule for any function \(f\) of several fluctuating quantities: \(\langle\delta f^2\rangle = \sum_{i,k}(\partial f/\partial x_i)(\partial f/\partial x_k)\langle\delta x_i\,\delta x_k\rangle\) (vol. 5, §111). Three cases matter:

  • \(\rho_{ij} = 0\): the items are independent, the cross terms vanish, and the errors add in quadrature (chapter 1).
  • \(\rho_{ij} = 1\): the items move in lock-step, \(\delta K_j = (\sigma_j/\sigma_i)\,\delta K_i\). Then \(\sigma_K^2 = (\sum_i\sigma_i)^2\) and the errors add linearly. Nothing cancels.
  • \(\rho_{ij} = -1\): one goes up when the other goes down, and they cancel.

Where does a correlation come from? From a shared cause. Write each error as a common part and a private part, \(\delta K_i = \sigma_i(\sqrt{\rho}\,Z_0 + \sqrt{1 - \rho}\,Z_i)\), where \(Z_0\) and the \(Z_i\) are independent random numbers of mean 0 and variance 1. Then \(\langle\delta K_i^2\rangle = \sigma_i^2(\rho + 1 - \rho) = \sigma_i^2\), and for \(i \neq j\) only the common part survives the average: \(\langle\delta K_i\,\delta K_j\rangle = \sigma_i\sigma_j\rho\). The common cause \(Z_0\) might be inflation, the price of a metal, a labour rate or a slip in the schedule that keeps every team paid for longer. This model is also how a Monte Carlo draws correlated items (below).

Many identical items. Take \(N\) items of the same expected cost \(\bar{K}\) and the same \(\sigma\), with the same \(\rho\) between every pair. There are \(N\) variance terms and \(N(N - 1)\) ordered cross terms, so \(\sigma_K^2 = N\sigma^2 + N(N - 1)\rho\sigma^2\). Relative to the total \(N\bar{K}\),

\[ \frac{\sigma_K}{N\bar{K}} = \frac{\sigma}{\bar{K}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{N} + \Big(1 - \frac{1}{N}\Big)\rho} . \]

For independent items the relative error falls as \(1/\sqrt{N}\). Any correlation puts a floor under it: for large \(N\) it tends to \((\sigma/\bar{K})\sqrt{\rho}\), however many items there are.

At work. The LHC has 1232 main dipoles. Suppose each has a cost uncertainty of 10 per cent (my assumption, for the arithmetic). If the errors are independent, the uncertainty of the dipole total is \(0.1/\sqrt{1232} = 0.28\) per cent. With \(\rho = 0.01\) it is 1.0 per cent; with \(\rho = 0.1\), 3.2 per cent; with \(\rho = 1\), the full 10 per cent. A shared cause as weak as \(\rho = 0.1\) makes the total eleven times less certain than independence promises. This is why a cost that every magnet shares, such as the price of its superconducting cable, is estimated as one item and not left to average out.

Independent items (blue) fall as \(1/\sqrt{N}\), a straight line of slope \(-1/2\) on these logarithmic axes. Any correlation bends the line over into a floor at \(10\sqrt{\rho}\) per cent, reached once \(1/N\) is small against \(\rho\). The dots at \(N = 1232\) are the LHC dipoles of the example: 0.28, 1.0, 3.2 and 10 per cent.

Percentiles of a Gaussian. The \(P\)th percentile of an uncertain quantity is the value it stays below with probability \(P\) per cent; the 50th is the median, the middle value. If the total is Gaussian with mean \(\bar{K}\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_K\), the probability that it comes out below \(\bar{K} + z\sigma_K\) is

\[ \Phi(z) = \frac{1}{2}\left[1 + \text{erf}\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right] , \]

with the error function of chapter 2. \(\Phi(z)\) is the area under the Gaussian bell (F6, section 5) to the left of \(z\) standard deviations above the mean. A sum of many independent items tends to a Gaussian whatever the items’ own shapes: this is the central limit theorem (F6, section 7), met in chapter 20, and chapter 6 showed why any sharply peaked distribution looks Gaussian near its peak. Inverting \(\Phi\) (in Python, scipy.stats.norm.ppf):

A contingency set at the \(P\) percentile is \(z\sigma_K\) added to the expected cost. The choice of \(P\) is a policy; the \(z\) follows.

At work. Take two items (illustrative): a tunnel at 6000 million CHF with \(\sigma = 1500\) and magnets at 4000 with \(\sigma = 1000\). Then \(\sigma_K = \sqrt{1500^2 + 1000^2 + 2\rho\times1500\times1000}\): 1803 for \(\rho = 0\), 2179 for \(\rho = 0.5\) and 2500 for \(\rho = 1\). The 80 per cent contingency is \(0.842\sigma_K\): 1517, 1834 and 2104 million CHF, or 15, 18 and 21 per cent of the total.

Skewed items and Monte Carlo. A cost has a floor but almost no ceiling: an item is rarely half its estimate and sometimes double. A log-normal distribution has this shape. Write \(K = K_m e^{sZ}\) with \(Z\) a Gaussian random number of mean 0 and variance 1; \(K_m\) is the median and \(s\) sets the spread. Its mean and variance are

\[ \bar{K} = K_m e^{s^2/2}, \qquad \sigma^2 = \bar{K}^2\left(e^{s^2} - 1\right), \]

To see where they come from, average \(e^{sZ}\) over the Gaussian density \(e^{-z^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}\) of \(Z\) (F6, section 5), and complete the square in the exponent, \(sz - z^2/2 = -(z - s)^2/2 + s^2/2\):

\[ \langle e^{sZ}\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{sz}\,\frac{e^{-z^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,dz = e^{s^2/2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-(z - s)^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,dz = e^{s^2/2} , \]

since the last integral is a whole Gaussian, shifted by \(s\), whose area is 1. So \(\bar{K} = K_me^{s^2/2}\). The same with \(2s\) in place of \(s\) gives \(\langle K^2\rangle = K_m^2\langle e^{2sZ}\rangle = K_m^2e^{2s^2}\), and the variance is \(\sigma^2 = \langle K^2\rangle - \bar{K}^2 = K_m^2e^{s^2}(e^{s^2} - 1) = \bar{K}^2(e^{s^2} - 1)\). So, given the mean and standard deviation of an item, \(s^2 = \ln[1 + (\sigma/\bar{K})^2]\) and \(K_m = \bar{K}e^{-s^2/2}\). \(K_m\) is the median: half the values of \(Z\) lie below 0, and since \(e^{sZ}\) grows whenever \(Z\) grows, half the values of \(K\) lie below \(K_me^0 = K_m\). For the same reason its percentiles are those of \(Z\) carried through the exponential: the 80th is \(K_me^{0.842s}\).

At work. An item with mean 100 and \(\sigma = 25\) has \(s = 0.246\) and median 97.0: there is a 55 per cent chance it costs less than its mean and a long tail above. Its 80th percentile is 119.4 and its 95th 145.5, against 121.0 and 141.1 for a Gaussian of the same mean and spread. The skew shows in the far tail. (The 55 per cent: \(K < 100\) means \(Z < \ln(100/97.0)/s = 0.123\), and \(\Phi(0.123) = 0.549\).)

Figure 29.2. The probability density of the item above, mean 100 and standard deviation 25: log-normal (red, \(s = 0.246\), median 97.0) and Gaussian (blue, dashed). The log-normal is zero below zero cost, peaks below its mean, and has the longer tail above, so its 95th percentile, 145.5, lies beyond the Gaussian’s 141.1. The area under each curve is 1.

A sum of log-normal items has no simple formula, so it is done by Monte Carlo, the name (after the casino) for any calculation that lets random numbers drawn by a computer stand in for an uncertain world (F1, section 11 for computing what has no formula): draw every item from its distribution (with a common \(Z_0\) where items are correlated, as above), add them, repeat a million times, sort the totals and read off the percentile. For four independent items like the one above (total mean 400, \(\sigma_K = 50\)), the 80th and 95th percentiles come out at 440.7 and 487.3, against 442.1 and 482.2 for the Gaussian rule. Adding independent items makes the total more nearly Gaussian, so the rule is close. Fully correlated (one \(Z\) for all four), the total keeps the full skew: 477.5 and 582.0, against 484.2 and 564.5. The Gaussian rule then understates the bad tail.

A value known only to an interval. Suppose all we know is that a quantity lies somewhere in an interval of width \(a\), equally likely anywhere in it: a reading rounded to the nearest step of a digitiser, or a frequency known only to lie in one bin of a spectrum. Put the interval at \(-a/2 < u < a/2\). The probability density (F6, section 4) is \(1/a\), so that its area over the interval is 1, the mean is 0, and the variance is

\[ \langle u^2\rangle = \int_{-a/2}^{a/2}u^2\,\frac{du}{a} = \frac{1}{a}\cdot\frac{2}{3}\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^3 = \frac{a^2}{12} . \]

The rms is \(a/\sqrt{12} = 0.289a\), not \(a/2\): most values lie well inside the interval.

At work. A 16-bit digitiser spanning \(\pm10\) mm of a beam position monitor has a step of \(20\ \text{mm}/65\,536 = 0.305\) µm, so rounding adds an rms error of \(0.305/\sqrt{12} = 0.088\) µm. Section 6 uses the same result with \(a = 1/N\), the width of one bin of an \(N\)-turn spectrum in tune units.

Exercise E1. Three cost items of 50, 30 and 20 million CHF each carry a 20 per cent standard deviation. Find \(\sigma_K\) for independent and for fully correlated items, and the contingency for a 90 per cent chance of not overrunning in each case, assuming a Gaussian total.

Answer

\(\sigma_i = 10\), 6 and 4. Independent: \(\sqrt{100 + 36 + 16} = 12.3\); correlated: \(10 + 6 + 4 = 20\). With \(z = 1.282\): 15.8 and 25.6 million CHF, 16 and 26 per cent of the total of 100.

Exercise E2. For the 1232 dipoles with a 10 per cent uncertainty each, how small must a common correlation \(\rho\) be to keep the uncertainty of the total below 1 per cent?

Answer

\(0.1\sqrt{1/N + (1 - 1/N)\rho} \le 0.01\) gives \(1/N + (1 - 1/N)\rho \le 0.01\), so \(\rho \le (0.01 - 1/1232)/(1 - 1/1232) = 0.0092\). Almost any shared cause breaks it.

Exercise E3. An item has mean 200 and standard deviation 100, log-normally distributed. Find its median and its 95th percentile, and compare with a Gaussian of the same mean and spread.

Answer

\(s^2 = \ln(1 + 0.25) = 0.223\), \(s = 0.472\). Median \(200e^{-0.112} = 178.9\). 95th percentile \(178.9\,e^{1.645\times0.472} = 389\). The Gaussian gives \(200 + 1.645\times100 = 364\): it understates the tail by 25.

2. Cost, scaling and contingency

Scaling. The equation for \(C\) already holds the main cost trade-off. For a given momentum, the tunnel shrinks as \(1/B\), but the cost per metre of magnet grows with field. A frontier ring’s cost is dominated by the magnets and RF, followed by tunnels, power and auxiliary systems (Shiltsev and Zimmermann). Dividing the LHC’s cost to CERN, 4980 million CHF (3756 plus 1224), by its circumference gives 187 million CHF per km. This is only a ratio, not a rule, but it gives the scale. The FCC feasibility study of 2025 puts the 90.7 km FCC-ee at 14 020 million CHF for CERN: 6160 for civil engineering (44 per cent), 2840 for technical infrastructure, 4140 for the collider and booster, 590 for injectors and transfer lines and 290 as CERN’s share of the experiments; with the non-CERN part of the experiments, 15 320 million CHF. These figures include contractors’ materials and labour but not the laboratory’s own staff.

Contingency. A cost estimate is a sum of items, each uncertain. Let item \(i\) have expected cost \(K_i\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_i\), and let \(\rho_{ij}\) be the correlation coefficient between items \(i\) and \(j\) (here \(\rho\) with two subscripts is not a bending radius). The total \(K = \sum K_i\) has variance

\[ \sigma_{\text{var}}^2 = \sum_i\sigma_i^2 + 2\sum_{i<j}\rho_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j . \]

If the items are independent (\(\rho_{ij} = 0\)) the errors partly cancel and the total is relatively more certain than its parts. If they are fully correlated (\(\rho_{ij} = 1\), as when one cause such as inflation, a metal price or a schedule slip drives them all), \(\sigma_{\text{var}} = \sum\sigma_i\) and nothing cancels. Contingency is the amount added to the expected cost so that the chance of exceeding it is acceptable. If the total is roughly Gaussian, the 80th percentile lies \(0.842\,\sigma_{\text{var}}\) above the mean.

Worked number. Take the five FCC-ee items above and assume (my assumption, for the arithmetic) a 25 per cent standard deviation on each; the cost estimates of the collider proposals of 2020 were quoted with some 20 to 30 per cent accuracy (Shiltsev and Zimmermann, table VIII). Independent: \(\sigma_{\text{var}} = 1993\) million CHF, 14.2 per cent of the total, and the 80th percentile needs 1678 million CHF, 12 per cent. Fully correlated: \(\sigma_{\text{var}} = 3505\) million CHF and the 80th percentile needs 2950 million CHF, 21 per cent. A Monte Carlo with skewed (log-normal) items gives 11 per cent in the independent case. The lesson is that a contingency computed as if risks were independent is too small when one cause moves many items, which is what happened to the SSC between 1987 and 1993.

Schedule. Time is costed the same way, but a schedule is not a sum: it is the longest chain of tasks that must follow one another, the critical path. A delay off the critical path costs nothing until it uses up its slack (the time a task can slip before something that waits on it is held up); a delay on it moves the end date. The LHC’s 2009 restart had the new quench protection system “on the critical path” (Lamont 2009).

3. Tolerances, survey and geodesy

A machine is specified by tolerances: how far each element may be from its ideal position, field and timing. The tolerance on quadrupole position comes from the closed-orbit amplification of chapter 18. In short: a quadrupole displaced sideways by \(\Delta\) meets the beam off its centre and kicks it by \(kL\,\Delta\), like a small dipole; each kick sets up a closed orbit amplified by \(1/(2\sin\pi Q_x)\) (chapter 18, section 2), where \(Q_x\) is the tune, the number of betatron oscillations in one turn; and random kicks add in quadrature. For random quadrupole offsets of rms \(\sigma_\Delta\) (m) in a ring of FODO cells,

\[ \sigma_{x,co} = \kappa\,\sigma_\Delta, \qquad \kappa = \sqrt{\frac{\beta_{x,\max}}{8\sin^2\pi Q_x}\,N_c\,(kL)^2\,(\beta_{x,\max} + \beta_{x,\min})} , \]

with \(\sigma_{x,co}\) the rms closed-orbit error at the focusing quadrupoles (where \(\beta_x = \beta_{x,\max}\)), \(N_c\) the number of cells and \(kL\) the integrated quadrupole strength (m\(^{-1}\)), the same for the focusing and defocusing quadrupoles. For an LHC-like ring, \(\kappa = 30.5\): 0.1 mm of misalignment gives 3 mm of orbit. Correctors and monitors absorb most of it, so the real budget is what the correctors can hold.

A tolerance is shared out among independent contributions: the magnetic axis relative to the outside of the magnet (fiducialisation), the magnet on its girder (a rigid beam that carries several magnets and is moved as one), the girder in the tunnel, and ground motion since the last survey. Independent errors add in quadrature, \(\sigma^2 = \sum\sigma_k^2\), so one large term dominates and is the one to attack. The FCC feasibility study assumes arc quadrupoles and sextupoles pre-aligned to 50 µm on girders about 6 m long, girder-to-girder tolerances of 200 µm over 50 m and 500 µm over 200 m, and 30 µm for the final-focus quadrupoles; it requires the measurement and adjustment to be at least three times more precise than the tolerance.

Why geodesy. A ring is a plane; the Earth’s level surface is curved. A plane tangent to a sphere of radius \(R_E\) at the ring’s centre lies above the sphere, at horizontal distance \(r\), by \(h \approx r^2/(2R_E)\). To see it, draw the right-angled triangle with corners at the Earth’s centre, the ring’s centre and the ring’s edge (figure 29.3). Its sides are \(R_E\) and \(r\), so by Pythagoras the edge is \(\sqrt{R_E^2 + r^2}\) from the Earth’s centre, and it stands above the sphere by

\[ h = \sqrt{R_E^2 + r^2} - R_E = R_E\left(\sqrt{1 + r^2/R_E^2} - 1\right) \approx \frac{r^2}{2R_E} , \]

using \(\sqrt{1 + x} \approx 1 + x/2\) for small \(x\) (F1, section 8). And a plumb line hangs towards the Earth’s centre, so the one at \(r\) is tilted from the one at the centre by the angle the triangle has there, \(\arctan(r/R_E) \approx r/R_E\) radians. With \(R_E = 6371\) km:

  • LHC, \(r = C/2\pi = 4243\) m: \(h = 1.41\) m, tilt 0.67 mrad.
  • FCC, \(C = 90\,658\) m, \(r = 14\,429\) m: \(h = 16.3\) m, tilt 2.26 mrad.

Figure 29.3. Why a flat ring is not level, not to scale. The ring lies in the plane that touches the Earth’s level surface at its centre \(C\). At the ring’s edge, a distance \(r\) away, the plane stands \(h \approx r^2/(2R_E)\) above the level surface, and the plumb line there, which points to the Earth’s centre \(O\), leans by \(r/R_E\) from the one at \(C\). For the LHC, \(h = 1.41\) m and the lean is 0.67 mrad; the drawing exaggerates the angle about 700-fold.

So “level” is not “flat” on these scales. Spirit levels, hydrostatic levelling (water in connected pots, whose surfaces settle at one level) and plumb lines measure against gravity, which follows the geoid (the level surface of the Earth’s gravity, close to mean sea level, with its local bumps where the rock below is denser or lighter), while the machine is defined in a three-dimensional Cartesian frame. Survey for a large machine is therefore geodesy, the science of the Earth’s shape: a reference network of points in the tunnel, tied to the surface through the shafts, with a model of the geoid to convert between the frames. Within that frame, total stations (theodolites that measure angles and, by a laser, distances) and laser trackers (which follow a reflector held on the component and measure its position in three dimensions) place components in the initial alignment; then smoothing refines the relative positions of neighbours, using wire offsets radially (distances measured from a stretched wire) and levelling vertically, and is repeated during technical stops as the ground moves (FCC study, section 3.5).

4. Hardware commissioning: energy, heat and joints

Before any beam, every circuit is powered, every vacuum sector pumped and leak-tested, every cavity conditioned and every interlock tested. For the LHC in 2008 that meant about 1700 circuits and about 10 000 magnets, in 11 122 test steps (Wenninger 2009).

Stored energy. A superconducting circuit of inductance \(L_m\) (H) carrying current \(I\) (A) stores \(\tfrac12L_mI^2\) in its magnetic field (F4, section 15). An LHC sector’s dipole circuit, 154 magnets, stores about 1.08 GJ at full current (chapter 17); a full beam stores 362 MJ (chapter 21). Most of hardware commissioning is proving that this energy always goes where it is meant to.

A joint. The magnets of a circuit are connected in series by busbars, thick superconducting cables that carry the full current from one magnet to the next. Between magnets, the busbar cables are spliced: the two ends are overlapped and soldered inside copper. The cable itself has zero resistance, but solder is an ordinary metal, so the current must cross a thin layer of it from one cable to the other. The joint’s resistance \(R\) (here a resistance in Ω, not a ring’s radius) is that solder contact, and by Ohm’s law (F4, section 8) it dissipates

\[ P = I^2R . \]

Worked number (LHC, 2008). The nominal joint resistance was 0.35 nΩ; the faulty joint had about 220 nΩ (Wenninger 2009; Lamont gives the nominal as about 0.3 nΩ). At 7 kA the faulty joint dissipated 10.8 W against 17 mW for a good one, 630 times more. At 8.7 kA, where it failed, 16.7 W; at the design 11.85 kA it would have been 30.9 W. The 10.8 W showed up a few days before the incident as a steady temperature rise of up to 40 mK in its cryogenic cell during an hour at 7 kA. Nobody was looking for it then. Afterwards, splices of 50 to 100 nΩ were judged dangerous at high current; 100 nΩ at 1 kA is a drop of 0.1 mV and 0.1 W (Lamont writes the voltage as “100 mV”, which must be a slip for 100 µV). The new detection threshold of 0.3 mV per interconnection, at 11.85 kA, corresponds to \(R = V/I = 0.3\times10^{-3}/11\,850 = 25\) nΩ. (A quench is the sudden loss of superconductivity in a magnet or busbar, which then turns resistive and must have its current removed before it burns; chapter 17.)

Cool-down. Cooling a superconducting ring takes weeks per sector, because almost all the heat to be removed lies above 80 K and is taken out with liquid nitrogen (chapter 17, section on cool-down). The LHC’s eight sectors were cooled one after another through the spring and summer of 2008 and reached 1.9 K in the week before 10 September (Wenninger 2009).

A cavity in a vertical test. A superconducting cavity is first tested bare, standing in a bath of superfluid helium, with a probe and a weakly or critically coupled input. The wall loss at accelerating voltage \(\hat{V}\) is

\[ P_c = \frac{\hat{V}^2}{(R/Q)\,Q_0} , \]

with \(R/Q\) in the linac convention (circuit texts use half of it). The unloaded quality factor \(Q_0\) is found from the decay of the stored energy \(U\) when the drive is switched off. Recall the definition of a quality factor (F5, section 4): \(Q = \omega U/P\), with \(\omega\) the angular frequency (rad/s) and \(P\) the power lost. So the walls take \(P_c = \omega_{RF}U/Q_0\), and the coupler, through which energy also leaves towards the power source, takes \(\omega_{RF}U/Q_{ext}\), which defines the external quality factor \(Q_{ext}\). The two losses add:

\[ \frac{dU}{dt} = -\omega_{RF}U\left(\frac{1}{Q_0} + \frac{1}{Q_{ext}}\right) = -\frac{\omega_{RF}U}{Q_L}, \qquad U(t) = U(0)\,e^{-t/\tau_U}, \qquad \tau_U = \frac{Q_L}{\omega_{RF}} , \]

a first-order decay with the loaded quality factor \(Q_L\). Measuring \(\tau_U\) gives \(Q_L\). To get \(Q_0\), write \(1/Q_L = (1/Q_0)(1 + Q_0/Q_{ext})\) and name the ratio \(\beta_{cp} = Q_0/Q_{ext}\), the coupling factor (the subscript keeps it apart from the relativistic \(\beta\)), which is found from the power reflected back from the cavity:

\[ Q_0 = (1 + \beta_{cp})\,Q_L . \]

Critical coupling, \(\beta_{cp} = 1\), is the match at which no power is reflected in steady state; then the coupler and the walls take equal shares.

Worked number (TESLA cavity). Active length 1.038 m, 1.3 GHz, \(R/Q = 1036\ \Omega\) (518 Ω in the circuit convention of Aune and others). At 25 MV/m, \(\hat{V} = 25.95\) MV and the stored energy is \(U = \hat{V}^2/(\omega_{RF}\,(R/Q)) = 79.6\) J (from \(R/Q = \hat{V}^2/(\omega_{RF}U)\) in the linac convention). With \(Q_0 = 5\times10^{9}\), the TESLA goal, the wall dissipates 130 W into 2 K helium; with \(2\times10^{10}\), the best TESLA Test Facility cavity, 32.5 W. Critically coupled (\(\beta_{cp} = 1\)) at \(Q_0 = 10^{10}\), \(Q_L = 5\times10^9\) and \(\tau_U = 0.61\) s. The measurement is a curve of \(Q_0\) against gradient, ended by a quench (a spot of the niobium wall going normal-conducting) or by field emission (electrons pulled out of the wall by the strong surface field) (chapter 13).

5. Beam commissioning: threading the first turn

The trajectory response. Before a beam circulates, it makes one pass, and the only tool is its trajectory as seen by the beam position monitors (BPMs). In the Courant-Snyder description of chapter 16, the transfer matrix from point \(i\) to a later point \(j\) has the element

\[ M_{12}(i \to j) = \sqrt{\beta_{x,i}\beta_{x,j}}\,\sin(\psi_j - \psi_i) . \]

Here \(\beta_{x,i}\) (m) is the betatron function at \(i\) and \(\psi\) (rad) the betatron phase, which advances by \(2\pi\) for each full betatron oscillation. Where does the formula come from? In the normalised coordinates of chapter 18, \(X = x/\sqrt{\beta_x}\) and \(P = (\alpha_xx + \beta_xx')/\sqrt{\beta_x}\), the motion from \(i\) to \(j\) is a plain rotation by the phase advance \(\psi_j - \psi_i\). A particle at \(x = 0\) with slope \(x'\) has \(X_i = 0\) and \(P_i = \sqrt{\beta_{x,i}}\,x'\); the rotation gives \(X_j = P_i\sin(\psi_j - \psi_i)\); and \(x_j = \sqrt{\beta_{x,j}}\,X_j\). Together, \(x_j = \sqrt{\beta_{x,i}\beta_{x,j}}\sin(\psi_j - \psi_i)\,x'\).

A corrector, a small dipole magnet, at \(i\) gives a kick \(\theta_i\) (rad) to a beam arriving on axis, \((x, x') = (0, \theta_i)\), so downstream

\[ x_j = \sqrt{\beta_{x,i}\beta_{x,j}}\,\sin(\psi_j - \psi_i)\,\theta_i \quad (j \text{ after } i), \]

and \(x_j = 0\) upstream. Collected for all monitors and correctors, this is a response matrix \(R_{ji}\) (m/rad): row \(j\) for monitor \(j\), column \(i\) for corrector \(i\), so that a set of kicks \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) changes the readings by \(R\boldsymbol{\theta}\). It is lower-triangular in the order the beam meets them: a corrector cannot move the beam at monitors it has not yet reached, so every entry above the diagonal is zero. Given the measured trajectory \(\mathbf{x}\), the corrector settings that minimise \(|\mathbf{x} + R\boldsymbol{\theta}|^2\), the sum of the squares of the corrected readings, are \(\boldsymbol{\theta} = -R^{+}\mathbf{x}\), with \(R^{+}\) the pseudo-inverse, the least-squares answer of chapter 8. It is computed by singular-value decomposition (chapter 18, with its use in orbit correction), with the smallest singular values dropped: they belong to combinations of correctors that hardly move the beam, and inverting them would turn small noise in the readings into large kicks.

Worked number (LHC arc). The arc correctors give up to 80.8 µrad at 7 TeV. At a focusing quadrupole with \(\beta_{x} = 182.9\) m, a full kick produces downstream oscillations of \(\sqrt{182.9\times182.9}\times80.8\ \mu\text{rad} = 14.8\) mm at the focusing quadrupoles where the phase is right, and 6.1 mm at the defocusing ones (\(\beta_x = 30.9\) m) (model ring of chapter 18). The arc aperture is a few centimetres, so a few uncorrected kicks of this size lose the beam within a cell or two.

Figure 29.4. One corrector kick of 80.8 µrad seen on the first pass, computed from \(x_j = \sqrt{\beta_{x,i}\beta_{x,j}}\sin(\psi_j - \psi_i)\,\theta_i\) for the model ring of chapter 18 (90.6° of phase per cell). Red: monitors at the focusing quadrupoles, \(\beta_x = 182.9\) m, reaching 14.8 mm; blue: at the defocusing ones, \(\beta_x = 30.9\) m, reaching 6.1 mm. Upstream of the corrector nothing moves: the response matrix is lower-triangular.

Sector by sector. Hence the method used at the LHC on 10 September 2008: inject, let the beam run to a collimator (a block of metal that can be moved in close to the beam to intercept it) closed to stop it at the end of an insertion, correct the trajectory over that stretch, then open the collimator and go on to the next. Both beams went through all sectors at the first shot with small losses, and the first turn was closed for both within the morning shift (Wenninger 2009). CERN’s press release of that day gives 10:28 for beam 1’s first full turn.

The closed orbit. Once the beam goes round, the trajectory on turn two differs from turn one. The closed orbit is the path that closes on itself after one turn, the fixed point \(\mathbf{z}_{co} = M\mathbf{z}_{co} + \mathbf{d}\) of the one-turn map, with \(\mathbf{z} = (x, x')\), \(M\) the one-turn matrix and \(\mathbf{d}\) the sum of kicks propagated to the reference point; so \(\mathbf{z}_{co} = (I - M)^{-1}\mathbf{d}\), and the \(1/(2\sin\pi Q_x)\) of chapter 18 comes from the inverse. To see it, take one kick \(\theta\) at the reference point and work in the normalised coordinates above, where the one-turn map is a rotation by \(\mu = 2\pi Q_x\):

\[ I - M = \begin{pmatrix}1 - \cos\mu & -\sin\mu\\ \sin\mu & 1 - \cos\mu\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det(I - M) = 2 - 2\cos\mu = 4\sin^2\frac{\mu}{2} . \]

With \(\mathbf{d} = (0, \sqrt{\beta_x}\,\theta)\) and the inverse of a \(2\times2\) matrix (chapter 8), the first component is \(X_{co} = \sin\mu\,\sqrt{\beta_x}\,\theta/(4\sin^2\tfrac{\mu}{2})\). Since \(\sin\mu = 2\sin\tfrac{\mu}{2}\cos\tfrac{\mu}{2}\), this is \(X_{co} = \sqrt{\beta_x}\,\theta\cos(\pi Q_x)/(2\sin\pi Q_x)\), or in metres \(x_{co} = \beta_x\theta\cos(\pi Q_x)/(2\sin\pi Q_x)\). The determinant vanishes, and the orbit grows without limit, when \(Q_x\) is a whole number. Its response matrix differs from the single-pass one, and the correction continues with it.

Capture. A beam injected with the RF off, or at the wrong phase or frequency, is not held in a bucket, the region of stable motion round the RF phase that brings each particle back towards the bunch centre (chapter 19). Without it nothing pulls stragglers back. A particle with momentum offset \(\delta = \Delta p/p\) arrives later each turn by \(\eta T_{rev}\delta\), with \(T_{rev}\) the revolution period and \(\eta = \alpha_c - 1/\gamma^2\) the slip factor (chapter 12): \(\alpha_c\), the momentum compaction, is how much longer the orbit of a higher-momentum particle is, and \(1/\gamma^2\) how much faster it goes. Two particles at \(\pm2\sigma_\delta\) separate by \(4\eta T_{rev}\sigma_\delta\) per turn.

Worked number (LHC injection). At 450 GeV/\(c\), \(\gamma = 479.6\) and with \(\alpha_c = 3.225\times10^{-4}\) (chapter 19), \(\eta = 3.18\times10^{-4}\); \(T_{rev} = 88.92\) µs. An SPS bunch of 0.6 eVs and 1.6 ns (chapter 19; the longitudinal emittance in eV s is the area the bunch fills in the plane of arrival time and energy), treated as an upright ellipse of half-length 0.8 ns and area \(\pi\times(0.8\ \text{ns})\times\Delta E\) (an ellipse of half-axes \(a\) and \(b\) has area \(\pi ab\)), has a half-height \(\Delta E = 0.6/(\pi\times0.8\times10^{-9}) = 239\) MeV, or \(\Delta E/E = 5.3\times10^{-4}\); taking the ellipse’s edge at \(2\sigma_\delta\) gives \(\sigma_\delta \approx 2.7\times10^{-4}\) (my estimate). The \(\pm2\sigma_\delta\) particles then drift apart by \(4\eta T_{rev}\sigma_\delta = 4\times3.18\times10^{-4}\times88.92\ \mu\text{s}\times2.65\times10^{-4} = 0.030\) ns a turn (with \(\sigma_\delta\) unrounded, half of \(5.3\times10^{-4}\)): they are a 2.5 ns bucket length apart after about 83 turns, and 9 ns apart after 300 turns. On the evening of 10 September the LHC had “circulating, un-captured beam (300+ turns)”; captured beam with a lifetime of more than ten minutes followed on 11 September, and by the afternoon of 12 September a closed orbit almost within tolerance and a lifetime of over an hour (Wenninger 2009).

Pre-requisite (continued): Random signals and noise, spectral density

Pre-requisite. This section adds one result to the noise of earlier chapters: how precisely the frequency of a pure tone can be found from \(N\) noisy samples, and why the error falls as \(N^{-3/2}\), much faster than the width \(1/N\) of one bin of the spectrum. Section 6 uses it to judge how well a tune can be measured from turn-by-turn data. The derivation is the least-squares fit of chapter 8 applied to a sinusoid; the measurement setting follows the Handbook of Accelerator Physics and Engineering, section 4.7.8.1 (tune measurement), and Lee, Accelerator Physics, appendix B, section I.2 (the discrete Fourier transform).

Reminder: chapter 8 described noise as a random signal with a power in each band of frequency and defined the signal-to-noise ratio; chapter 16 found the tune between two bins by interpolation. Here the two meet: a tone with noise added.

The model. A beam position monitor records, once a turn,

\[ x_n = A\cos(2\pi\nu n + \phi) + e_n, \qquad n = 0, 1, \ldots, N - 1, \]

with \(A\) (m) the amplitude of the betatron oscillation after a kick, \(\nu\) the fractional tune (cycles per turn), \(\phi\) a phase, and \(e_n\) (m) the monitor’s noise. This is a sampled simple harmonic motion: after a kick the beam’s centre swings from side to side, \(\nu\) of a swing per turn beyond the whole number, and the monitor sees it once a turn. Take the noise to be white: independent from turn to turn, mean zero, rms \(\sigma\) (“white” because, like white light, it holds all frequencies equally). Assume too that \(A\) stays constant over the record; a real kicked beam decoheres (its particles, with slightly different tunes, drift out of step, so the swing of the centre fades; chapter 16), which shortens the useful record.

The best any method can do. Noise moves the peak of the spectrum. The question is how far, and the cleanest answer comes from the best estimator there is for this problem: a least-squares fit of \(A\), \(\nu\) and \(\phi\) to the samples. Near the true values, a small change \(\delta\nu\) and \(\delta\phi\) changes sample \(n\) by the derivative of the cosine times the change of its argument, \(\delta\theta_n = 2\pi n\,\delta\nu + \delta\phi\) (F1, sections 2 and 5):

\[ \delta x_n = -A\sin\theta_n\,(2\pi n\,\delta\nu + \delta\phi), \qquad \theta_n = 2\pi\nu n + \phi . \]

The fit chooses \(\delta\nu\) and \(\delta\phi\) to soak up as much of the noise \(e_n\) as possible. (A change of \(A\) moves the samples in proportion to \(\cos\theta_n\), which over many cycles is unrelated to \(\sin\theta_n\), so it can be left out.) This is a straight-line fit in disguise: multiply out and the model is \(-A\sin\theta_n\) times a line \(\delta\phi + 2\pi\delta\nu\,n\) in the turn number \(n\), with slope \(2\pi\delta\nu\). Chapter 8 gave the variances of fitted coefficients as the diagonal of \((A^TWA)^{-1}\) (in that formula \(A\) is the design matrix of the fit, not the amplitude). For a line \(p_0 + p_1n\) through points of equal variance \(s^2\), \(A^TWA = s^{-2}\begin{pmatrix}N & \sum n\\ \sum n & \sum n^2\end{pmatrix}\). The inverse of a \(2\times2\) matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}a & b\\ c & d\end{pmatrix}\) is \(\begin{pmatrix}d & -b\\ -c & a\end{pmatrix}\) divided by \(ad - bc\) (chapter 8), so the lower diagonal element of the inverse is \(s^2N/[N\sum n^2 - (\sum n)^2]\). Dividing top and bottom by \(N\) and using \(\bar{n} = \sum n/N\), the bottom becomes \(\sum n^2 - N\bar{n}^2 = \sum_n(n - \bar{n})^2\), so the element is \(s^2/\sum_n(n - \bar{n})^2\): the variance of the slope is the variance per point divided by the spread of the turn numbers. Here each point is weighted by \(A^2\sin^2\theta_n\), which averages to \(A^2/2\) over many cycles (the average of \(\sin^2\) over a cycle is \(\tfrac12\), since \(\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1\) and the two average alike), so the effective noise variance per point is \(\sigma^2/(A^2/2)\). For \(n = 0\) to \(N - 1\), \(\bar{n} = (N - 1)/2\) and \(\sum n^2 = (N - 1)N(2N - 1)/6\), so \(\sum(n - \bar{n})^2 = \sum n^2 - N\bar{n}^2 = N(N^2 - 1)/12 \approx N^3/12\), and

\[ \sigma_{2\pi\nu}^2 = \frac{2\sigma^2}{A^2}\cdot\frac{12}{N^3}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \sigma_\nu = \frac{\sqrt{6}}{\pi}\,\frac{\sigma}{A}\,\frac{1}{N^{3/2}} . \]

Read it as a phase measurement. Each turn tells the phase of the oscillation to about \(\sigma/A\) radians. The tune is the slope of phase against turn number. Doubling the record doubles the number of points and doubles the lever arm over which the slope is measured, so the error falls as \(N^{-3/2}\), a factor 2.8 for each doubling. The bin width falls only as \(1/N\). For Gaussian noise the least-squares fit is also the most likely fit, and this variance is then the Cramér-Rao bound of statistics (stated here, not proved): no unbiased method can beat it, and a good one comes within a small factor of it.

At work. Take \(\sigma/A = 0.05\), the 5 per cent noise of section 6’s simulation. The bound gives \(7.6\times10^{-5}\), \(9.5\times10^{-6}\) and \(1.19\times10^{-6}\) for \(N\) = 64, 256 and 1024. I ran the Hann-window, two-bin interpolation of chapter 16 on 2000 records at each \(N\), with tunes drawn at random between 0.2 and 0.3: its rms errors were \(1.4\times10^{-4}\), \(1.7\times10^{-5}\) and \(2.2\times10^{-6}\), about 1.8 times the bound at every \(N\). (Section 6 quotes a separate run with somewhat larger errors; the law is the same.) The window gives little weight to the record’s ends, where the lever arm is longest, which costs that factor; but the \(N^{-3/2}\) law holds. Without noise the same interpolation errs by only \(10^{-7}\) at \(N = 64\), so here the noise, not the method, sets the error. The bare peak bin, \(4.4\times10^{-3}\) at \(N = 64\), follows the \(1/(N\sqrt{12})\) of the uniform error and is no better with or without noise.

For a real machine: a kick of \(A = 0.5\) mm seen by a monitor with \(\sigma = 0.1\) mm per turn (illustrative figures) has \(\sigma/A = 0.2\). The bound reaches \(10^{-4}\) at \(N = 134\) turns and \(4.8\times10^{-6}\) at 1024 turns, if the beam stays coherent that long.

Answer

6. Measuring tune and chromaticity

Tune. Kick the beam, record its position at one BPM for \(N\) turns, and Fourier-transform: break the record into sinusoids of different frequencies (F5, section 16) with the discrete Fourier transform of chapter 8. The spectrum has bins spaced \(1/N\) in tune: the transform of \(N\) samples has \(N\) bins spread evenly over a range of one oscillation per turn, so the peak gives the tune only to the nearest \(1/N\). If the true fractional tune falls anywhere within a bin with equal probability, the error of taking the peak bin is uniform over \(\pm1/(2N)\), and its rms is

\[ \sigma_Q = \frac{1}{N\sqrt{12}} . \]

For 1024 turns, \(2.8\times10^{-4}\). Interpolating between the peak bin and its neighbour, after multiplying the data by a smooth window (a weight that falls to zero at both ends of the record, which stops the abrupt start and stop from smearing the peak; chapter 16), does far better. In my simulation with a Hann window, a pure tone and 5 per cent noise, the rms error for \(N\) = 64, 256 and 1024 was \(2.0\times10^{-4}\), \(2.0\times10^{-5}\) and \(2.3\times10^{-6}\), against \(4.5\times10^{-3}\), \(1.1\times10^{-3}\) and \(2.8\times10^{-4}\) for the bare peak. Decoherence and the size of the kick the beam can tolerate set the real limits. At the LHC in 2008, tunes were measured with a chirp excitation, a small drive whose frequency sweeps across the expected tune and makes the beam respond most where it matches (Wenninger 2009).

Chromaticity. The chromaticity \(Q' = \Delta Q/\delta\) is the change of tune per unit relative momentum offset: a quadrupole focuses a stiffer particle less, so its tune differs (chapter 16). To measure it the momentum must be changed on purpose, and the RF does that. The RF system sets the revolution frequency: a bunch must meet the RF at the same phase every turn, so with the harmonic number \(h\) (the whole number of RF cycles in one turn, chapter 19) fixed, \(f_{RF} = h/T_{rev}\). The revolution period of an off-momentum particle is \(T_{rev} = C/(\beta c)\), with \(C\) the length of its orbit and \(\beta c\) its speed. For small changes, the relative change of a quotient is the relative change of the top minus that of the bottom (F1, section 5), so \(\Delta T_{rev}/T_{rev} = \Delta C/C - \Delta\beta/\beta = \alpha_c\delta - \delta/\gamma^2 = \eta\delta\), using the momentum compaction \(\Delta C/C = \alpha_c\delta\) (chapter 16) and \(\Delta\beta/\beta = \delta/\gamma^2\). If the RF frequency is changed, the beam’s momentum moves until its revolution period matches, \(\Delta f_{RF}/f_{RF} = -\Delta T_{rev}/T_{rev} = -\eta\delta\):

\[ \delta = -\frac{1}{\eta}\,\frac{\Delta f_{RF}}{f_{RF}} . \]

Measure the tune at several RF frequencies and fit \(Q' = \Delta Q/\delta\).

Worked number (LHC, 450 GeV). \(f_{RF} = 400.789\) MHz. With \(\eta = 3.18\times10^{-4}\) (section 5), a trim of +100 Hz gives \(\delta = -(100/400.789\times10^6)/3.18\times10^{-4} = -7.84\times10^{-4}\). With \(Q' = 2\) the tune moves by \(2\times(-7.84\times10^{-4}) = -0.0016\), easily seen. Repeat at several trims, plot the tune against \(\delta\), and the slope of the best straight line (chapter 8) is \(Q'\). Measurements of the same kind, of RF frequency against orbit, also give the beam’s momentum; at the LHC’s first beam it was found to be 450.5 GeV/\(c\) within 0.2 GeV/\(c\) (Wenninger 2009).

Pre-requisite (continued): Counting statistics (Poisson)

Pre-requisite. This section turns the statistics of random counts to random failures: the chance that a system survives a given time, why the failure rates of systems in series add, the expected length of a fill that a fault may cut short, the number of trips in a run and the availability when each trip costs a repair. Section 7 uses it for the reliability of a collider. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 5, §114 (Poisson’s formula, as the limit of many small, independent chances).

Reminder: chapter 1 gave Poisson’s rule for counts, and chapter 8 showed that the gap between random counts at a mean rate \(n\) exceeds \(t\) with probability \(e^{-nt}\). A failure is a count we would rather not have.

Random failure. Suppose a system can fail at any moment, with the same small chance \(dt/M\) in every short interval \(dt\), whatever has happened before. \(M\) (h) is its mean time between failures (MTBF), and \(1/M\) its failure rate. This describes electronics and many other parts in the middle of their lives, after early defects have been weeded out and before wear sets in; it does not describe a part that wears. Let \(S(t)\) be the probability that the system is still working at time \(t\). To survive to \(t + dt\) it must survive to \(t\) and then not fail in \(dt\):

\[ S(t + dt) = S(t)\left(1 - \frac{dt}{M}\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dS}{dt} = -\frac{S}{M} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad S(t) = e^{-t/M} . \]

The middle step moves \(S(t)\) to the left and divides by \(dt\); the last solves the equation “the rate of fall is proportional to what is left”, whose solution is the exponential, with \(S(0) = 1\) (F1, section 10). This is chapter 8’s gap law with \(n = 1/M\). The mean time to the first failure is \(M\), as the name requires. To check it: the chance that the failure comes between \(t\) and \(t + dt\) is the fall of \(S\) there, \(f(t)\,dt = -(dS/dt)\,dt = e^{-t/M}\,dt/M\), and the mean is \(\int_0^\infty t\,f(t)\,dt\). With \(u = t/M\) this is \(M\int_0^\infty u\,e^{-u}\,du = M\), the integral being 1 (by parts, as below). Over a long time \(T\) the number of failures \(k\) (with each failure repaired at once and the clock started again) follows Poisson’s formula (F6, section 6) with mean \(T/M\): \(P(k) = (T/M)^ke^{-T/M}/k!\). Landau derives it as the limit of many small, independent chances, which is exactly this picture.

Systems in series. A machine runs only if every one of its systems runs. If the systems fail independently, the chance that all survive to \(t\) is the product of their chances:

\[ S_{tot}(t) = \prod_i e^{-t/M_i} = e^{-t\sum_i 1/M_i}, \qquad \frac{1}{M_{tot}} = \sum_i\frac{1}{M_i} . \]

Failure rates add, like conductances in parallel. \(N\) identical systems give \(M_{tot} = M/N\): a thousand units that each fail once in a hundred thousand hours stop the machine once in a hundred hours.

The fill that may be cut short. A fill is planned to last \(T_f\) and is ended early by the first failure. It completes with probability \(e^{-T_f/M}\). Its expected length is the planned length if nothing fails, and the failure time \(t\) if something fails first:

\[ \langle t_{fill}\rangle = \int_0^{T_f}t\,\frac{e^{-t/M}}{M}\,dt + T_fe^{-T_f/M} = M\left(1 - e^{-T_f/M}\right), \]

after integrating the first term by parts (F1, section 7): with \(u = t\) and \(dv = e^{-t/M}dt/M\), so \(v = -e^{-t/M}\),

\[ \int_0^{T_f}t\,\frac{e^{-t/M}}{M}\,dt = \Big[-t\,e^{-t/M}\Big]_0^{T_f} + \int_0^{T_f}e^{-t/M}\,dt = -T_fe^{-T_f/M} + M\left(1 - e^{-T_f/M}\right), \]

and the first part cancels the \(T_fe^{-T_f/M}\) of the fills that complete. The result is the area under \(S(t)\) from 0 to \(T_f\), which makes sense: at each moment the fill is still running with probability \(S\). For \(T_f \ll M\) it is close to \(T_f\); for \(T_f \gg M\) it saturates at \(M\).

At work. With \(M_{tot} = 10\) h and a planned fill of 7.5 h, the fill completes with probability \(e^{-0.75} = 0.47\) and lasts on average \(10(1 - 0.47) = 5.28\) h, 70 per cent of the plan. Doubling \(M_{tot}\) to 20 h gives 0.69 and 6.25 h.

A fill planned for 7.5 h reaches its end with probability 0.47 or 0.69. The shaded area under the red curve up to 7.5 h is the mean length of a fill, 5.28 h; the whole area under each curve is \(M_{tot}\), the mean time to the first fault.

Availability. If each failure costs a mean repair and recovery time \(R\) (h; here not a resistance), the machine alternates between up-times of mean \(M\) and down-times of mean \(R\). Over a long run of \(n\) such cycles it is up for about \(nM\) out of \(n(M + R)\), so the fraction of time it is up, its availability, is

\[ A = \frac{M}{M + R} . \]

At work. Take a light source (illustrative figures) whose RF fails on average every 200 h, its power supplies every 500 h, its vacuum and cryogenics every 1000 h and its controls every 300 h. Then \(1/M_{tot} = 1/200 + 1/500 + 1/1000 + 1/300 = 0.01133\) h\(^{-1}\) and \(M_{tot} = 88\) h. A year of 5000 scheduled hours brings \(5000/88 = 57\) trips on average. At \(R = 1.5\) h each, 85 h are lost and \(A = 88/(88 + 1.5) = 98.3\) per cent. A 12 h shift passes without a trip with probability \(e^{-12/88} = 0.87\). For a busier machine with \(M_{tot} = 8\) h, a day holds on average 3 trips: the chance of none is \(e^{-3} = 0.05\), and of three or more, one minus the chances of 0, 1 and 2, \(1 - e^{-3}(1 + 3 + 3^2/2) = 0.58\).

Exercise F1. A ring has 400 power converters, each with an MTBF of \(2\times10^5\) h. What is their combined MTBF, and the chance that a 10 h fill is not ended by one of them?

Answer

\(M_{tot} = 2\times10^5/400 = 500\) h. \(e^{-10/500} = 0.980\): one fill in fifty is lost to the converters alone.

Exercise F2. What overall MTBF is needed for 80 per cent of 10 h fills to complete?

Answer

\(e^{-10/M} = 0.8\) gives \(M = 10/\ln1.25 = 44.8\) h.

Exercise F3. Fills are planned for 12 h on a machine whose \(M_{tot}\) is 12 h. What is the mean fill length?

Answer

\(12(1 - e^{-1}) = 7.59\) h. Only 37 per cent of fills reach the plan, and the average fill delivers 63 per cent of the planned time.

7. Running: availability and integrated luminosity

A collider’s product is integrated luminosity. Over a year,

\[ \int\mathcal{L}\,dt = \bar{\mathcal{L}}\,T_{sb} , \]

where \(T_{sb}\) is the time in collisions (“stable beams”) and \(\bar{\mathcal{L}}\) the average luminosity during it. \(T_{sb}\) is the scheduled time minus faults, minus the turnaround between fills (injection; the ramp, which raises the energy; the squeeze, which reduces \(\beta^*\) at the collision points, chapter 23), minus machine-development time.

Worked number (HL-LHC). The goal is about 250 fb\(^{-1}\) a year at a levelled \(5\times10^{34}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\) (Brüning and Zerlauth). That is \(2.5\times10^{41}/5\times10^{34} = 5\times10^{6}\) s, 57.9 days, of levelled collisions. A nominal fill of 7.5 h at that level gives \(5\times10^{34}\times7.5\times3600\) s \(= 1.35\times10^{39}\) cm\(^{-2}\), 1.35 fb\(^{-1}\), so 185 fills; with a turnaround of at least 2.5 h, that is \(185\times10\) h, 77 days, if nothing ever failed. In the last three years of Run 2 the LHC was in collision “during almost half the time” of high-intensity proton operation, with the rest shared about equally between equipment failures and the regular operational sequence. At 50 per cent, 58 days of collisions need 116 scheduled days. The optimum fill length when the luminosity decays is derived in chapter 23.

Systems in series. A machine stops if any of its systems stops. If system \(i\) fails at random with a mean time between failures \(M_i\), the rates add: the machine’s mean time between failures is \(M_{tot} = 1/\sum(1/M_i)\), and the probability that a fill of length \(T_f\) ends as planned is \(e^{-T_f/M_{tot}}\). As an illustration, 1700 power converters each with \(M_i = 10^5\) h (an assumed figure) would by themselves stop the machine every 59 h. A machine whose overall \(M_{tot}\) is 10 h completes only 47 per cent of 7.5 h fills. This is why reliability is designed in from the start, and why the LHC keeps an Accelerator Fault Tracker that records and classifies every stop, so that repeated failures can be found and removed (Brüning and Zerlauth).

Pre-requisite (continued): Energy loss in matter, range, Bragg peak

Pre-requisite. This section adds what radiation protection needs to the energy loss of earlier chapters: the energy deposited per kilogram (absorbed dose, in grays) and its weighting for biological harm (equivalent dose, in sieverts); the exponential attenuation of photons and neutrons in a shield, with its half- and tenth-value layers; and the bremsstrahlung X-rays that an electron beam makes wherever it stops. Section 8 uses all three, and “How one person can begin” uses the last for a home-built tube. Taught from Livingston and Blewett, Particle Accelerators, chapter 14, sections 14-1 (permissible dose and its units), 14-3 (electron absorption and radiation loss), 14-4 (X-ray attenuation) and 14-10 (nucleonic showers); Lee, Accelerator Physics, appendix B, section V (dose units); and the Handbook of Accelerator Physics and Engineering, section 3.3.1 (the critical energy of electrons).

Reminder: chapter 1 gave the energy a charged particle loses per unit length, \(-dW/dx\) (the Bethe formula), and the range at which it stops, and showed that per gram crossed the loss hardly depends on the material. Divided by the density \(\rho\) it is the mass stopping power \(S = -(1/\rho)\,dW/dx\) (MeV cm\(^2\)/g), the energy lost per unit of areal density crossed. Here the question turns round: not what happens to the particle, but what happens to the matter, and to people.

Absorbed dose. The absorbed dose \(D\) is the energy deposited per unit mass:

\[ D = \frac{dE}{dm}, \qquad 1\ \text{Gy} = 1\ \text{J/kg} . \]

The older unit, the rad, was 100 erg/g, so 1 Gy = 100 rad. For charged particles it follows from the stopping power. Let a fluence \(\Phi\) (particles per cm\(^2\)) cross a thin slab of area \(a\), thickness \(dx\) and density \(\rho\). Each particle deposits \(S\rho\,dx\); there are \(\Phi a\) of them; the slab’s mass is \(\rho a\,dx\). So

\[ D = \frac{\Phi a\,S\rho\,dx}{\rho a\,dx} = \Phi S , \]

in MeV/g when \(\Phi\) is in cm\(^{-2}\) and \(S\) in MeV cm\(^2\)/g. Multiply by \(1.602\times10^{-13}\) J/MeV and by 1000 g/kg for grays.

At work. Protons near minimum ionisation, the lowest point of the Bethe formula near \(\beta\gamma \approx 3\) (a kinetic energy of about 2 GeV), lose about 2 MeV cm\(^2\)/g in water. A fluence of \(10^9\) cm\(^{-2}\) deposits \(2\times10^9\) MeV/g, which is \(2\times10^9\times1.602\times10^{-13}\ \text{J}\times1000/\text{kg} = 0.32\) Gy. A beam of \(10^{9}\) protons focused to 1 cm\(^2\) gives that to whatever it crosses.

Equivalent dose. The same joule per kilogram does different harm depending on how densely the particle ionises along its track: a cell may repair one break but not several close together. Livingston and Blewett describe the older practice of weighting by a relative biological effectiveness, giving the rem. Today the equivalent dose is

\[ H = w_R\,D, \qquad 1\ \text{Sv} = 1\ \text{J/kg (weighted)} = 100\ \text{rem}, \]

with the radiation weighting factor \(w_R\) set by the International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP, 2007): 1 for photons, electrons and muons; 2 for protons; 20 for alpha particles and heavy ions; and between 2.5 and 20 for neutrons, depending on their energy. Older tables, such as Lee’s, give 10 for protons. For photons, 1 Gy counts as 1 Sv, which section 8 uses. What area monitors read, and what the limits of section 8 are written in, is the ambient dose equivalent: a standard estimate of the equivalent dose 10 mm deep in a body at that place. For scale, natural background gives of the order of 1 to 3 mSv a year, depending on where one lives.

Distance. From a small source radiating equally in all directions, the same particles cross ever larger spheres, of area \(4\pi r^2\), so the number per unit area, and with it the dose rate, falls as \(1/r^2\). Going from 1 m to 3 m cuts it ninefold, with no shielding at all.

Exponential attenuation. A charged particle loses energy steadily and stops at a range. A photon or a neutron does not: it crosses matter unchanged until a single event removes it (for a photon, photoelectric absorption, Compton scattering or pair production; for a neutron, a nuclear collision). In a thin layer \(dx\) the chance of such an event is \(n\sigma\,dx\), with \(n\) the number of atoms per unit volume and \(\sigma\) the cross-section (chapter 3). Write \(\mu = n\sigma\) (m\(^{-1}\)), the linear attenuation coefficient (here \(\mu\) is not the phase advance of later sections). Of \(I\) photons entering the layer, \(I\mu\,dx\) are removed, so the intensity obeys \(dI/dx = -\mu I\), the same equation as the survival law above with depth in place of time (F1, section 10), and

\[ I(x) = I_0e^{-\mu x} . \]

Since \(\mu\) is proportional to density, tables give the mass attenuation coefficient \(\mu/\rho\) (cm\(^2\)/g), and the attenuation depends on the areal density \(\rho x\) (g/cm\(^2\)). The thickness that halves the intensity is the half-value layer, \(\text{HVL} = \ln2/\mu\) (from \(e^{-\mu x} = \tfrac12\), take logarithms: \(\mu x = \ln 2\)); the thickness that divides it by ten is the tenth-value layer,

\[ \text{TVL} = \frac{\ln10}{\mu} = \frac{\ln10}{\ln2}\,\text{HVL} = 3.32\ \text{HVL} . \]

To reduce an intensity by a factor \(F\) needs \(e^{-\mu d} = 1/F\), so \(d = \ln F/\mu = \text{TVL}\times\log_{10}F\): one TVL per factor of ten. This is section 8’s shielding formula.

Narrow and broad beams. The law above holds for a narrow beam, in which a scattered photon leaves the beam and counts as removed. In a thick shield lit by a broad beam, many scattered photons still travel forwards and reach the far side, with less energy (Livingston and Blewett, section 14-4). The dose behind a real shield is therefore larger than the narrow-beam law predicts, by a “build-up” factor, and practical shielding uses broad-beam TVLs measured or computed for the real geometry. For the 1.25 MeV gamma rays of cobalt-60 (a radioactive isotope made in reactors, whose decays give two gamma rays of 1.17 and 1.33 MeV, 1.25 MeV on average; F7, section 13) in concrete of 2.35 g/cm\(^3\), the narrow-beam \(\mu/\rho\) is about 0.057 cm\(^2\)/g, so \(\mu = 0.134\) cm\(^{-1}\) and the narrow-beam TVL is 17.2 cm. The broad-beam TVL used in section 8 is 23 cm.

Neutrons from high-energy protons. Above a few hundred MeV, a lost proton starts a nuclear cascade: it breaks a nucleus, the fragments and knocked-out neutrons break others, and so on, until the energy is shared among many slower particles. Neutrons, having no charge, lose no energy to ionisation and travel furthest, and deep in a shield its neutrons again fall off exponentially. Livingston and Blewett (section 14-10) report a half-value thickness of 8.5 in. for the shower of 3 GeV protons in iron-loaded concrete of 4.3 g/cm\(^3\), which scales by density to 15.6 in. (39.6 cm) of ordinary concrete. The Brookhaven group called this a lower limit, and its most penetrating part may need up to 18.3 in.; take the figures below as a minimum. That is a TVL of \(3.32\times39.6 = 132\) cm and an attenuation length (\(1/\mu\), the depth that divides by \(e\)) of \(39.6/\ln2 = 57\) cm, or about 134 g/cm\(^2\) for concrete of 2.35 g/cm\(^3\). A reduction by \(10^6\) needs 7.9 m of concrete, which is why high-energy machines are built in tunnels under metres of earth.

Bremsstrahlung. An electron that strikes matter is deflected by the electric fields of the nuclei, and an accelerated charge radiates (chapter 1). The result is bremsstrahlung, “braking radiation”: a continuous spectrum of X-rays, most numerous at low photon energy and ending at the electron’s kinetic energy \(W\), since one photon cannot carry more energy than the electron had. A tube at voltage \(V\) makes X-rays up to \(eV\): 25 keV at 25 kV (F7, section 5).

How much of the electron’s energy becomes X-rays? The radiative loss rate grows in proportion to the electron’s energy (and as \(Z^2\)), while the ionisation loss is nearly constant. Their ratio is about \(W/E_c\), where the critical energy is \(E_c \approx 800\ \text{MeV}/(Z + 1.2)\) (Handbook, section 3.3.1): 26.5 MeV for copper, 10.6 MeV for tungsten. While the electron slows by \(dW\), the fraction \(r/(1 + r)\) of that, with \(r = W/E_c\), goes into radiation. An electron that stops in a thick target, starting from \(W_0\), therefore radiates a fraction

\[ Y = \frac{1}{W_0}\int_0^{W_0}\frac{W/E_c}{1 + W/E_c}\,dW = 1 - \frac{E_c}{W_0}\ln\left(1 + \frac{W_0}{E_c}\right) \approx \frac{W_0}{2E_c} \quad (W_0 \ll E_c). \]

The steps: write the integrand as \(1 - 1/(1 + W/E_c)\); the first part integrates to \(W_0\) and the second to \(E_c\ln(1 + W_0/E_c)\) (F1, section 4: the integral of \(1/(1 + u)\) is \(\ln(1 + u)\)). For small \(x = W_0/E_c\), the Taylor series \(\ln(1 + x) \approx x - x^2/2\) (F1, section 8) gives \(Y \approx 1 - (1 - x/2) = x/2\).

The ratio \(W/E_c\) is a high-energy formula and only a rough guide at tens of keV, but it gives the right order of magnitude.

At work. A 25 kV electron beam stopped in copper radiates \(Y \approx 0.025/(2\times26.5) = 4.7\times10^{-4}\) of its energy: 1 mA (25 W of beam) makes about 12 mW of X-rays up to 25 keV. That is little power but a great deal of radiation, and it is why any tube run at kilovolts needs shielding and a dosimeter. A 10 MeV electron linac on a tungsten target radiates \(Y = 1 - 1.064\ln1.94 = 0.29\) of its beam power: 1 mA gives about 3 kW of X-rays, the basis of industrial radiography and sterilisation (and of the DARHT radiographs in Dual use).

8. Radiation protection

Beams that are lost make prompt radiation while the beam is on, and they activate the material they strike (nuclear reactions turn some of its atoms into radioactive isotopes), which then radiates during maintenance. Protection rests on three things: areas classified by dose rate with access control, interlocks that stop the beam if an area is entered, and shielding.

Shielding. A barrier is specified by its tenth-value layer (TVL), the thickness that reduces the dose by ten. To reduce an unshielded dose \(D\) to a design limit \(P\) needs

\[ d = \text{TVL}\times\log_{10}(D/P) . \]

Worked number. The IAEA’s example for a cobalt-60 therapy unit (STI/PUB/1296, 2008, annex XV) finds 10 Gy per week at the control console without shielding against a constraint of 0.1 mSv per week, a factor of \(10^5\) (for photons 1 Gy of absorbed dose counts as 1 Sv), five TVLs. With a TVL of 23 cm in concrete of 2.35 g/cm\(^3\), the wall must be \(5\times23 = 115\) cm. For a high-energy hadron machine the same logic holds, but the neutrons from lost protons dominate and the calculation is done with Monte Carlo codes such as FLUKA.

Worked number (losses). The principle behind every number is to keep doses as low as reasonably achievable, not only below the limit.

How it was researched and built

Small groups, and what made them work

Lawrence’s laboratory. On 26 August 1931 the University of California gave Lawrence an old engineering building, which became the Radiation Laboratory. Its method was to build, run and rebuild: the 11-inch cyclotron’s amplification stuck at about seventy-five steps until thin iron shims corrected its field, and it reached 1.22 MeV on 9 January 1932. The magnet for the 27-inch came from the Federal Telegraph Company, a derelict Poulsen-arc magnet of about 80 tons valued at USD 25 000; rebuilding, moving and setting it up cost about USD 12 000, paid by the Research Corporation, the Chemical Foundation and the university president. The 60-inch Crocker cyclotron of 1939 had a 220-ton magnet that cost USD 31 546; through September 1939 the cyclotron had cost about USD 63 500 and the labour USD 20 750. Its first paper carried eight names. Medicine paid much of the way: radioisotopes and neutrons for John Lawrence’s patients (chapter 10). What made it work was a leader who found money and people, a single shared machine on which students learned everything, and the habit of running before the design was final.

Cockcroft and Walton. Four years from Gamow’s tunnelling calculation to the split atom. The first apparatus of 1929 to 1930 gave several microamperes at about 280 kV, but they looked for gamma rays, not alphas, and found nothing. The second, of 1932, had a fourfold cascade, rectifiers and a two-section tube built of straight glass cylinders, and oil diffusion pumps of the kind C. R. Burch had developed at Metropolitan-Vickers; it held up to 690 kV, giving protons of about 710 keV, and this time they looked for alphas with a zinc sulphide screen (chapter 5). Cockcroft had trained as an electrical engineer at Metropolitan-Vickers, and the group used industrial practice where it could. What made it work was a calculation that set a reachable target (hundreds of kilovolts, not millions) and a small team that could change the apparatus quickly.

Kerst’s betatron. Kerst analysed the orbits, injection and every effect he could think of before building anything, then designed the magnet, vacuum system and power supply in detail. His biographers call it the first accelerator built from a careful scientific analysis and a completely engineered design. It worked on 15 July 1940 at 2.3 MeV. With General Electric’s engineers he then designed 20 and 100 MeV machines (chapter 11). Here the method was the reverse of Berkeley’s: the theory first, then a machine that worked as designed.

Big projects use both. The analysis comes first, as with Kerst; the prototypes and test stands are the Berkeley habit kept on purpose, so that the build-and-rebuild happens on one magnet rather than on a thousand.

The life of a machine

Figure 29.8. The life of a machine, in the order of the paragraphs below, with the LHC’s dates from this chapter in red. The first row decides what to build, the second builds it, the third runs it; upgrades send a machine back into operation, sometimes more than once. Research, in theory, codes and test facilities, runs alongside every stage.

The physics case and the parameter list. A user community states what it needs; the accelerator designers turn it into energy, intensity or luminosity, emittance and duty factor, and test whether known technology can give them. For a frontier collider this is done in a strategy process: the HL-LHC began as a design study, and became CERN’s major construction project after the CERN Council approved the updated European Strategy for Particle Physics on 30 May 2013, which made the full exploitation of the LHC Europe’s top priority, and put its budget into the Medium Term Plan in June 2014 (Apollinari and others).

The conceptual design report (CDR). A CDR shows that a consistent set of parameters exists and that nothing in it is impossible: the lattice, the main systems, a first cost and schedule, and the list of research and development still needed. It is reviewed by outside experts. The FCC’s feasibility study of 2025 is of this kind, at the scale of a 90.7 km tunnel.

The technical design report (TDR). A TDR fixes the design enough to build it: every system specified, prototypes tested, the cost estimated item by item with contingency, the schedule planned to its critical path, and the risks listed with their mitigations. Shiltsev and Zimmermann judged that the CLIC proposal needed about ten years of research to reach a comprehensive TDR, and the 16 T hadron colliders about twice that.

Prototypes and test stands. Every new kind of component is built and tested before the series is ordered: short and then full-length magnet models, cold-tested and measured; superconducting cavities in vertical tests, then in cryomodules; ion sources on a test stand with an emittance scanner (chapter 7); kickers, power converters and instrumentation. For the LHC, CERN built a magnet test station of up to twelve benches with a 6 kW refrigerator at 4.5 K, 120 W of pumping at 1.8 K and a nitrogen precooler, and assembled strings of prototype magnets in the tunnel’s real configuration (Lebrun). ISABELLE’s lesson is in this step: one good prototype is not a production line.

Approval. Governments approve a project with a budget and a schedule. A collider usually operates for many years once built: on average 13 years for the colliders of Shiltsev and Zimmermann’s table. Their judgement of colliders, the frontier hadron colliders above all, is plain: they are costly, “often at the brink of financial and political affordability”, and therefore at risk.

Civil engineering. Tunnels, shafts, caverns and surface buildings are the largest single item for a new site: 44 per cent of FCC-ee’s cost. For the HL-LHC, two new underground structures were dug near ATLAS and CMS, mostly during Long Shutdown 2, while the LHC was off, finished by the end of 2022, with surface buildings handed over in January 2023 (Brüning and Zerlauth).

Survey, installation and interconnection. The beam line is marked on the floor from the optics files (MAD-X sequences at CERN, the computer description of every element and its position; see the codes below), the jacks are placed and checked, components are installed and pre-aligned in the geodetic frame, interconnected, and then smoothed (section 3). Installation is logistics: the order in which thousands of heavy objects move through a few shafts along a narrow tunnel.

Hardware commissioning. Each system is tested alone, then in combination, without beam (section 4). It ends when the whole machine has been powered to the planned energy.

Beam commissioning. Threading, the first turn, the closed orbit, tune and chromaticity, RF capture, optics measurement and correction, then the ramp, the squeeze, the collimators and machine protection (the interlocks and beam dump that remove the beam safely before it can damage anything), and only then more intensity (sections 5 and 6). At the LHC in 2009 about four weeks were planned to first collisions with low-intensity, unsqueezed beams (Lamont 2009).

Operation. Physics is scheduled in runs between shutdowns, with machine-development periods in which accelerator physicists have the beam for experiments, and technical stops for maintenance. The LHC uses about 120 MW of electrical power, about half of CERN’s 230 MW (Shiltsev and Zimmermann).

Upgrades. An upgrade replaces what limits performance or what wears out. At the LHC the quadrupoles next to ATLAS and CMS accumulate radiation damage; the HL-LHC replaces them with larger-aperture Nb\(_3\)Sn magnets, adds crab cavities (RF cavities that tilt the bunches so they meet head-on despite the crossing angle, chapter 23) among other new systems, and raises the goal to 3000 fb\(^{-1}\), a tenfold increase, by the early 2040s. The nominal LHC run was projected to end in mid-2026 (Brüning and Zerlauth), when Long Shutdown 3 was to begin.

Decommissioning. The machine is switched off, the activated parts identified by radiation survey and simulation, and the rest removed and recycled or disposed of. The four LEP experiments were dismantled, with the machine, to make way for the LHC: teams of 10 to 15 people each, an overall budget of about 3 million CHF in 2000 prices, and roughly 10 000 tonnes of return-yoke steel sold (FCC study, section on LEP experience).

Failures and their lessons

  • ISABELLE (1974 to 1983). Funded before its magnets were proven, and production failed where the prototype had worked. Lesson: series production is a separate development, and a project’s schedule must not run ahead of its key technology. The magnet work done for ISABELLE, UNK and the SSC nevertheless lowered the cost of collider energy for everyone who came after (Shiltsev and Zimmermann).
  • The SSC (1987 to 1993). The cost nearly doubled in six years while the political support thinned. Lesson: a correlated growth of cost erodes confidence faster than any single technical problem; contingency and honest estimates are part of the physics case.
  • The LHC splice (2008). A joint of 220 nΩ among some ten thousand, in a system that was not instrumented to see it. The protection systems behaved as designed, but the busbar protection and the pressure relief had not been designed for this failure. Lesson: the failure that matters may be in the humblest component; every joint must be measured, and the protection must cover the maximum credible incident, which was then defined as the rupture of all three helium lines in an interconnect with a peak discharge of 40 kg/s (Lamont 2009).
  • A second axis that broke down (2003 to 2008). The second axis of the DARHT radiographic facility at Los Alamos was found unusable on completion in 2003 because of electrical breakdown, and needed an extensive redesign and rebuild finished in 2008; the Handbook of Accelerator Physics and Engineering (section 1.6.9) records unforeseen problems during construction with the ferromagnetic cores and with how voltage divided within the induction cavities. Lesson: high voltage at scale must be proved on full-size prototypes (chapter 4).
  • Reliability as a design choice. Fermilab’s 750 kV Cockcroft-Walton injectors were called in 2009 a liability and a large source of linac downtime, and were replaced with an RFQ in 2012 (chapter 5). Lesson: the old parts of a complex set the availability of the new ones; Brüning and Zerlauth note that LHC availability is now dominated by its injectors and by long stops of the cryogenics.

Pre-requisite (continued): Symplectic matrices

Pre-requisite. This section shows the two ways a code writes a nonlinear map: as a Taylor series, which stops being symplectic when it is cut off, and as a product of Lie transformations, which stays symplectic however it is cut off. The next section, on how accelerator research is done, names both. Taught from the Handbook of Accelerator Physics and Engineering, sections 2.3.3 (Taylor map, Hénon map) and 2.3.4 (Lie algebraic methods), both by A. Dragt; Tong, Classical Mechanics, sections 10.3 (Poisson brackets) and 10.4.1 (infinitesimal canonical transformations); and Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §42 (Poisson brackets).

Reminder: chapter 16 defined a symplectic matrix by \(M^T\mathbf{S}M = \mathbf{S}\), which in one plane is \(\det M = 1\), and chapter 18 applied the same condition to the Jacobian of a nonlinear map at every point and showed that drifts and kicks composed stay symplectic. Chapter 21 defined the Poisson bracket \(\lbrace f, g\rbrace = \partial_xf\,\partial_pg - \partial_pf\,\partial_xg\), which is Tong’s convention and the one used here; Landau’s bracket in §42 has the opposite sign, so every generator below would change sign in his notation. In this section \(x\) is a position and \(p\) its conjugate momentum, which for a beam is close to \(x'\).

The Taylor map. Any element, or a whole turn, sends initial coordinates \(\mathbf{z}_i\) to final ones \(\mathbf{z}_f\). Near the design orbit the final coordinates can be expanded in powers of the initial ones:

\[ z_{f,j} = \sum_kR_{jk}z_{i,k} + \sum_{k,l}T_{jkl}z_{i,k}z_{i,l} + \sum_{k,l,m}U_{jklm}z_{i,k}z_{i,l}z_{i,m} + \cdots \]

This is the Taylor series of F1, section 8 in several variables at once: \(\mathbf{z}\) lists the coordinates (\(x\), \(x'\), and in general \(y\), \(y'\) and the longitudinal pair), \(z_{f,j}\) is the \(j\)th final coordinate, and the indices \(k\), \(l\), \(m\) run over the initial ones. \(R\) is the transfer matrix of the earlier chapters; \(T\) holds the second-order terms (the aberrations of sextupoles and of chromatic focusing); \(U\) the third-order ones. This is the form in which codes print maps, and it is convenient: maps can be multiplied by substituting one series into another. But a code must stop somewhere, at a degree \(D\). The symplectic condition ties the coefficients together by nonlinear relations, and cutting the series off breaks those ties. A truncated Taylor map is in general not symplectic (Handbook, sections 2.3.3 and 2.3.4).

At work. Take the simplest nonlinear ring, the Hénon map of the Handbook: in normalised coordinates (chapter 18, scaled so that the sextupole’s strength is 1), a thin sextupole kick \(p \to p + x^2\) followed by a rotation by the phase advance \(\mu\) (here \(2\pi\times0.21\)). One turn of it is exactly symplectic; its Jacobian, the matrix of partial derivatives \(\partial(x_f, p_f)/\partial(x_i, p_i)\), which acts on small displacements as a transfer matrix does, has determinant 1 everywhere, so small areas of phase space are kept. Two turns make a polynomial of degree 4. Keep only its terms up to degree 2, as a code working to second order would. At \((x, p) = (0.1, 0)\) the Jacobian of this truncated two-turn map has determinant 1.0093, and at \((0.05, 0)\) 1.0023: it creates phase-space area. Started at amplitude 0.05 and iterated, the exact two-turn map stays at radius 0.050 (no more than 0.0506 in 20 000 iterations), while the truncated one has grown to 0.076 after 1000 iterations and runs away after 1791. Keeping degree 3 helps (determinant 1.0036 at \((0.1, 0)\)), but a particle started at 0.1 still runs away after 13 966 iterations, while the exact map keeps it within 0.102. A tracking code that did this would report a dynamic aperture (the largest amplitude that survives many turns, chapter 18) that is not there.

Lie operators. For any function \(f(x, p)\) on phase space, define an operator \(:f:\) that acts on any other function \(g\) by taking the bracket:

\[ :f:\,g = \lbrace f, g\rbrace, \qquad :f:^2g = \lbrace f, \lbrace f, g\rbrace\rbrace, \quad\ldots \]

and the Lie transformation as the exponential series of that operator,

\[ e^{:f:}g = g + \lbrace f, g\rbrace + \frac{1}{2!}\lbrace f, \lbrace f, g\rbrace\rbrace + \frac{1}{3!}\lbrace f, \lbrace f, \lbrace f, g\rbrace\rbrace\rbrace + \cdots \]

Applied to \(g = x\) and \(g = p\), it gives the new coordinates: a map. The function \(f\) is its generator.

Examples. Take \(f\) depending on \(x\) only. Then \(\lbrace f, x\rbrace = 0\) and \(\lbrace f, p\rbrace = \partial f/\partial x\), which again depends on \(x\) only, so \(\lbrace f, \lbrace f, p\rbrace\rbrace = 0\) and the series stops after one term:

\[ e^{:f(x):}:\quad x \to x, \qquad p \to p + \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} . \]

That is a thin kick. With \(f = -kx^2/2\) it is a thin quadrupole, \(p \to p - kx\), with \(k\) (m\(^{-1}\)) the inverse focal length; with \(f = Sx^3/3\), the thin sextupole kick \(p \to p + Sx^2\) of the Hénon map. In the same way \(f = -Lp^2/2\) gives \(\lbrace f, x\rbrace = -\partial f/\partial p = Lp\) and then zero, so \(x \to x + Lp\), \(p \to p\): a drift of length \(L\).

A rotation needs the whole series. Take \(f = -\mu(x^2 + p^2)/2\). Then \(\lbrace f, x\rbrace = \mu p\) and \(\lbrace f, p\rbrace = -\mu x\), so each bracket turns \(x\) into \(\mu p\) and \(p\) into \(-\mu x\). The series for \(x\) is

\[ x + \mu p - \frac{\mu^2}{2!}x - \frac{\mu^3}{3!}p + \frac{\mu^4}{4!}x + \cdots = x\cos\mu + p\sin\mu , \]

because the terms in \(x\) are the Taylor series \(\cos\mu = 1 - \mu^2/2! + \mu^4/4! - \cdots\) and those in \(p\) are \(\sin\mu = \mu - \mu^3/3! + \cdots\) (F1, section 8); and for \(p\) it gives \(-x\sin\mu + p\cos\mu\): a rotation by the phase advance \(\mu\), the linear one-turn map in normalised coordinates. The Hénon map is \(e^{:-\mu(x^2+p^2)/2:}\) and \(e^{:Sx^3/3:}\) applied in turn.

Why a Lie transformation is symplectic. Put a parameter \(\tau\) in front: \(g(\tau) = e^{\tau:f:}g\). Differentiating the series term by term gives

\[ \frac{dg}{d\tau} = \lbrace f, g\rbrace = \lbrace g, -f\rbrace . \]

By chapter 21, \(dg/dt = \lbrace g, H\rbrace\) is how any quantity moves under a Hamiltonian \(H\). So \(e^{\tau:f:}\) is the motion under the Hamiltonian \(H = -f\) for a “time” \(\tau\), and the map is that motion at \(\tau = 1\) (Tong’s infinitesimal canonical transformations, section 10.4.1, are the first term of this series). The motion under any Hamiltonian is symplectic (chapters 16 and 18). Hence every Lie transformation is symplectic, and so is every product of them.

This is what makes the Lie form useful. Dragt and Finn showed that any symplectic map near the design orbit can be written as a product of factors \(e^{:f_2:}e^{:f_3:}e^{:f_4:}\cdots\), with \(f_m\) a polynomial of degree \(m\): \(f_2\) gives the linear part, \(f_3\) the second-order aberrations, and so on. Cutting the product off after \(f_4\), say, gives a map that is wrong at high order but still exactly symplectic, provided each factor is evaluated by a symplectic method (Handbook, section 2.3.4). One truncates the generators, not the map. Codes such as MaryLie (Dragt) and the Polymorphic Tracking Code inside MAD-X work in this spirit; the drift-and-kick integrators of chapter 18 are the simplest case, a product of factors each of whose series stops.

Exercise L1. Find the map \(e^{:f:}\) for \(f = -kx^2/2\), write it as a matrix and check that it is symplectic.

Answer

\(\lbrace f, p\rbrace = \partial f/\partial x = -kx\), and the next bracket is zero. So \(x \to x\), \(p \to p - kx\): the matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ -k & 1\end{pmatrix}\), a thin lens of focal length \(1/k\), with determinant 1.

Exercise L2. A thin octupole has \(f = Ox^4/4\). Find its map and the determinant of its Jacobian at any point.

Answer

\(p \to p + Ox^3\), \(x \to x\). Jacobian \(\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 3Ox^2 & 1\end{pmatrix}\), determinant exactly 1 at every point, not only near the axis.

Exercise L3. The one-turn Hénon map is exactly symplectic, and the two-turn map is its square. Why is the two-turn map’s Taylor series, cut off at degree 2, not symplectic, when its first two degrees are computed exactly?

Answer

The determinant of the full two-turn Jacobian is 1 only because terms of all degrees, up to 4, cancel one another’s contributions. Dropping degrees 3 and 4 leaves terms of degree 2 in the determinant with nothing to cancel them, so the determinant is \(1 + O(x^2)\): 1.0093 at \(x = 0.1\). The Lie form avoids this because each factor is symplectic on its own.

Pre-requisite (continued): Hamiltonian perturbation theory, resonances, KAM and chaos

Pre-requisite. This section explains the idea that IOTA was built to test: why the nonlinear magnets of an ordinary ring cannot give a large spread of tunes without resonances and chaos, and how Danilov and Nagaitsev made a strongly nonlinear lattice integrable, by first removing the clock from the nonlinear motion so that its Hamiltonian is conserved, and then finding a second invariant. The test-facilities paragraph of the next section uses it. Taught from Tong, Classical Mechanics, sections 10.4.2 (a quantity whose bracket with the Hamiltonian vanishes is conserved) and 10.5.2 (integrable systems); the Handbook of Accelerator Physics and Engineering, section 2.3.11 (the Integrable Optics Test Accelerator, by S. Nagaitsev and A. Valishev); and V. Danilov and S. Nagaitsev, Phys. Rev. ST Accel. Beams 13, 084002 (2010).

Reminder: chapter 12 showed that a nonlinear field repeated each turn drives resonances, which make islands; that the KAM theorem keeps most invariant tori when the disturbance is small; and that where resonances overlap the motion becomes chaotic. Chapter 18 applied this to sextupoles and the dynamic aperture.

Why a spread of tunes is wanted. If every particle of a beam oscillates at the same tune, a small coherent oscillation of the whole beam, driven by its own wake fields, keeps its shape and can grow. If the tunes are spread, the particles drift out of step and the coherent motion dephases before it can grow. This is Landau damping, met for synchrotron oscillations in chapter 19 and treated in chapter 22. The usual tool is an octupole, whose field makes the tune depend on amplitude. But an octupole sits at a few places round the ring, so its effect switches on and off with \(s\), and every harmonic of that switching can meet a resonance \(mQ_x + nQ_y = p\). Strengthen the octupoles to widen the tune spread, and the islands grow and overlap: the spread one can use is limited by the chaos it brings.

Counting invariants. One degree of freedom with a Hamiltonian that does not depend on the clock is always regular: \(H\) is conserved, and each particle stays on its level curve (chapter 21). Two degrees of freedom, \(x\) and \(y\), need two conserved quantities, \(I_1\) and \(I_2\), whose Poisson bracket \(\lbrace I_1, I_2\rbrace\) is zero. Then the motion lies on two-dimensional tori and cannot be chaotic; the system is integrable (Tong, section 10.5.2). With only \(H\) conserved, a particle wanders over a three-dimensional energy surface, and chaos is possible. In an ordinary ring the situation is worse: the Hamiltonian depends on \(s\), because magnets come and go, so not even \(H\) is conserved. Linear motion is rescued by the Courant-Snyder invariants of chapter 16, one per plane; any nonlinear magnet breaks them.

Step 1: take the clock out of the nonlinear motion. Danilov and Nagaitsev place the nonlinear element in a straight section where the linear focusing is round, \(\beta_x = \beta_y = \beta(s)\). In the normalised coordinates of chapter 16, \(u = x/\sqrt{\beta}\) with conjugate momentum \(v\), and the same for \(y\), with the betatron phase \(\psi\) as the clock (\(d\psi/ds = 1/\beta\)), the linear motion is a uniform rotation with Hamiltonian

\[ H_N = \frac{1}{2}\left(u^2 + v^2 + u_y^2 + v_y^2\right), \]

which does not depend on \(\psi\). Now add a thin-lens potential \(V(x, y, s)\) (dimensionless: the field’s potential divided by the rigidity), which kicks the slope at the rate \(dx'/ds = -\partial V/\partial x\). The normalised momentum is \(v = (\alpha x + \beta x')/\sqrt{\beta}\) (chapter 18), \(\sqrt{\beta}\) times the slope plus a term in \(x\); a kick changes only the slope, so over \(ds = \beta\,d\psi\) it changes \(v\) by

\[ dv = -\sqrt{\beta}\,\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\,\beta\,d\psi = -\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\Big[\beta(s)\,V(\sqrt{\beta}u, \sqrt{\beta}u_y, s)\Big]\,d\psi , \]

using \(\partial/\partial u = \sqrt{\beta}\,\partial/\partial x\). So the potential enters the normalised Hamiltonian as \(\beta(s)V(\sqrt{\beta}u, \sqrt{\beta}u_y, s)\), and in general this depends on the clock through \(\beta(s)\). Danilov and Nagaitsev’s choice is to build the magnet so that

\[ V(x, y, s) = \frac{1}{\beta(s)}\,U\!\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{\beta(s)}}, \frac{y}{\sqrt{\beta(s)}}\right) \]

for a fixed function \(U\). Then the added term is just \(U(u, u_y)\), with no \(\psi\) in it, and \(H_N + U\) is conserved through the whole insert. The magnet’s strength must fall as \(1/\beta\) along the insert and its transverse scale grow as \(\sqrt{\beta}\). The rest of the ring is linear and round, and is built so that, in normalised coordinates, it brings each particle back to the entrance of the insert as if nothing had happened: a phase advance of a whole multiple of \(\pi\) with the \(\beta\) function matched. (An odd multiple reverses the signs of \(u\), \(v\), \(u_y\) and \(v_y\), which leaves \(H_N + U\) unchanged when \(U\) is symmetric under \((u, u_y) \to (-u, -u_y)\), as IOTA’s is.) IOTA calls this part the T-insert; it fills 95 per cent of the ring (Handbook, section 2.3.11). Turn after turn, \(H_N + U\) is then an exact invariant.

At work. Take a nonlinear insert 1.8 m long with a phase advance of 0.3 of a turn in both planes (round figures of IOTA’s scale, my choice for the arithmetic). In a drift with a waist at its centre, \(\beta(s) = \beta^* + s^2/\beta^*\) (as for the waist at a collision point, chapter 23), and the phase advance across it is

\[ \Delta\psi = \int_{-L/2}^{L/2}\frac{ds}{\beta^* + s^2/\beta^*} = 2\arctan\frac{L}{2\beta^*} , \]

since \(d\psi/ds = 1/\beta\); the integral follows from substituting \(s = \beta^*\tan u\), which makes \(ds = \beta^*(1 + \tan^2u)\,du\) and \(\beta = \beta^*(1 + \tan^2u)\), so the integrand becomes plain \(du\). Setting \(\Delta\psi = 0.6\pi\) gives \(\beta^* = 0.9/\tan(0.3\pi) = 0.654\) m at the centre and \(\beta = 0.654 + 0.9^2/0.654 = 1.893\) m at the ends. So the magnet must be \(1.893/0.654 = 2.9\) times stronger at the centre than at the ends, and its pattern \(\sqrt{2.9} = 1.7\) times narrower. A continuous taper is hard to build; IOTA approximates it by a string of short pieces of constant cross-section (Handbook, section 2.3.11).

Step 2: a second invariant. One invariant is not enough for two planes. The second comes from the shape of \(U\).

  • If \(U\) depends only on \(u^2 + u_y^2\), the problem is unchanged by rotating the \((u, u_y)\) plane, and the corresponding conserved quantity is the angular momentum \(L = uv_y - u_yv\): its bracket with \(H_N + U\) vanishes (Tong, section 10.4.2, the inverse of Noether’s theorem, which says that every symmetry of a Hamiltonian, here rotation, comes with a conserved quantity, here angular momentum). But a static magnetic field in vacuum cannot make such a potential: its potential obeys Laplace’s equation in \((x, y)\), \(\partial^2U/\partial x^2 + \partial^2U/\partial y^2 = 0\) (F2, section 12), and the only round solutions are a constant and \(\ln r\). A round nonlinear lens must therefore be made of charge, which IOTA does with an electron lens: a low-energy electron beam whose own space-charge field acts on the circulating beam.
  • For a magnet, Danilov and Nagaitsev found potentials that do obey Laplace’s equation and separate in elliptic coordinates, giving a second invariant quadratic in the momenta. This is the special nonlinear magnet of IOTA. Its form is beyond this course; the point is that it exists.

With two invariants the motion lies on tori at every amplitude. The nonlinearity can then be made strong, and the tune can change with amplitude by a large amount, without islands or chaos from the main nonlinear element. That was IOTA’s aim: a tune spread of at least 0.25, large compared with what octupoles in a conventional ring can give before resonances limit them. In practice the T-insert’s chromatic and higher-order errors break the symmetry a little, and IOTA’s experiments measure how much the scheme tolerates (Handbook, section 2.3.11).

Exercise I1. A nonlinear insert is 2 m long with a phase advance of a quarter turn. Find \(\beta^*\) at its centre and \(\beta\) at its ends, and how much the magnet’s strength and aperture must change along it.

Answer

\(2\arctan(1/\beta^*) = \pi/2\) gives \(\beta^* = 1\) m. At the ends \(\beta = 1 + 1^2/1 = 2\) m. Strength \(\propto 1/\beta\): twice as strong at the centre. Transverse scale \(\propto\sqrt{\beta}\): \(\sqrt{2} = 1.41\) times wider at the ends.

Exercise I2. Show that a round potential \(U(r^2)\), with \(r^2 = u^2 + u_y^2\), conserves \(L = uv_y - u_yv\).

Answer

\(\lbrace L, U\rbrace = \partial_uL\,\partial_vU - \partial_vL\,\partial_uU + \partial_{u_y}L\,\partial_{v_y}U - \partial_{v_y}L\,\partial_{u_y}U\). \(U\) has no momenta, so only \(-\partial_vL\,\partial_uU - \partial_{v_y}L\,\partial_{u_y}U = -(-u_y)(2uU') - (u)(2u_yU') = 0\). The same calculation with \(\tfrac12(u^2 + u_y^2)\) in place of \(U\), and a similar one for the momenta, give \(\lbrace L, H_N\rbrace = 0\).

Exercise I3. Why can an ordinary octupole not provide a round potential \(U \propto r^4\)?

Answer

A vacuum magnetic potential must satisfy \(\partial^2U/\partial x^2 + \partial^2U/\partial y^2 = 0\). For \(U = r^4 = (x^2 + y^2)^2\) the Laplacian is \(16r^2 \neq 0\). An octupole’s potential is \(\propto x^4 - 6x^2y^2 + y^4\), the real part of \((x + iy)^4\), which has a Laplacian of zero but is not round: it has four-fold symmetry, conserves no angular momentum, and so gives only one invariant.

How accelerator research is done

Theory and models. A beam is a Hamiltonian system (chapter 16, chapter 19). Theory works with the Hamiltonian and with maps, the transformation of phase-space coordinates through an element or a turn. Linear maps are matrices; nonlinear maps are expanded as Taylor series or Lie transformations, and must be symplectic so that Liouville’s theorem holds in the computation as in nature (chapter 18). Background: Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §40 (Hamilton’s equations), §45 (canonical transformations), §46 (Liouville’s theorem), §49 (adiabatic invariants); Tong, Classical Mechanics, sections 10.1 to 10.6.

Codes and what each is for. No single code does everything; each solves one model well.

Two words in the table: a particle-in-cell code follows many sample particles, each standing for a crowd of real ones, and at each step spreads their charge onto a grid, solves for the field of that charge on the grid, and pushes the particles with it; this is how a beam’s own field (space charge) is included. Wakefields and impedance are the fields a bunch leaves behind in the walls round it, which act on the bunches that follow (chapter 22).

A typical design uses a chain: a field code for the components, an optics code for the lattice, a tracking code with errors for the dynamic aperture, a collective-effects code for instabilities and a Monte Carlo code for losses and shielding. The codes must be benchmarked against each other and against beam.

Beam experiments in machine-development time. Theory and codes are tested in the machine itself: optics are measured from turn-by-turn BPM data, and a beta-beating fit (beta-beating is the error \(\Delta\beta_x/\beta_x\) of the betatron function, which oscillates round the ring; chapter 18) at the LHC in 2008 traced a 90 per cent vertical error to two trim-quadrupole cables swapped between the rings (Wenninger 2009). Experiments are proposed, granted shifts, run with a plan and reported.

Test facilities. Some ideas need a machine of their own.

  • ATF and ATF2 (KEK). A damping ring for low-emittance electrons, and a final-focus test line built like the ILC’s (chapter 25). ATF2 measured a vertical beam size of 41 nm on 10 March 2016, against a design of 37 nm (Okugi).
  • CTF3 (CERN). The CLIC test facility demonstrated drive-beam recombination, high-power RF production and two-beam acceleration (CLIC project implementation plan).
  • FACET (SLAC). In 2014 Litos and others accelerated about 74 pC of a trailing electron bunch by about 1.6 GeV in a plasma at about 4.4 GV/m, with more than 30 per cent energy transfer from the wake at best (17.7 per cent on average) (chapter 27).
  • AWAKE (CERN). In 2018, electrons were accelerated up to 2 GeV in a 10 m rubidium plasma driven by 400 GeV proton bunches from the SPS.
  • IOTA (Fermilab). A 40 m storage ring for protons and electrons (injectors of 70 and 150 MeV/\(c\)), built to test nonlinear integrable optics. Danilov and Nagaitsev (2010) found lattices whose nonlinear motion has two invariants, so that a large tune spread, which damps instabilities, comes without chaotic resonances; IOTA’s goal was a tune spread of at least 0.25 (Antipov and others).

The literature. Physical Review Accelerators and Beams began in 1998 as Physical Review Special Topics - Accelerators and Beams and took its present name in 2016. It is fully open access, free to read and free to publish in, sponsored by more than 40 institutions. The Joint Accelerator Conferences Website (JACoW) publishes the proceedings of some 20 to 30 conference series (19 by its own count, 28 when sub-series are counted separately), among them IPAC (from 2010), LINAC, FEL, SRF, IBIC and ICALEPCS, all free online; most results appear there first. The CERN Accelerator School proceedings, the Yellow Reports, are free on the CERN Document Server and arXiv.

The schools. The CERN Accelerator School was established at the beginning of 1983 and holds general and specialised courses; since 1985 it has also held courses outside Europe with partners such as USPAS, JINR and KEK. The Joint Universities Accelerator School (JUAS), established in 1994, runs two five-week courses each year at Archamps near Geneva, one on theory and one on technology and applications. The US Particle Accelerator School (USPAS) is the American counterpart.

Dual use

Accelerators built for physics serve other ends, some of them military. Lawrence’s cyclotrons made radiosodium and, for John Lawrence’s patients, phosphorus-32 in the 1930s (chapter 10). Kerst built a portable 4 MeV betatron to radiograph unexploded bombs, and at Los Alamos a 20 MeV betatron, whose first pictures of an implosion were taken on 15 January 1945 (chapter 11). The same need led to induction linacs for hydrodynamic testing: the first axis of DARHT, completed in 1999, gave a 60 ns pulse of 2 kA at 20 MeV, whose bremsstrahlung images material during a simulated implosion, as part of the stockpile stewardship programme of the US Department of Energy. Radiography of cargo and castings and the production of medical isotopes are the civilian side of the same technology (chapter 28).

How one person can begin

Research begins with reproducing a known result exactly. Each of these can be done alone, with a computer and the chapters named:

  • A FODO lattice. Write the thin-lens matrices, multiply them round a cell, find \(\cos\mu\) and \(\beta_{x,\max}\), and check against the LHC’s 106.9 m cell and its \(\beta_{x,\max}\) of about 180 m (182.9 m in the model ring of section 5) (chapter 16). Then add random quadrupole offsets and reproduce the amplification factor of section 3 by Monte Carlo.
  • A multiplier. Compute the voltage drop and ripple of a Cockcroft-Walton cascade with \(n\) stages under load and compare with the formula (chapter 5).
  • A cyclotron orbit. Integrate a proton through a field that falls slightly with radius, find the turn at which it falls out of phase, and compare with Lawrence’s numbers (chapter 10).
  • Read PRAB. Pick a paper on a machine in this course, reproduce one figure from its stated parameters, and find where your result differs and why.
  • Build a tube. A cathode-ray tube with a filament, an anode and a phosphor screen (chapter 2), on a vacuum system (chapter 3). Electrons stopped in matter make bremsstrahlung X-rays up to the full accelerating voltage, so any tube run at kilovolts needs shielding, interlocks and a dosimeter, and the rules of section 8 apply to it.

At the bench

This section describes the control room of a large machine and its daily practice. The specific numbers are from the LHC’s first commissioning in 2008, the LHC’s later operation and the PSI cyclotron; the general practice is common to large laboratories.

What the operators watch

The control room shows, on fixed screens, the state of every beam and system:

  • Intensity. Total beam current from DC current transformers (which sense the magnetic field of the circulating beam, chapter 8), and the charge per bunch. The LHC’s 2008 commissioning used single bunches of \(2\) to \(4\times10^9\) protons, a few per cent of a nominal bunch of \(1.15\times10^{11}\), to avoid quenches (Wenninger 2009).
  • Orbit and trajectory. Positions at hundreds of BPMs per ring (516 per LHC ring), as a plot along the machine; the rms orbit and its change since a reference. In 2008 about 1 per cent of LHC BPMs were unavailable.
  • Losses. Beam loss monitors along the ring, plotted against position with their thresholds; each threshold dumps the beam if exceeded. A loss pattern that repeats at one place points to an aperture restriction or a mis-steered orbit.
  • Tune and chromaticity. The tunes, continuously or after a kick, in a tune diagram with the resonance lines (chapter 18); typical LHC collision tunes are 64.31 and 59.32.
  • Vacuum. Pressures by sector, with interlocks that close valves if a sector rises.
  • RF. Cavity voltages and phases, the beam phase, the bunch length, and the mountain-range display of bunch profiles turn by turn (chapter 19).
  • Machine state. Energy, \(\beta^*\), the state of the injectors, the beam dump, the interlocks, the cryogenics and the access system.

Controls, alarms and the logbook

The screens are clients of a control system that connects thousands of devices. Two open systems are widely used:

  • EPICS began in 1988 as the control system of the Ground Test Accelerator at Los Alamos (Bob Dalesio, Jeff Hill and others); Marty Kraimer of Argonne joined in 1989, and it was renamed EPICS and presented at ICALEPCS in 1991. It became freely distributable in 2004. Input/output controllers (IOCs) hold databases of records; each value is a process variable (PV) read or written over the network by name, through Channel Access or pvAccess. It is used at the Advanced Photon Source, LCLS, Diamond, ESS, J-PARC and many others.
  • TANGO was proposed at the ESRF in 1998 and started there in 1999, an object-oriented system built on CORBA, with SOLEIL joining in 2000. ALBA, ELETTRA, DESY, MAX IV and SOLEIL are among the institutes that finance it.

An alarm system classifies faults by urgency and tells the operator what to do. An electronic logbook records every action, observation and fault, with screenshots and the time, so that a later investigation can rebuild the sequence. Every signal is also archived, so that a post-mortem (the analysis after a beam dump or fault, from buffers that every system freezes at that moment) can replay the seconds before a dump.

The shift handover

At the end of a shift the outgoing crew tells the incoming one, in person and in the logbook: the state of the machine and its beams; what was done and why; what is broken, who has been called, and what the workaround is; settings that differ from the reference; the programme for the coming shift; and any access or safety restriction in force. The habit to learn is that anything not written down did not happen.

A commissioning checklist

The LHC’s sequence of 2008 (Wenninger 2009; Lamont 2009) is a model of the order:

  1. Cool-down and hardware commissioning: all circuits powered to the target energy (in 2008, 5.5 TeV, because dipole retraining appeared between 6 and 6.5 TeV).
  2. Injection tests into the first sectors with a pilot bunch, stopped on a collimator: 8 August 2008 into sector 23 of ring 1; 22 August into sector 78 of ring 2.
  3. Polarity checks of correctors from kick-response measurements; dispersion and trajectory checks of the optics; aperture scans. In the first test a 12 mm vertical oscillation caused the first beam-induced quench, from a local loss of about \(4\times10^9\) protons.
  4. Threading sector by sector with collimators closed; first turn.
  5. Circulating beam; RF capture; lifetime.
  6. Closed orbit correction; tune and chromaticity measurement and correction; energy measurement.
  7. Optics measurement and correction of beta-beating to within tolerance (±20 per cent at the LHC).
  8. Beam instrumentation, beam dump and collimation commissioning; then the ramp, the squeeze, and increases of intensity, each preceded by machine-protection tests.

A fault and its investigation

The LHC incident of 19 September 2008 is the classic case (Wenninger 2009; Lamont 2009).

  • The sequence. Beam commissioning stopped on the evening of 12 September after a transformer failure at point 8. Powering tests resumed on circuits not fully commissioned, most of them in sector 34. On the morning of 19 September the main dipole circuit of sector 34, 154 magnets, was ramped towards 9.3 kA (5.5 TeV). At 8.7 kA a resistive voltage appeared in the busbar between a dipole and a quadrupole and grew to 1 V in less than 0.5 s; the power converter tripped and the energy-extraction switch opened (it puts a resistor into the circuit, which turns the stored magnetic energy into heat outside the magnets). No magnet had quenched. Within the first second an arc punctured the helium enclosure. The relief discs opened but could not hold the pressure below 0.15 MPa; the pressure wave displaced magnets and broke floor anchors over about 700 m. About 2 tonnes of helium escaped quickly, triggered oxygen-deficiency detectors and an emergency stop; another 4 tonnes followed, 6 of the sector’s 15 tonnes.
  • The evidence. The post-mortem data showed which systems had acted and when. The cryogenic temperature records showed the earlier 40 mK anomaly at 7 kA in the same cell, which gave the 220 nΩ. A simulation reproduced the incident with a joint lacking solder in the copper stabiliser.
  • The search for others. Calorimetry (finding the heat a bad joint makes from the temperature rise of the helium round it) with about 40 nΩ resolution, nanovoltmeter measurements and quench-protection data averaged hard found magnets with internal splices of 47 and 100 nΩ, which were replaced. Warm measurements found joints whose copper path was also poor: a good joint measures about 12 µΩ at room temperature, and 16 segments showed excess resistances over 30 µΩ; their splices were redone.
  • The repair and the cure. 53 magnets (39 dipoles, 14 short straight sections) removed, cleaned or replaced and retested; beam vacuum cleaned of soot and insulation over most of the arc; a new protection layer reading every interconnection at 0.3 mV instead of a single 1 V threshold; 200 mm relief discs on every dipole cryostat of the four sectors that had been warmed up, reducing the maximum pressure there by a factor of 40 (in the four sectors that stayed cold, spring reliefs on existing ports gave a factor of 10 until they too were refitted); new anchoring. Beam returned on 20 November 2009. The machine ran at 3.5 TeV per beam from 2010 and 4 TeV in 2012, and reached 6.5 TeV in 2015 only after the splices had been consolidated (chapter 17).

Where it stops

The physical limits of a machine are in the earlier chapters: the field of a magnet, the gradient of a cavity, synchrotron radiation, instabilities. The practical limit on what can be built is the one this chapter is about: size, cost and time. At fixed field the circumference grows in proportion to momentum, \(C = 2\pi p/(qBF)\): 50 TeV/\(c\) per beam at 16 T is a bending radius of \(3.3356\times50\,000/16\) m \(= 10.4\) km, 65.5 km of dipoles, and with the LHC’s fill factor of 0.66 a ring of about 99 km. The FCC-ee alone is estimated at about 14 billion CHF for CERN, invested over about 15 years; the FCC-hh at about 18.9 billion CHF, dominated by about 10 billion CHF of collider magnets. Shiltsev and Zimmermann report estimates that magnets of up to 16 T may need at least 15 to 20 years of development. A machine that takes longer to build than a career, at the limit of what governments will pay, is near the end of this road.

There are three ways past. Raise the field and lower its cost per metre, which is the work of high-field and high-temperature superconducting magnets (chapter 17). Accelerate in a different way, with gradients of gigavolts per metre in plasma or dielectric structures, so that the machine is short (chapter 27). Or collide a lighter particle without synchrotron radiation in a linac (chapter 25), or a muon in a ring. Each needs the whole craft of this chapter again: physics case, test facility, prototypes, cost and the patience to commission.

Exercises

1. The faulty LHC splice had 220 nΩ and a good one 0.35 nΩ (Wenninger’s figure; Lamont gives about 0.3 nΩ). Find the heat in each at 7 kA, and their ratio.

Answer

\(P = I^2R\): \(7000^2\times220\times10^{-9} = 10.8\) W; \(7000^2\times0.35\times10^{-9} = 0.017\) W. Ratio 629. The 10.8 W raised the cell’s temperature by up to 40 mK in an hour, which is how the joint was found afterwards.

2. CERN’s annual limits are 1, 6 and 20 mSv for non-designated, supervised and simple controlled areas, whose permanent-workplace dose rates are 0.5, 3 and 10 µSv/h. How many hours a year do the two sets assume?

Answer

\(1\ \text{mSv}/0.5\ \mu\text{Sv/h} = 2000\) h; \(6/0.003 = 2000\) h; \(20/0.01 = 2000\) h. A working year of 2000 hours, about 50 weeks of 40 hours.

3. The HL-LHC is to deliver 250 fb\(^{-1}\) a year, levelled at \(5\times10^{34}\) cm\(^{-2}\) s\(^{-1}\). How many days of levelled collisions is that? If the machine is in collision half of its scheduled time, how many days must be scheduled?

Answer

\(250\ \text{fb}^{-1} = 2.5\times10^{41}\) cm\(^{-2}\). Time \(= 2.5\times10^{41}/5\times10^{34} = 5.0\times10^{6}\) s \(= 57.9\) days. At 50 per cent, 116 days.

4. A ring is built in a plane tangent to the Earth’s level surface at its centre. How far above that surface is its edge for the LHC (\(C = 26\,659\) m) and for the FCC (\(C = 90\,658\) m)? By how much does the plumb line at the edge tilt relative to the centre?

Answer

\(r = C/2\pi\): 4243 m and 14 429 m. \(h = r^2/(2R_E)\) with \(R_E = 6.371\times10^6\) m: 1.41 m and 16.3 m. Tilt \(r/R_E\): 0.67 mrad and 2.26 mrad. Gravity-based levelling must be corrected for this, and for the local shape of the geoid.

5. An LHC arc corrector next to a focusing quadrupole (\(\beta_x = 182.9\) m) gives 80.8 µrad. What is the largest trajectory change it produces downstream at the focusing quadrupoles, and at the defocusing ones (\(\beta_x = 30.9\) m)?

Answer

\(x = \sqrt{\beta_{x,i}\beta_{x,j}}\,\theta\) at the right phase: \(\sqrt{182.9\times182.9}\times80.8\times10^{-6} = 14.8\) mm; \(\sqrt{182.9\times30.9}\times80.8\times10^{-6} = 6.1\) mm.

6. At 450 GeV in the LHC (\(\eta = 3.18\times10^{-4}\), \(f_{RF} = 400.789\) MHz) the RF frequency is raised by 100 Hz and the tune falls by 0.0040. Find \(\delta\) and \(Q'\).

Answer

\(\delta = -(1/\eta)(\Delta f_{RF}/f_{RF}) = -(100/400.789\times10^6)/3.1815\times10^{-4} = -7.84\times10^{-4}\). \(Q' = \Delta Q/\delta = (-0.0040)/(-7.84\times10^{-4}) = 5.1\). Raising the frequency above transition lowers the momentum.

7. How many turns of BPM data are needed for the bare FFT peak to give the tune with an rms error of \(10^{-3}\)?

Answer

\(\sigma_Q = 1/(N\sqrt{12}) = 10^{-3}\) gives \(N = 289\); in practice a power of two, 512 turns. Interpolation with a window reaches the same precision with far fewer turns.

8. A TESLA cavity (1.038 m, \(R/Q = 1036\ \Omega\) linac convention, 1.3 GHz) is tested at 25 MV/m. Find the wall loss for \(Q_0 = 10^{10}\), and the energy decay time if it is critically coupled.

Answer

\(\hat{V} = 25.95\) MV. \(P_c = \hat{V}^2/((R/Q)Q_0) = (2.595\times10^7)^2/(1036\times10^{10}) = 65\) W. Critical coupling: \(Q_L = Q_0/2 = 5\times10^9\), \(\tau_U = Q_L/\omega_{RF} = 5\times10^9/(2\pi\times1.3\times10^9) = 0.61\) s (the field decays with twice that, 1.22 s).

9. Take the FCC-ee cost items 6160, 2840, 4140, 590 and 290 million CHF, each with a 25 per cent standard deviation. Find the contingency needed for an 80 per cent chance of not overrunning if the items are independent, and if they are fully correlated.

Answer

Total 14 020. Independent: \(\sigma_{\text{var}} = 0.25\sqrt{\sum K_i^2} = 1993\); \(0.842\times1993 = 1678\) million CHF, 12 per cent. Correlated: \(\sigma_{\text{var}} = 0.25\times14\,020 = 3505\); contingency 2950 million CHF, 21 per cent. The assumption about correlation nearly doubles the answer.
