In one page

The wall. A weak-focusing synchrotron (chapter 15) holds its beam with a field that falls gently with radius, field index \(0 < n < 1\). Its tunes obey \(Q_x^2 + Q_y^2 = 1\), so both are below one, and a particle’s oscillation is longer than the ring. The orbits of particles with the wrong momentum move in proportion to the radius, and the betatron amplitudes grow with it too. The Bevatron of 1954 (6.2 GeV) needed a beam space of about four square feet and a magnet of about 10,000 tons. A 30 GeV machine built the same way could not be afforded.

The idea. Maxwell’s equations forbid a magnetic lens that focuses in both planes at once: a gradient that focuses horizontally defocuses vertically. But a focusing lens and a defocusing lens of equal strength, set apart, focus in both planes, because the ray is farther from the axis in the focusing lens than in the defocusing one. Alternate strong gradients around the ring and the tunes can be made as large as one likes. Nicholas Christofilos applied for a patent on this in 1950; Courant, Livingston and Snyder at Brookhaven found it again in 1952 and published it.

The equations to remember. Hill’s equation for the horizontal displacement \(x\) (m) against path length \(s\) (m), with bending radius \(\rho\) (m), normalised quadrupole strength \(k\) (m\(^{-2}\)) and momentum deviation \(\delta\):

\[ x'' + K_x(s)\,x = \frac{\delta}{\rho}, \qquad K_x = \frac{1}{\rho^2} + k, \qquad K_y = -k \]

Its solution, with the betatron function \(\beta_x(s)\) (m), the emittance \(\varepsilon\) (m rad) and the dispersion \(D_x\) (m):

\[ x = \sqrt{\varepsilon\,\beta_x}\,\cos(\psi + \psi_0) + D_x\,\delta, \qquad \psi' = \frac{1}{\beta_x}, \qquad Q_x = \frac{1}{2\pi}\oint\frac{ds}{\beta_x} \]

The thin-lens FODO cell of length \(L\) with quadrupoles of focal length \(f\):

\[ \sin\frac{\mu}{2} = \frac{L}{4f}, \qquad \beta_{x,\max} = \frac{L\,(1 + \sin\frac{\mu}{2})}{\sin\mu} \]

The smooth approximation for a ring of mean radius \(R\): \(\beta_x \approx R/Q_x\), \(D_x \approx R/Q_x^2\), momentum compaction \(\alpha_c \approx 1/Q_x^2\), so transition comes at \(\gamma_t \approx Q_x\). The beam size is \(\sigma_x = \sqrt{\varepsilon\beta_x + D_x^2\sigma_\delta^2}\), and the normalised emittance \(\varepsilon_n = \beta\gamma\varepsilon\) is conserved during acceleration.

Numbers to know. The CERN PS: 628.3 m round, 100 combined-function magnets, tunes near 6.2, mean \(\beta_x\) about 16 m, momentum compaction 0.025 to 0.027, so transition at \(\gamma_t \approx 6.1\) to 6.3, about 5 GeV of kinetic energy; its vacuum chamber today is an ellipse 146 mm by 70 mm. A 90° FODO cell has \(\beta_{x,\max} = 1.71\,L\) and \(\beta_{x,\min} = 0.29\,L\). The natural chromaticity of a strong-focusing ring is about \(-Q_x\) (and \(-Q_y\)). In the LHC arcs at 7 TeV, \(\beta_x = 180\) m and \(\varepsilon = 0.5\) nm give an rms beam size of 0.3 mm.

Pre-requisite (continued): Maxwell’s equations and the wave equation

Pre-requisite (continued). Chapter 13 taught Maxwell’s equations for the fields of a cavity, which change direction a thousand million times a second. This section takes the opposite limit, the steady field of a magnet in the vacuum of the beam pipe, where only \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0\) and \(\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = 0\) are left. It then proves the result that the next section, “The wall”, rests on: those two conditions tie the horizontal and vertical gradients together, so that no magnet focuses in both planes at once. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, sections 3.1 (Ampère’s law) and 2.2.6 (Earnshaw’s theorem, the electric cousin); Landau and Lifshitz, The Classical Theory of Fields, section 43; and Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, sections I.2 to I.4.

From four equations to two. Maxwell’s equations for the magnetic field are

\[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0, \qquad \nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{j} + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}{\partial t} \]

with \(\mathbf{B}\) in T, the current density \(\mathbf{j}\) in A/m² and \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) in V/m (F4, section 16; here \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) is the electric field, and \(\nabla\cdot\) and \(\nabla\times\) are the divergence and the curl of F2, sections 10 and 11). The first holds always: there are no magnetic charges, so as much flux leaves any closed surface as enters it. In the beam pipe there is no current, \(\mathbf{j} = 0\) (the beam’s own current is a separate, small field, left to chapter 22). What of the displacement current? A ring magnet’s field changes over a ramp of about a second. The ratio of the displacement term to the curl it would have to balance is of order \((\ell/cT)^2\), with \(\ell\) the size of the magnet and \(T\) the time over which the field changes. The reason, in orders of magnitude: the curl of \(\mathbf{B}\) is about \(B/\ell\). A field \(B\) that changes in a time \(T\) induces, by Faraday’s law, an electric field of about \(\ell B/T\) over the magnet (F4, section 14); that field changes in the same time \(T\), so the displacement term is about \(\mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\ell B/T^2 = \ell B/(c^2T^2)\), using \(\mu_0\varepsilon_0 = 1/c^2\). Divide the two: \((\ell B/c^2T^2)/(B/\ell) = (\ell/cT)^2\). For \(\ell = 1\) m and \(T = 1\) s that is \(1.1\times10^{-17}\). So in the aperture of a magnet, to any accuracy that matters,

\[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0, \qquad \nabla\times\mathbf{B} = 0 \]

The field is static and current-free. Chapter 10 derived the second, one component at a time, by shrinking Ampère’s loop to a small rectangle in the gap.

In components, for a long magnet. Use the coordinates of the chapter: \(x\) horizontal, \(y\) vertical, \(s\) along the beam. In the body of a long magnet the field does not change along \(s\) and has no \(s\) component. Then the two conditions read

\[ \frac{\partial B_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial B_y}{\partial y} = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial B_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial B_x}{\partial y} = 0 \]

The first comes from the flux through a small box, the second from the circulation round a small rectangle in the \((x, y)\) plane. Chapter 7 used exactly this pair to show that \(B_y + iB_x\) is an analytic function of \(x + iy\).

The theorem: no lens for both planes. Near the axis any smooth field can be written to first order as

\[ B_x = a\,x + b\,y, \qquad B_y = c\,x + d\,y \]

with four constant gradients \(a\), \(b\), \(c\), \(d\) in T/m (we take the field on the axis to be zero; a dipole field added to it changes nothing below). Since \(\partial B_x/\partial x = a\), \(\partial B_y/\partial y = d\), \(\partial B_y/\partial x = c\) and \(\partial B_x/\partial y = b\), the divergence condition gives \(a + d = 0\) and the curl condition gives \(c = b\). A particle of charge \(q\) moves along \(s\) with speed \(v\). The magnetic force \(q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\) (F4, section 10), worked out in section 2 of the chapter and drawn in figure 16.3, gives the transverse force

\[ F_x = -q v B_y = -q v\,(c\,x + d\,y), \qquad F_y = q v B_x = q v\,(a\,x + b\,y) \]

A focusing force in \(x\) is one that pulls back in proportion to \(x\), so its strength is \(-\partial F_x/\partial x = q v c\). In \(y\) it is \(-\partial F_y/\partial y = -q v b\). Add them:

\[ -\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial y} = q v\,(c - b) = 0 \]

by the curl condition. Whatever focusing the field gives in one plane it takes away, with the same strength, in the other. Divide by \(pv\), with \(p\) the momentum, to get the focusing constants of Hill’s equation. (Why \(pv\): the force changes the transverse momentum, \(dp_x/dt = F_x\) (F3, section 2); along the path \(ds = v\,dt\), so \(dp_x/ds = F_x/v\); and the slope is \(x' = dx/ds = p_x/p\), so \(x'' = F_x/(pv)\). A force \(F_x = -(\text{strength})\,x\) therefore gives \(x'' + Kx = 0\) with \(K = (\text{strength})/(pv)\).) \(K_x + K_y = 0\) for any static magnetic field along a straight path. The bending of the orbit adds \(1/\rho^2\) to \(K_x\) alone, which is where the chapter’s sum rule \(K_x + K_y = 1/\rho^2\) comes from.

The divergence condition has its own job. It makes the cross terms equal: the \(y\) in \(F_x\) has coefficient \(-qvd = qva\), and the \(x\) in \(F_y\) has coefficient \(qva\). That symmetry means the force is minus the gradient of a single function, a potential for the transverse motion. Section 14 of the chapter returns to this as symplecticity.

Chapter 14 proved Earnshaw’s theorem for static electric fields, and noted that magnetic forces escape its argument because they depend on velocity and are not the gradient of a potential energy. For a beam moving along \(s\) they do not escape this cousin of it: at a fixed speed along \(s\) the transverse magnetic force does act like the gradient of a function, and the curl condition then does for magnets what Laplace’s equation did for charges. As there, a static field can stabilise one direction only at the cost of another.

At work. In the LHC at 7 TeV the rigidity is \(B\rho = p/q = 23{,}349\) T m (for a singly charged particle \(B\rho\) in T m is \(3.3356\) times \(p\) in GeV/\(c\), chapter 1, section 4.1). A main quadrupole of gradient 223 T/m gives \(k = g/B\rho = 0.00955\) m\(^{-2}\) in one plane and \(-0.00955\) m\(^{-2}\) in the other; over its 3.1 m it is a lens of focal length \(\pm 33.8\) m. The curvature term of the dipoles, \(\rho = 2{,}804\) m, is \(1/\rho^2 = 1.27\times10^{-7}\) m\(^{-2}\), 75,000 times smaller. In a strong-focusing ring the shared weak focusing of the orbit is negligible and the quadrupoles do everything. In a weak-focusing ring of 100 m radius, \(1/\rho^2 = 10^{-4}\) m\(^{-2}\) is all there is.

The loophole: round lenses. A solenoid or an electrostatic einzel lens (chapter 2) does focus in both planes. It escapes the theorem, which assumed a field with no \(s\) component and no change along \(s\): a solenoid’s field points along \(s\) and changes at its ends. Its focusing comes at second order in the field. For a solenoid \(K = [B_s/(2B\rho)]^2\) in both planes (chapter 2, section 9, where the electron lenses are worked out). With 5 T at 7 TeV that is \(1.1\times10^{-8}\) m\(^{-2}\), nearly a million times weaker than the quadrupole. Round lenses serve slow beams; fast beams need quadrupoles, and quadrupoles need alternation.

Exercise W1. A measured field near the axis of a magnet is \(B_x = 0.4y\) and \(B_y = 0.5 + 0.4x\) (T, with \(x\), \(y\) in m). Check it against Maxwell’s equations, and name its parts.

Answer

\(\partial B_x/\partial x + \partial B_y/\partial y = 0 + 0 = 0\) and \(\partial B_y/\partial x - \partial B_x/\partial y = 0.4 - 0.4 = 0\). Both hold. It is a uniform dipole field of 0.5 T plus a quadrupole of gradient 0.4 T/m: a combined-function magnet, like those of the PS.

Exercise W2. Someone proposes a magnet with \(B_y = g\,x\) and \(B_x = 0\), to focus horizontally and leave the vertical motion alone. What is wrong?

Answer

\(\partial B_y/\partial x - \partial B_x/\partial y = g \ne 0\), so the field has a curl. In the empty beam pipe that would need a current density \(j_s = g/\mu_0\) flowing through the beam’s path: for \(g = 10\) T/m, about \(8\times10^{6}\) A/m². Without that current, a field \(B_y = gx\) always comes with \(B_x = gy\), and the vertical plane is defocused.

Exercise W3. Show that a field with \(B_x = 0.4y\) and \(B_y = 0.2x\) cannot exist in a current-free gap, and say what force law it would give.

Answer

The curl is \(0.2 - 0.4 = -0.2\) T/m, not zero. It would give \(F_x = -0.2\,qvx\) and \(F_y = 0.4\,qvy\): focusing of strength 0.2 in \(x\) and defocusing of strength 0.4 in \(y\), a sum of \(-0.2\,qv\) instead of zero. The sum is what the curl measures, and in a current-free gap it must vanish.

The wall

By 1950 the synchrotron of chapter 15 was the way to high energy. A ring magnet with a rising field keeps the orbit radius fixed while radio-frequency gaps add energy, and phase stability (chapter 12) keeps the particles in step. Brookhaven’s Cosmotron reached 3.3 GeV in 1953. Berkeley’s Bevatron, built for 6 GeV, began operating in 1954 and reached 6.2 GeV.

Both held the beam the way Kerst’s betatron did (chapter 11). The guide field falls slowly with radius, \(B_y \propto r^{-n}\), with \(0 < n < 1\). A displaced particle then oscillates about the design orbit, with \(Q_x = \sqrt{1-n}\) oscillations per turn horizontally and \(Q_y = \sqrt{n}\) vertically (chapter 11, section 4; chapter 15, section 5). These numbers of oscillations per turn are the tunes, and the oscillations themselves are called betatron oscillations after the machine in which they were first worked out. The weakness is in the sum: \(Q_x^2 + Q_y^2 = 1\). Whatever \(n\) is chosen, both tunes are below one, so one oscillation is longer than the circumference. For a given emittance (the area the beam fills in the plane of position and slope, chapter 7) the amplitude of a betatron oscillation scales as \(\sqrt{R/Q_x}\) (section 8 shows why), and the orbit of a particle whose momentum is off by a fraction \(\delta\) is displaced by \(R\delta/(1-n)\) (section 10). For a ring of 100 m radius with \(n = 0.5\), a momentum error of one part in a thousand moves the orbit by 0.2 m. Double the momentum at the same field and the radius doubles; the orbit shift doubles with it, and the betatron amplitude grows by \(\sqrt{2}\). The vacuum chamber and the magnet gap had to grow with the ring, and the iron and copper with the square of the gap.

The Bevatron shows where that leads. Its magnet weighed about 10,000 tons and its gap, the beam space, was 1 ft by 4 ft (0.30 m by 1.22 m), four square feet in cross-section (Lawrence and His Laboratory). E. J. N. Wilson’s history of the synchrotron records that the aperture of a weak-focusing machine was hard to estimate, and that a fall-back design for the Bevatron was so large that it drew jokes about accelerating jeeps. The European laboratory being planned in 1952, CERN, was designing a weak-focusing proton synchrotron of about 10 GeV. The Soviet synchrophasotron at Dubna reached 10 GeV in 1957 with weak focusing. Nobody could see how to go much higher.

The obstacle is not a matter of engineering. In the vacuum of the beam pipe a static magnetic field has no curl and no divergence. For a field in the midplane with only a vertical component that varies with \(x\), \(\partial B_x/\partial y = \partial B_y/\partial x\). So the gradient that pushes a particle back towards the orbit horizontally pushes it away vertically, with the same strength. In the physics section this becomes \(K_x + K_y = 1/\rho^2\): the only focusing that acts in both planes at once is the weak focusing of the curved orbit itself, and it is shared between them. That is the sum rule for the tunes.

The way out came from two places. Nicholas Christofilos, an engineer working in Athens, conceived in 1949 a focusing system in which the force alternates in sign along the orbit. He filed a United States patent, “Focussing system for ions and electrons”, on 10 March 1950; it was issued as US 2,736,799 on 28 February 1956. Its claims describe a field whose focusing force varies periodically along the path, so that particles meet alternately converging and diverging forces and are, on balance, focused; they cover closed orbits and straight paths. He also wrote an unpublished report in 1950. In 1949 he had set out the idea in a letter to the Radiation Laboratory at Berkeley, where it was filed without a reply (Coleman). Nothing was published in a journal, and the idea went unnoticed.

The other was at Brookhaven in 1952. Wilson’s account, from the recollections of those involved, goes like this. The Cosmotron’s magnets were C-shaped in cross-section: the iron that carries the flux back from the top pole to the bottom one (the return yoke) stood on one side of the gap only, and the gap was open to the outside of the ring. At high field the iron saturated, that is, it approached the limit near 2 T beyond which it can carry no more flux (chapter 15). It then added an extra negative gradient, a field falling off with radius more steeply than designed (a larger field index), and that limited the energy. M. Stanley Livingston proposed to turn some magnets round, with their return yokes facing outward, so that their saturation gradients would cancel (figure 16.1). He was worried about what the alternating gradients would do at low field. Ernest Courant was asked to check and found that, far from harming the focusing, it improved as the alternating part of the gradient grew stronger. Hartland Snyder pointed to the optical analogy of equal converging and diverging lenses. The three published “The strong-focusing synchrotron: a new high energy accelerator” in Physical Review (received 21 August, published 1 December 1952). Their example was a 30 GeV proton machine of 300 ft orbit radius with a magnet aperture of 1 by 2 inches.

Figure 16.1. The Cosmotron’s C-shaped magnets in cross-section, schematic. The yoke that returns the flux stands on one side of the gap only. As built, open to the outside, the saturated iron made the field stronger on the yoke side, inside, so it fell off outward more steeply than designed. Turned round, open to the inside, the same saturation makes the field rise outward. Alternate the two and the gradients alternate: the observation that led to strong focusing.

The physics

1. Two lenses that focus together

Start with light. Describe a ray by its height \(x\) above the axis and its slope \(x' = dx/ds\), with \(s\) the distance along the axis; for the small angles of all lens optics the slope is the angle to the axis in radians. A thin lens of focal length \(f\) changes a ray’s slope by \(-x/f\), where \(x\) is the ray’s distance from the axis: a ray that arrives parallel leaves with slope \(-x/f\) and crosses the axis a distance \(f\) beyond the lens, which is what a focal length means. \(f > 0\) converges, \(f < 0\) diverges. Put a converging lens of focal length \(f_1\) and a diverging lens of focal length \(f_2\) a distance \(d\) apart. A ray enters parallel to the axis at height \(x_0\) (figure 16.2).

After the first lens its slope is \(-x_0/f_1\). Between the lenses it runs straight, so over the distance \(d\) its height changes by \(d\) times its slope, and it reaches the second lens at \(x_1 = x_0 - d\,x_0/f_1 = x_0(1 - d/f_1)\). The second lens adds \(-x_1/f_2\) to the slope, so the final slope is

\[ x'_2 = -\frac{x_0}{f_1} - \frac{x_0}{f_2}\left(1 - \frac{d}{f_1}\right) = -x_0\left(\frac{1}{f_1} + \frac{1}{f_2} - \frac{d}{f_1 f_2}\right) \]

The pair acts as one lens of focal length \(F\) with

\[ \frac{1}{F} = \frac{1}{f_1} + \frac{1}{f_2} - \frac{d}{f_1 f_2} \]

Now take equal and opposite lenses, \(f_1 = f\) and \(f_2 = -f\). The first two terms cancel, and

\[ \frac{1}{F} = \frac{d}{f^2} > 0 \]

The pair converges, whichever lens comes first: swapping them changes \(f\) to \(-f\), and \(f^2\) does not notice. The picture is this. In the converging-then-diverging order, the ray is bent inward first and reaches the diverging lens closer to the axis, where the diverging kick is smaller. In the other order, the diverging lens throws the ray outward, and it reaches the converging lens farther out, where the converging kick is larger. Either way the converging lens wins, by an amount of second order in the lens strength.

Figure 16.2. A ray entering parallel at height \(x_0\) through two thin lenses of focal length \(f = 2\) m and \(-2\) m, 0.5 m apart, computed with the steps above. Top: converging first. The ray is bent inward and reaches the diverging lens at \(0.75\,x_0\), where its outward kick is small; it leaves still converging and crosses the axis 6.0 m beyond the second lens (dashed: where the first lens alone would have brought it, 2 m on). Bottom: diverging first. The ray is thrown out to \(1.25\,x_0\), where the converging lens is stronger; it crosses the axis 10.0 m beyond. Both leave with the same slope, \(-x_0/8\) m: one lens of \(F = 8\) m in either order. The heights are stretched about four times relative to the lengths.

A quadrupole is exactly such a lens: converging in one plane and diverging in the other. A doublet of quadrupoles therefore converges in both planes. For \(f = 2\) m and \(d = 0.5\) m, \(F = f^2/d = 8\) m in both planes: four times weaker than either lens alone, but positive.

Pre-requisite (continued): Two-dimensional magnetic potentials and multipole expansion

Pre-requisite (continued). Chapter 7 wrote a current-free two-dimensional field as the analytic function \(B_y + iB_x = \sum_n C_n(x + iy)^n\) and named its terms dipole, quadrupole, sextupole and octupole. Chapter 14 wrote the magnetic quadrupole’s field as minus the gradient of the scalar potential \(-gxy\). This section develops that potential: it obeys Laplace’s equation, each multipole has one, and the section uses it to find the shape of a quadrupole’s iron poles (hyperbolas), its field at the pole tip and the current its coils must carry. It ends with the normalised strengths and the conventions in which multipole components are quoted. Section 2 of the chapter, the quadrupole field, uses all of it. Taught from Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 1, section II (quadrupoles) and exercise 1.12; the Handbook of Accelerator Physics and Engineering, section 7.2.1 (Mills and Harding on resistive magnets: iron poles as equipotentials, the ideal pole shapes and the quadrupole’s ampere-turns); and Tong, Electromagnetism, section 3.2.1, where the magnetic scalar potential is introduced.

The scalar potential. Chapter 14 called it \(\psi\); the chapter calls it \(V\). In the aperture \(\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = 0\) (the previous pre-requisite). A field with no curl is the gradient of a function, just as the electrostatic field is, so we may write

\[ \mathbf{B} = -\nabla V, \qquad B_x = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}, \qquad B_y = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial y} \]

with \(V\) in T m. Put this into \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0\):

\[ \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial y^2} = 0 \]

Laplace’s equation, the same one that governs the potential between electrodes (chapter 2). Everything learned there carries over, including uniqueness: fix \(V\) on the boundary and the field inside is fixed.

The potential of each multipole. Chapter 7’s expansion and the potential are two faces of one analytic function. Take \(F(w) = C_n w^{n+1}/(n+1)\), with \(w = x + iy\) and \(C_n\) real, and set \(V = -\mathrm{Im}\,F\). Differentiating, \(-\partial V/\partial x = \mathrm{Im}\,F'(w) = \mathrm{Im}(C_n w^n)\) and \(-\partial V/\partial y = \mathrm{Im}(iF') = \mathrm{Re}(C_n w^n)\), which are \(B_x\) and \(B_y\) of chapter 7. In polar coordinates \(w^{n+1} = r^{n+1}e^{i(n+1)\theta}\), so

\[ V_n = -\frac{C_n}{n+1}\,r^{n+1}\sin\big((n+1)\theta\big) \]

The cases that matter:

  • Dipole (\(n = 0\)): \(V = -C_0\,y\), a uniform field \(B_y = C_0\). Equipotentials are horizontal lines; the poles are flat.
  • Quadrupole (\(n = 1\), \(C_1 = g\)): \(V = -\tfrac{g}{2}r^2\sin 2\theta = -g\,x\,y\). This is the potential of section 2 of the chapter.
  • Sextupole (\(n = 2\)): \(V = -\tfrac{C_2}{3}r^3\sin 3\theta = -\tfrac{C_2}{3}(3x^2y - y^3)\), giving \(B_y = C_2(x^2 - y^2)\) and \(B_x = 2C_2xy\).

A \(2(n+1)\)-pole magnet has a potential that changes sign \(2(n+1)\) times round a circle: that is where the alternate north and south poles sit.

Iron poles are equipotentials. Iron with a large relative permeability \(\mu_r\) (thousands, below saturation) carries almost no magnetising field \(H\) inside (\(H = B/\mu_0\mu_r\), in A/m: the part of the field that the coil’s current has to supply; F4, section 15 and chapter 8). Across the iron surface the tangential \(H\) is continuous, so just outside it the tangential \(H\) is also nearly zero: the field leaves the iron at right angles. A surface that the field crosses at right angles is an equipotential. So an iron pole shaped along an equipotential of the wanted potential produces that potential in the aperture, and by uniqueness, nothing else.

For the quadrupole the equipotentials are \(xy = \text{const}\), rectangular hyperbolas with the axes as asymptotes. Let \(a\) be the aperture radius, the distance from the axis to the nearest point of each pole. The pole in the first quadrant has its nearest point at \(r = a\), \(\theta = 45°\), where \(x = y = a/\sqrt{2}\), so its surface is

\[ x\,y = \frac{a^2}{2} \]

and the other three poles are this hyperbola reflected into the other quadrants, alternately north and south. Figure 16.3 draws them, with the field and the forces it gives. Real poles follow the hyperbola near the axis and are cut off at the sides to leave room for the coils; the truncation adds small higher multipoles (12-pole and 20-pole terms for a symmetric quadrupole), which chapter 17 treats.

Figure 16.3. A quadrupole seen along the beam, which comes out of the page, for \(g > 0\). The iron poles follow the hyperbolas \(xy = \pm a^2/2\) near the axis and are cut off at the sides, where the coils (not drawn) sit; their nearest points are at the aperture radius \(a\), on the 45° lines. The potential \(V = -gxy\) is highest on the poles at upper left and lower right, which are therefore north poles: the field (blue arrows, \(B_x = gy\), \(B_y = gx\)) runs from them to the other two. Its size \(gr\) grows in proportion to the distance from the axis, and on the axis it is zero. Black arrows: the force \(q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\) on a positive particle, pointing back towards the axis along \(x\) and away from it along \(y\), with the same strength at the same distance.

Pole-tip field and ampere-turns. The field’s magnitude is \(|B| = g\,r\), the same at every angle (exercise M1 of chapter 7), so at the pole tip

\[ B_{\text{tip}} = g\,a \]

Iron saturates near 2 T. The pole tip is where the field in the aperture is strongest, and the iron of the pole behind it must carry that flux, more densely where the pole narrows; so conventional quadrupoles are designed for a pole-tip field below about 1 T (Lee gives 0.9 T). That limits the gradient: \(g = B_{\text{tip}}/a\), so small apertures give strong gradients.

The coil current follows from Ampère’s law (F4, section 12), $H,dl = $ the current enclosed, round a loop that runs from the axis out to the pole tip along the 45° line, back through the iron, and home along the \(x\) axis. On the 45° line the field is radial with size \(g r\), so that leg contributes \(\int_0^a g\,r\,dr/\mu_0 = g a^2/(2\mu_0)\). In the iron \(H \approx 0\). On the \(x\) axis the field \(B_y = gx\) is at right angles to the path and contributes nothing. The loop encloses the coil of one pole, \(N\) turns each carrying the current \(I\), so the enclosed current is \(NI\), counted in ampere-turns:

\[ N I = \frac{g\,a^2}{2\mu_0} \]

per pole. The same number is the scalar potential of the pole, \(|V| = g a^2/2\), divided by \(\mu_0\).

At work. A conventional quadrupole of 40 mm aperture radius with a 0.9 T pole tip has \(g = 22.5\) T/m and needs \(NI = 22.5 \times 0.040^2/(2\times4\pi\times10^{-7}) = 14{,}300\) ampere-turns per pole. The LHC’s 223 T/m is out of reach of iron: at its 28 mm aperture radius that gradient means 6.2 T, so its quadrupoles are shaped by superconducting coils, not by iron poles (chapter 17).

Normalised strengths. A field of gradient \(g\) bends a particle of rigidity \(B\rho = p/q\) (T m) as a lens of strength

\[ k = \frac{g}{B\rho} \quad (\text{m}^{-2}) \]

and a sextupole of \(B_y = C_2(x^2 - y^2)\) has the normalised strength \(m = 2C_2/B\rho\) (m\(^{-3}\)), so that a thin sextupole of length \(l\) changes the slope by \(\Delta x' = -\tfrac{1}{2}ml\,(x^2 - y^2)\). Its local gradient, \(\partial B_y/\partial x = 2C_2 x\), grows with \(x\): a sextupole is a quadrupole whose strength depends on where the particle passes. Placed where the dispersion sorts particles by momentum (\(x = D_x\delta\)), it gives each momentum its own focusing, which is how chromaticity is corrected (section 12 of the chapter).

How components are quoted. A real magnet’s field is mostly one multipole with small others mixed in. Magnet builders quote the others relative to the main field at a reference radius \(r_0\), inside the aperture:

\[ B_y + iB_x = B_{\text{main}}\sum_n (b_n + i\,a_n)\left(\frac{x + iy}{r_0}\right)^{n-1} \]

The \(b_n\) are the normal components (fields like those above, with \(B_x = 0\) on the midplane), the \(a_n\) the skew ones (the same magnet rotated by \(90°/n\)). They are often given in “units” of \(10^{-4}\). Note the index: in this convention, common in Europe, \(n = 1\) is the dipole, \(n = 2\) the quadrupole and \(n = 3\) the sextupole. Chapter 7 counted from \(n = 0\) for the dipole, as Lee does; American papers often do too. Always check which.

Exercise M6. A sextupole has \(C_2 = 300\) T/m². What gradient does a proton of the PS at 25.4 GeV (\(B\rho = 87.8\) T m) see if it passes 2.6 mm off axis, and what is that as a normalised strength?

2. The quadrupole field

The beam travels along \(s\); \(x\) is horizontal and \(y\) vertical. In the vacuum of the aperture a static field satisfies \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0\) and \(\nabla \times \mathbf{B} = 0\), so it can be written \(\mathbf{B} = -\nabla V\) with \(\nabla^2 V = 0\). The simplest field that is zero on the axis and grows linearly away from it comes from

\[ V = -g\,x\,y, \qquad B_x = g\,y, \qquad B_y = g\,x \]

where \(g = \partial B_y/\partial x\) is the gradient in T/m. Both conditions hold: \(\partial B_x/\partial x + \partial B_y/\partial y = 0\) and \(\partial B_y/\partial x - \partial B_x/\partial y = g - g = 0\). The equipotentials \(xy = \text{const}\) are hyperbolas, and iron poles cut to those hyperbolas produce this field; how they are built is the subject of chapter 17.

The force on a particle of charge \(q\) moving along \(s\) with speed \(v\) is \(\mathbf{F} = q\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}\). With \((x, y, s)\) right-handed (with \(x\) to the right and \(y\) up, \(s\) points out of the page, as in figure 16.3), \(\hat{s} \times \hat{y} = -\hat{x}\) and \(\hat{s} \times \hat{x} = \hat{y}\) (F2, section 3), so

\[ F_x = -q v B_y = -q v g\,x, \qquad F_y = q v B_x = +q v g\,y \]

For \(qg > 0\) the horizontal force is a restoring force proportional to \(x\), and the vertical force pushes outward in proportion to \(y\), with the same strength. This is the curl condition at work. A quadrupole of length \(l\) and gradient \(g\) is a thin lens of focal length \(f = 1/(kl)\) in one plane and \(-f\) in the other. (Inside it \(x'' = F_x/(pv) = -(qg/p)\,x = -kx\), dividing the force by \(pv\) as in the first pre-requisite; if the magnet is short enough that \(x\) hardly changes while the particle crosses it, the slope changes by \(x''\,l = -klx\), which is a thin lens’s \(-x/f\) with \(1/f = kl\).) Here

\[ k = \frac{g}{B\rho} \]

is the normalised strength (m\(^{-2}\)) and \(B\rho = p/q\) is the magnetic rigidity (T m). Dividing by the rigidity makes the optics independent of energy: a lattice tuned at one momentum works at another if every field is scaled with \(p\).

Pre-requisite (continued): Ordinary differential equations (linear, constant and varying coefficients)

Pre-requisite (continued). Chapter 2 solved \(x'' + Kx = 0\) for constant \(K\), changed the independent variable from time to distance along the path, and linearised equations by keeping first-order terms. Chapter 12 built a transfer matrix from the cosine-like and sine-like solutions and showed, through the Wronskian, that its determinant is 1; chapter 14 solved a driven equation as one particular solution plus the free motion. This section adds what sections 3, 4 and 10 of the chapter need beyond that: the three constant-\(K\) solutions side by side as matrices, the Wronskian of an equation that does have an \(x'\) term (which is what acceleration produces), and the particular solution for a constant drive and for a periodic one, which give the bending magnet’s third column and the dispersion. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, sections 21 and 22 (free and forced oscillations); Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, section II.1; and Tong, Classical Mechanics, section 2.5.2 (the driven oscillator: one particular solution plus the free ones).

What is in hand. The equation \(x'' + K(s)x = 0\) is linear, so if the cosine-like solution \(C(s)\) starts as \((1, 0)\) and the sine-like solution \(S(s)\) as \((0, 1)\), every solution is \(x = x_0C + x_0'S\), and

\[ \begin{pmatrix} x \\ x' \end{pmatrix}_s = \begin{pmatrix} C & S \\ C' & S' \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ x' \end{pmatrix}_{s_0} \]

The Wronskian \(W = CS' - SC'\) is the determinant of this matrix, and \(W' = C S'' - S C'' = -KCS + KSC = 0\), so the determinant stays at its starting value, 1. For constant \(K\) over a length \(l\), with \(\phi = \sqrt{|K|}\,l\), the three cases are

  • \(K > 0\): \(C = \cos\phi\), \(S = \sin\phi/\sqrt{K}\). The particle oscillates.
  • \(K < 0\): \(C = \cosh\phi\), \(S = \sinh\phi/\sqrt{|K|}\). It runs away exponentially. (The hyperbolic functions \(\cosh u = (e^u + e^{-u})/2\) and \(\sinh u = (e^u - e^{-u})/2\), met in chapter 2, have \((\cosh u)' = \sinh u\), \((\sinh u)' = \cosh u\) and \(\cosh^2u - \sinh^2u = 1\), all checked by writing out the exponentials (F1, section 4).)
  • \(K = 0\): \(x'' = 0\), so \(C = 1\), \(S = l\). A straight line.

These are the chapter’s \(M_F\), \(M_D\) and \(M_{\text{drift}}\). At work: a quadrupole with \(k = 0.05\) m\(^{-2}\) and \(l = 1\) m has \(\phi = 0.2236\); in its focusing plane \(C = 0.9751\), \(S = 0.9917\) m, \(C' = -0.04958\) m\(^{-1}\); in the other plane \(C = 1.0251\), \(S = 1.0084\) m, \(C' = +0.05042\) m\(^{-1}\). In both, \(CS' - SC' = 1.0000\).

An \(x'\) term changes the Wronskian. For \(x'' + P(s)\,x' + K(s)\,x = 0\) the same steps give

\[ \begin{aligned} W' &= C S'' - S C'' = C(-PS' - KS) - S(-PC' - KC) = -P\,W, \\ W(s) &= W(s_0)\exp\left(-\int_{s_0}^{s} P\,ds\right) \end{aligned} \]

The determinant is no longer 1. Such a term appears when the beam is accelerated. The slope is \(x' = p_x/p\), with \(p_x\) the transverse momentum and \(p\) the total. The cavities push along \(s\) only, so the change of \(p_x\) along the path comes from the magnets alone, \(dp_x/ds = -Kp\,x\) (for a quadrupole, \(-qg\,x\) with \(k = qg/p\)). Then

\[ x'' = \frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{p_x}{p}\right) = \frac{p_x'}{p} - \frac{p_x\,p'}{p^2} = -Kx - \frac{p'}{p}\,x' \]

by the quotient rule (F1, section 2) and \(p_x' = -Kp\,x\). This is the equation above with \(P = p'/p\). The integral of \(P\) is \(\ln(p/p_0)\), because \(p'/p\) is the derivative of \(\ln p\) (F1, section 4); and \(\exp(-\ln(p/p_0)) = p_0/p\), so

\[ \det M = \frac{p_0}{p} \]

The area of the motion in \((x, x')\) shrinks as \(1/p\): the adiabatic damping of section 9 of the chapter, seen from the differential equation. A numerical solution for electrons rising from 1 GeV to 3 GeV over 100 m, with \(K = 0.01\) m\(^{-2}\), gives \(\det M = 0.33333\), as the formula says.

A constant drive: the bending magnet. Off momentum, the chapter’s equation has a right-hand side, \(x'' + K(s)\,x = F(s)\) with \(F = \delta/\rho\). As chapter 14 showed, every solution is one particular solution \(x_p\) plus a free solution, because the difference of two solutions of the driven equation solves the free one. Inside a bending magnet \(K = 1/\rho^2\) and \(F = \delta/\rho\) are constant, and a constant works: \(x_p/\rho^2 = \delta/\rho\) gives \(x_p = \rho\delta\). A particle with more momentum has a larger circle, offset outward by \(\rho\delta\). Add free solutions to start at \((x_0, x_0')\), with \(\theta = l/\rho\) the bending angle:

\[ x = \rho\delta + (x_0 - \rho\delta)\cos\theta + x_0'\rho\sin\theta = x_0\cos\theta + x_0'\rho\sin\theta + \rho(1 - \cos\theta)\,\delta \]

and its derivative, \(x' = -(x_0/\rho)\sin\theta + x_0'\cos\theta + \delta\sin\theta\). These are the three columns of the chapter’s \(M_{\text{bend}}\). For one LHC dipole, \(\rho = 2{,}804\) m and \(\theta = 2\pi/1232 = 5.10\) mrad, the third column is \(\rho(1 - \cos\theta) = 0.0365\) m and \(\sin\theta = 5.10\times10^{-3}\): a particle with \(\delta = 10^{-4}\) leaves 3.6 µm further out and 0.51 µrad steeper.

A periodic drive: the dispersion. In a ring both \(K\) and \(F\) repeat every period, and the solution wanted is the one particular solution that repeats too: the closed orbit of an off-momentum particle, \(D_x\delta\). There is exactly one, unless the free motion can itself repeat after one period, which happens when the phase advance is a whole number of turns. Then adding a free solution would give another periodic one, or, with the drive pushing in step, none at all. The next pre-requisite finds the periodic solution with a matrix inverse and shows where it fails.

3. The equations of motion: Hill’s equation

Take a reference particle of momentum \(p_0\) that follows the design orbit, curved with local radius \(\rho(s)\) in the horizontal plane. Measure the actual particle’s position by \(s\), its distance along the reference orbit, and its displacements \(x\) (outward) and \(y\) (up). Derivatives with respect to \(s\) are written with a prime. Figure 16.4 shows these coordinates, which move along with the reference particle. We want the equation for \(x(s)\) to first order in \(x\), \(y\), their slopes, and \(\delta = (p - p_0)/p_0\).

Figure 16.4. The coordinates that ride along the design orbit. Inside a bending magnet the reference orbit (dashed) is an arc of radius \(\rho\) about the centre \(O\). A particle is located by the distance \(s\) travelled along that orbit and by its displacement \(x\), outward, measured at right angles to it; \(y\) is up, out of the page here. Its distance from \(O\) is \(r = \rho + x\). While the reference particle moves \(ds\), a particle at \(x\) on a parallel arc covers \((1 + x/\rho)\,ds\), the outer lane of a running track (section 10 uses this). Not to scale: \(x\) is drawn far larger than any real displacement.

Inside a bending magnet the reference orbit is locally a circle of radius \(\rho\) about a centre \(O\). The particle is at distance \(r = \rho + x\) from \(O\). In a static magnetic field the speed \(v\) and momentum \(p = \gamma m v\) are constant. The radial equation of motion in polar coordinates about \(O\) is Newton’s second law with the centripetal term \(r\dot\theta^2\) of motion on a circle (F2, section 4; F3, section 8), with the relativistic mass \(\gamma m\), which is constant here because the speed is:

\[ \gamma m\left(\ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2\right) = F_r = -q v_\theta B_y \]

with the force from section 2 (inward for \(qB_y > 0\)). To first order the particle moves almost along \(\hat\theta\), so \(v_\theta \approx v\) and \(r\dot\theta \approx v\). Change the independent variable from \(t\) to \(s\): along the orbit \(ds = v\,dt\,\rho/r\), but the difference between \(d/dt\) and \(v\,d/ds\) only multiplies \(x''\) by \((1 + x/\rho)^{-2}\) and adds a term in \(x'^2\), which give terms of second order. So \(\ddot r \approx v^2 x''\) (since \(r = \rho + x\) with \(\rho\) fixed), and \(r\dot\theta^2 = (r\dot\theta)^2/r \approx v^2/(\rho + x)\). Dividing by \(\gamma m v^2 = pv\):

\[ x'' - \frac{1}{\rho + x} = -\frac{q B_y(x)}{p} \]

Now expand each piece to first order, with the binomial series \((1 + u)^{-1} \approx 1 - u\) for small \(u\) (F1, section 8); for instance \(1/(\rho + x) = (1/\rho)(1 + x/\rho)^{-1}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{1}{\rho + x} &\approx \frac{1}{\rho} - \frac{x}{\rho^2} \\ B_y(x) &\approx B_0 + g\,x \\ \frac{q}{p} &= \frac{q}{p_0(1+\delta)} \approx \frac{1 - \delta}{B_0\,\rho} \end{aligned} \]

The last line uses the design condition \(B_0\rho = p_0/q\): the reference particle is bent on radius \(\rho\) by the field \(B_0\), so \(q/p_0 = 1/(B_0\rho)\). Multiplying the last two lines, \(qB_y/p \approx (B_0 + gx)(1 - \delta)/(B_0\rho) = 1/\rho + gx/(B_0\rho) - \delta/\rho - gx\delta/(B_0\rho)\), and the last term, a product of two small quantities, is dropped. Substituting:

\[ x'' - \frac{1}{\rho} + \frac{x}{\rho^2} = -\frac{1}{\rho} - \frac{g}{B_0\rho}\,x + \frac{\delta}{\rho} \]

The \(1/\rho\) terms cancel, which is the reference orbit satisfying itself. With \(k = g/(B\rho)\) what remains is

\[ x'' + \left(\frac{1}{\rho^2} + k\right)x = \frac{\delta}{\rho} \]

Vertically there is no curvature, and the force \(qvB_x = qvg\,y\) gives

\[ y'' - k\,y = 0 \]

Write \(K_x(s) = 1/\rho^2 + k\) and \(K_y(s) = -k\). Both are functions of \(s\): zero in drifts, \(1/\rho^2\) in a pure dipole, \(\pm k\) in a quadrupole, both in a combined-function magnet such as those of the PS. For a particle on momentum the equation is

\[ x'' + K(s)\,x = 0 \]

with \(K(s)\) periodic with the period of the lattice. This is Hill’s equation, named after the astronomer G. W. Hill, who met it in 1886 in the theory of the Moon’s perigee. Landau and Lifshitz treat the same equation in time, as a system with periodically varying parameters, in Mechanics section 27 (parametric resonance).

Three remarks.

The \(1/\rho^2\) term is the focusing of the curved orbit itself. A particle displaced outward on a parallel circle travels a longer arc in the same field and so is bent back towards the design orbit. It acts only horizontally.

The sum rule. Adding the two strengths, \(K_x + K_y = 1/\rho^2\) everywhere. No arrangement of gradients can make both \(K_x\) and \(K_y\) large and positive. For a uniform weak-focusing ring with \(\rho = R\), the gradient is described by the field index \(n = -(\rho/B)\,\partial B/\partial r\), so \(g = \partial B/\partial r = -nB/\rho\) and \(k = g/(B\rho) = -n/\rho^2\). Then \(K_x = (1-n)/R^2\) and \(K_y = n/R^2\). With constant \(K_x\) the solution is \(\cos(\sqrt{K_x}\,s)\), which goes through \(\sqrt{K_x}\,2\pi R/2\pi = \sqrt{1-n}\) oscillations in one turn of length \(2\pi R\). So the tunes are \(Q_x = \sqrt{1-n}\), \(Q_y = \sqrt{n}\), with \(Q_x^2 + Q_y^2 = 1\). That is the wall of the last section in one line.

Approximations. The equation is linear because we kept only first-order terms. It ignores sextupole and higher field components, the second-order terms of the geometry, the slope-dependent focusing at the ends of rectangular dipoles (edge focusing), coupling between \(x\) and \(y\) from rotated quadrupoles, and the fact that \(\delta\) itself changes the focusing (that is chromaticity, section 12). It fails at large amplitude or large \(\delta\), and it assumes the fields do not change during one passage. Chapter 18 takes up what it leaves out.

Pre-requisite (continued): Matrices, determinants, eigenvalues

Pre-requisite (continued). Chapter 8 taught matrix products and why their order matters, the transpose, the determinant as an area factor, and the inverse; chapter 14 added eigenvalues and eigenvectors, and showed that a symmetric matrix has perpendicular eigenvectors; chapter 12 used the trace of a one-period matrix to decide stability. This section adds the pieces the chapter’s sections 4 to 10 use beyond those: the \(3\times3\) matrices that carry a momentum error and how they multiply, the sum and product of the eigenvalues of any \(2\times2\) matrix, why moving the starting point of a period leaves the trace alone, the \(N\)th power of a matrix through its eigenvectors, the area of an ellipse \(\mathbf{x}^TG\,\mathbf{x} = \varepsilon\) for any \(G\), and a periodic solution found by inverting \(I - M\). Taught from Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, sections II.1 and II.7, and Penrose, The Road to Reality, sections 13.3 to 13.5 (linear transformations, determinants and traces, eigenvalues and eigenvectors).

Three by three. For an off-momentum particle the state is \((x, x', \delta)\), and \(\delta\) does not change in a magnetic lattice. Every element then has the form

\[ \begin{pmatrix} M & \mathbf{d} \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \]

with \(M\) its \(2\times2\) matrix and \(\mathbf{d}\) a column of two numbers, the position and slope that \(\delta = 1\) produces (the sector dipole’s \(\rho(1 - \cos\theta)\) and \(\sin\theta\); zero for drifts and quadrupoles). Multiplying two of them, block by block,

\[ \begin{pmatrix} M_2 & \mathbf{d}_2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} M_1 & \mathbf{d}_1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} M_2M_1 & M_2\mathbf{d}_1 + \mathbf{d}_2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \]

The betatron part multiplies as before; the dispersion made upstream is carried through the downstream optics by \(M_2\), and the new element adds its own.

Sum and product of the eigenvalues. For \(M = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d\end{pmatrix}\) the eigenvalues solve \(\det(M - \lambda I) = (a - \lambda)(d - \lambda) - bc = 0\), that is

\[ \lambda^2 - (a + d)\,\lambda + (ad - bc) = 0 \]

the characteristic polynomial. Compare it with a quadratic written through its roots, \((\lambda - \lambda_1)(\lambda - \lambda_2) = \lambda^2 - (\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)\lambda + \lambda_1\lambda_2\):

\[ \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = \operatorname{tr}M, \qquad \lambda_1\lambda_2 = \det M \]

This holds for any \(2\times2\) matrix. For a transfer matrix \(\det M = 1\), the eigenvalues are a pair \(\lambda\) and \(1/\lambda\), and the trace alone decides them, as chapter 12 used. For the accelerating linac of the previous pre-requisite, \(\det M = p_0/p < 1\) and the eigenvalues’ product falls below 1: both can lie inside the unit circle, and the motion in \((x, x')\) can shrink.

Moving the starting point leaves the trace alone. For any two matrices, \(\operatorname{tr}(AB) = \sum_i\sum_j A_{ij}B_{ji}\), and swapping the names of \(i\) and \(j\) shows this equals \(\operatorname{tr}(BA)\). Now let \(M\) be the one-period matrix starting at \(s_1\), and \(A\) the matrix from \(s_1\) to \(s_2\). The one-period matrix starting at \(s_2\) goes back to \(s_1\), round one period, then forward to \(s_2\): it is \(AMA^{-1}\). Its trace is \(\operatorname{tr}(MA^{-1}A) = \operatorname{tr}M\), and its determinant is \(\det A\,\det M\,\det A^{-1} = \det M\). Same characteristic polynomial, same eigenvalues. A change \(M \to AMA^{-1}\) is called a similarity transformation. In the chapter’s 90° FODO cell, the matrix started at the centre of F has \(M_{12} = 182.5\) m and the one started at the centre of D has \(M_{12} = 31.3\) m, but both have trace 0.

Powers through eigenvectors. To follow a particle for \(N\) periods we need \(M^N\mathbf{x}_0\). If \(M\) has two different eigenvalues, its eigenvectors \(\mathbf{v}_1\), \(\mathbf{v}_2\) point in different directions, so any start can be split as \(\mathbf{x}_0 = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2\) (two linear equations for \(c_1\), \(c_2\)). Each part is only stretched at each pass:

\[ M^N\mathbf{x}_0 = c_1\lambda_1^N\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\lambda_2^N\mathbf{v}_2 \]

The sizes of \(\lambda_1^N\) and \(\lambda_2^N\) decide everything. Two cells of length 106.9 m show both cases.

  • With \(f = 37.8\) m (90°), \(\operatorname{tr}M = 0\) and the eigenvalues are \(\pm i\), of modulus 1. Since \(i^4 = 1\), four cells return every particle to its start: \(M^4 = I\). The motion is bounded.
  • With \(f = 25\) m, stronger than the limit \(f > L/4 = 26.7\) m, \(\operatorname{tr}M = -2.571\) and the eigenvalues are \(-0.478\) and \(-2.093\) (product 1, sum the trace). After ten cells the second part is multiplied by \(2.093^{10} = 1{,}616\). The beam is lost within a few cells.

The area of any ellipse. Chapter 8 found the area \(\pi\varepsilon\) for the ellipse of a beam matrix. The same question for any symmetric, positive \(G\): what area does \(\mathbf{x}^TG\,\mathbf{x} = \varepsilon\) enclose? Turn the axes to lie along the perpendicular eigenvectors of \(G\) (chapter 14). In the new coordinates \((u, v)\) the form has no cross term:

\[ \lambda_1 u^2 + \lambda_2 v^2 = \varepsilon \]

an ellipse with semi-axes \(\sqrt{\varepsilon/\lambda_1}\) and \(\sqrt{\varepsilon/\lambda_2}\). A rotation changes no areas, and \(\lambda_1\lambda_2 = \det G\), so

\[ \text{area} = \pi\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\lambda_1}}\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\lambda_2}} = \frac{\pi\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\det G}} \]

When \(\det G = 1\), as for the Twiss matrix with \(\beta_x\gamma_x - \alpha_x^2 = 1\), the area is \(\pi\varepsilon\). A check: \(G = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0.6 \\ 0.6 & 1\end{pmatrix}\) has \(\det G = 1.64\), so \(\mathbf{x}^TG\mathbf{x} = 1\) encloses \(\pi/\sqrt{1.64} = 2.453\); scattering two million random points over a square and counting those inside gives 2.448.

A periodic solution by inversion. The previous pre-requisite needed the closed orbit of an off-momentum particle: a state \(\mathbf{X} = (D_x, D_x')\) that one period of the \(3\times3\) map returns unchanged, \(\mathbf{X} = M\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{d}\). Rearranged, \((I - M)\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{d}\), so \(\mathbf{X} = (I - M)^{-1}\mathbf{d}\), with the inverse of chapter 8. It exists when

\[ \det(I - M) = (1 - a)(1 - d) - bc = 1 - \operatorname{tr}M + \det M = 2 - \operatorname{tr}M \]

is not zero, using \(\det M = 1\). With \(\operatorname{tr}M = 2\cos\mu\) this is \(2(1 - \cos\mu)\), zero only when the phase advance is a whole number of turns. For the chapter’s worked LHC-like cell with six bends, \(\det(I - M) = 2\) and the solution gives \(D_x = 2.214\) m and \(D_x' = 0\) at the F quadrupole, the value of section 10.

Exercise X3. For the same kick, how much larger is a closed-orbit or dispersion response when the phase advance per turn is 0.1 rad from a whole turn than when it is a quarter turn?

The response goes as \(1/\det(I - M) = 1/(2 - 2\cos\mu)\). At \(\mu = \pi/2\) this is \(1/2\); at \(\mu = 0.1\) rad it is \(1/0.00999 = 100.1\). The response is 200 times larger near the integer. This is why tunes are kept away from integers.

Pre-requisite (continued): Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics, the charged-particle Hamiltonian

Pre-requisite (continued). Chapter 1 built the Lagrangian, the momentum as \(\partial L/\partial v\), the Hamiltonian \(H = pv - L\) and the canonical momentum \(\mathbf{p} + q\mathbf{A}\) of a charge; later chapters used Hamilton’s equations \(\dot{x} = \partial H/\partial p\), \(\dot{p} = -\partial H/\partial x\). This section adds two things the chapter’s sections 4, 9 and 14 rely on: the Hamiltonian of transverse motion with the distance \(s\) along the beam as the clock, in which the momentum conjugate to \(x\) is the transverse momentum \(p_x\) and \(x' = p_x/p\); and the reason a Hamiltonian system, which a lossless magnetic lattice is, can have no damping term \(x'\) in its equation of motion. Taught from Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, section I.1 (the Hamiltonian with \(s\) as the independent variable) and section II.4; Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, sections 40 and 46; and Tong, Classical Mechanics, sections 10.1.4 and 10.2.

Changing the clock. In a beam line we want \(x(s)\), not \(x(t)\). In a static magnetic field the energy \(E\), and so the momentum \(p\), of a particle is constant. Use that. Let the reference path be straight along \(s\) (a curved reference path adds the \(1/\rho^2\) and \(\delta/\rho\) terms that the chapter derives by hand). The particle has the canonical momenta \(P_x\), \(P_y\), \(P_s\), and \(E = H(x, y, s, P_x, P_y, P_s)\). Since \(E\) is fixed, the equation \(H = E\) can be solved for one of the momenta; solve it for \(P_s\):

\[ P_s = \mathcal{P}(x, P_x, y, P_y, s) \]

Now differentiate \(H(\ldots, P_s = \mathcal{P}) = E\) with respect to \(P_x\), keeping the energy fixed. By the chain rule \(\partial H/\partial P_x + (\partial H/\partial P_s)(\partial\mathcal{P}/\partial P_x) = 0\), and Hamilton’s equations say \(\partial H/\partial P_x = \dot{x}\) and \(\partial H/\partial P_s = \dot{s}\). So

\[ \frac{\partial\mathcal{P}}{\partial P_x} = -\frac{\dot{x}}{\dot{s}} = -\frac{dx}{ds} \]

Differentiating with respect to \(x\) the same way gives \(\partial\mathcal{P}/\partial x = -(\partial H/\partial x)/\dot{s} = \dot{P}_x/\dot{s} = dP_x/ds\). These are Hamilton’s equations again, with \(s\) as the clock and

\[ \mathcal{H} = -P_s \]

as the Hamiltonian: \(dx/ds = \partial\mathcal{H}/\partial P_x\) and \(dP_x/ds = -\partial\mathcal{H}/\partial x\). Lee derives the same in his section I.1. The pair \((x, P_x)\) is still canonical; only the clock has changed.

The transverse Hamiltonian of a quadrupole. In a magnet there is no electric potential, and the vector potential of a long quadrupole can be taken along \(s\) only:

\[ A_s = -\frac{g}{2}\left(x^2 - y^2\right) \]

Check, with \(\mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}\) (F2, section 11) and only \(A_s\) present: \(B_x = \partial A_s/\partial y = g\,y\) and \(B_y = -\partial A_s/\partial x = g\,x\), the field of the chapter’s section 2. With \(A_x = A_y = 0\) the canonical transverse momenta are the ordinary ones, \(P_x = p_x\), \(P_y = p_y\). The canonical \(P_s\) is the mechanical \(p_s\) plus \(qA_s\), and \(p_s = \sqrt{p^2 - p_x^2 - p_y^2}\). So

\[ \mathcal{H} = -\sqrt{p^2 - p_x^2 - p_y^2} - qA_s \approx -p + \frac{p_x^2 + p_y^2}{2p} + \frac{qg}{2}\left(x^2 - y^2\right) \]

The approximation expands the square root for \(p_x, p_y \ll p\) (paraxial motion): \(\sqrt{p^2 - u} \approx p - u/(2p)\), with a relative error of order \((p_x/p)^4/8\), which is \(10^{-13}\) for a slope of 1 mrad. Hamilton’s equations give

\[ x' = \frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial p_x} = \frac{p_x}{p}, \qquad p_x' = -\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial x} = -qg\,x \]

Differentiate the first and use the second: \(x'' = p_x'/p = -(qg/p)\,x = -k\,x\), with \(k = qg/p = g/B\rho\). The \(y\) equations give \(y'' = +k\,y\). This is Hill’s equation with \(K_x = k\) and \(K_y = -k\), now from a Hamiltonian. The slope \(x'\) is the canonical momentum divided by the total momentum.

While \(p\) is constant we can divide \(\mathcal{H}\) by \(p\) and drop the constant: \(\mathcal{H}/p = \tfrac12 x'^2 + \tfrac12 K x^2\), with \((x, x')\) as the canonical pair. This is the “pseudo-Hamiltonian” Lee uses for Hill’s equation (his equation 2.47). It holds only while \(p\) does not change.

At work. In the LHC at 7 TeV a slope of 30 µrad is a transverse momentum \(p_x = 7000\ \text{GeV}/c\times3\times10^{-5} = 0.21\) GeV/\(c\), larger than the whole momentum of a 20 MeV proton (0.195 GeV/\(c\)). Transverse momenta are small only in comparison with \(p\).

Why no damping term. Let a flow in the plane be \(x' = u(x, p_x)\), \(p_x' = w(x, p_x)\). A small patch of phase space changes its area at the rate of the divergence of the flow, \(\partial u/\partial x + \partial w/\partial p_x\), times the area (Tong, section 10.2, proves this as the first step of Liouville’s theorem). For a Hamiltonian flow,

\[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial w}{\partial p_x} = \frac{\partial^2\mathcal{H}}{\partial x\,\partial p_x} - \frac{\partial^2\mathcal{H}}{\partial p_x\,\partial x} = 0 \]

so areas never change. Now take a damped oscillator, \(x'' + 2a\,x' + Kx = 0\), written as \(x' = u = p_x/p\) and \(p_x' = w = -Kp\,x - 2a\,p_x\). Its divergence is \(-2a\), and areas shrink as \(e^{-2as}\). No Hamiltonian can produce that, because no \(\mathcal{H}\) has unequal mixed derivatives. So a lattice of static magnets, whose fields do no work and which is described by a Hamiltonian, gives an equation with no \(x'\) term: that is why every transfer matrix in the chapter has determinant 1. Damping needs something outside the Hamiltonian: radiation in an electron ring (chapter 20) or a cooler (chapter 24).

The exception that is not one: acceleration. The ODE pre-requisite above found an \(x'\) term, \((p'/p)\,x'\), when the beam is accelerated. That term lives in the variables \((x, x')\). In the canonical pair \((x, p_x)\) the equations are still \(x' = p_x/p\) and \(p_x' = -Kp\,x\), whose divergence is \(0 + 0 = 0\): the area in \((x, p_x)\) is conserved. Only because \(x' = p_x/p\) and \(p\) grows does the area in \((x, x')\) shrink, as chapter 8 found from the scale factor between the two planes. For electrons gaining 20 MeV per metre at 1 GeV, \(p'/p = 0.02\) m\(^{-1}\). This is why the chapter’s normalised emittance, an area in \((x, p_x)\) divided by \(\pi mc\), is the conserved one.

Exercise H3. A lattice is modelled with a small fictitious damping, \(x'' + 2a\,x' + Kx = 0\) with \(a = 10^{-6}\) m\(^{-1}\), in a ring of 27 km. By how much does the phase-space area shrink in 1,000 turns, and why is such a model wrong for protons?

4. Transfer matrices

Because Hill’s equation is linear, the state \((x, x')\) at \(s_2\) is a linear function of the state at \(s_1\):

\[ \begin{pmatrix} x \\ x' \end{pmatrix}_2 = M(s_2 \mid s_1) \begin{pmatrix} x \\ x' \end{pmatrix}_1 \]

The matrix of a sequence of elements is the product of their matrices, the first element on the right, because the first matrix acts on the starting state first (chapter 8). Within a piece where \(K\) is constant the equation is that of a harmonic oscillator (F3, section 9), with \(s\) in place of time, and the matrices follow directly from the \(C\) and \(S\) of the ODE pre-requisite.

A drift of length \(l\) (\(K = 0\)):

\[ M_{\text{drift}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & l \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \]

A thin lens of focal length \(f\), the limit \(l \to 0\) with \(kl = 1/f\) fixed (in \(M_F\) below, \(\cos\phi \to 1\), \(\sin\phi/\sqrt{K} \to l \to 0\) and \(-\sqrt{K}\sin\phi \to -Kl = -1/f\)):

\[ M_{\text{lens}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -1/f & 1 \end{pmatrix} \]

A focusing quadrupole of length \(l\) (\(K > 0\)), with \(\phi = \sqrt{K}\,l\) (in this section \(\phi\) is the phase of the oscillation through the magnet, not an RF phase):

\[ M_F = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\phi & \dfrac{\sin\phi}{\sqrt{K}} \\ -\sqrt{K}\sin\phi & \cos\phi \end{pmatrix} \]

A defocusing quadrupole (\(K < 0\)), with \(\phi = \sqrt{|K|}\,l\):

\[ M_D = \begin{pmatrix} \cosh\phi & \dfrac{\sinh\phi}{\sqrt{|K|}} \\ \sqrt{|K|}\sinh\phi & \cosh\phi \end{pmatrix} \]

A sector dipole of radius \(\rho\) and bending angle \(\theta = l/\rho\), in which the beam enters and leaves at right angles to the pole edges, has \(K_x = 1/\rho^2\). Horizontally it is a weak focusing lens, and for an off-momentum particle the driving term \(\delta/\rho\) adds a third column. With the state \((x, x', \delta)\):

\[ M_{\text{bend}} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & \rho\sin\theta & \rho(1 - \cos\theta) \\ -\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\rho} & \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \]

Vertically the sector dipole is a drift of length \(l\). A quadrupole of strength \(k\) is \(M_F\) in one plane and \(M_D\) in the other.

The thin-lens limit is a good approximation when \(\phi \ll 1\). For \(k = 0.05\) m\(^{-2}\) and \(l = 1\) m (\(\phi = 0.22\)), the thick quadrupole has \(-1/M_{21} = 20.17\) m, against \(1/(kl) = 20.0\) m for the thin lens.

Every one of these matrices has determinant 1. This is no accident. For two solutions \(x_1, x_2\) of \(x'' + K x = 0\), the Wronskian \(W = x_1 x_2' - x_2 x_1'\) has \(W' = x_1 x_2'' - x_2 x_1'' = -K x_1 x_2 + K x_2 x_1 = 0\). The columns of \(M\) are the solutions that start as \((1, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\), so \(\det M = W(s_2) = W(s_1) = 1\). The reason the equation has no \(x'\) term is that the motion is Hamiltonian and lossless; section 14 returns to this.

Pre-requisite (continued): Complex numbers and complex functions

Pre-requisite (continued). Chapter 7 taught complex numbers in polar form, Euler’s formula \(e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta\), and powers \(w^n = r^ne^{in\theta}\); chapter 12 found that a stable one-period matrix has eigenvalues \(e^{\pm i\mu}\) and quoted Lee’s form \(M = I\cos\mu + J\sin\mu\). This section supplies the two steps behind those results that sections 5 and 8 of the chapter use: why the roots of a real quadratic come as a complex-conjugate pair, so that with product 1 they sit on the unit circle; and de Moivre’s formula, whose matrix twin, built on \(J^2 = -I\), gives \(M^N = I\cos N\mu + J\sin N\mu\). Taught from Penrose, The Road to Reality, sections 4.2 (solving equations with complex numbers) and 5.1 (the geometry of complex multiplication), and Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, sections II.1 (eigenvalues and stability) and II.2 (the form \(I\cos\mu + J\sin\mu\) with \(J^2 = -I\)).

Conjugate roots. Solve \(\lambda^2 - b\lambda + c = 0\) with real \(b\) and \(c\):

\[ \lambda = \frac{b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4c}}{2} \]

If \(b^2 < 4c\) the square root is of a negative number, \(\sqrt{b^2 - 4c} = i\sqrt{4c - b^2}\), and the roots are \(\tfrac{b}{2} \pm \tfrac{i}{2}\sqrt{4c - b^2}\): the same real part, opposite imaginary parts. They are complex conjugates, and that is no accident. Take the complex conjugate of the whole equation: since \(b\) and \(c\) are real, \(\bar\lambda^2 - b\bar\lambda + c = 0\), so whenever \(\lambda\) is a root, so is \(\bar\lambda\). The same holds for the characteristic polynomial of any real matrix.

On the unit circle. The product of the two roots is \(c\), and the product of a number and its conjugate is the square of its modulus, \(\lambda\bar\lambda = |\lambda|^2\). For a transfer matrix \(c = \det M = 1\), so \(|\lambda| = 1\), and the roots are \(e^{\pm i\mu}\) with \(2\cos\mu = b = \operatorname{tr}M\). The condition \(b^2 < 4c\) is \(|\operatorname{tr}M| < 2\): these are the three cases of section 5. At work: the one-turn matrix of the chapter’s exercise 10 has trace 0.2507 and determinant 1.0000; its eigenvalues are \(0.1254 \pm 0.9921i\), of modulus 1.0000, and \(\mu = 82.8°\). A 60° FODO cell has trace 1 and eigenvalues \((1 \pm i\sqrt3)/2 = e^{\pm i\pi/3}\).

De Moivre’s formula. Raising \(e^{i\mu}\) to the \(N\)th power multiplies its angle by \(N\) (chapter 7’s rule for powers, with \(r = 1\)):

\[ (\cos\mu + i\sin\mu)^N = \cos N\mu + i\sin N\mu \]

The modulus stays 1 however large \(N\) is. For the 60° cell, \((e^{i\pi/3})^6 = e^{2\pi i} = 1\): six cells return every particle to where it started.

Its matrix twin. The chapter writes the one-period matrix as \(M = I\cos\mu + J\sin\mu\) with \(J = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha_x & \beta_x \\ -\gamma_x & -\alpha_x\end{pmatrix}\). Multiply \(J\) by itself. The diagonal elements are \(\alpha_x^2 - \beta_x\gamma_x = -1\), using \(\beta_x\gamma_x - \alpha_x^2 = 1\); the off-diagonal ones are \(\alpha_x\beta_x - \beta_x\alpha_x = 0\) and \(-\gamma_x\alpha_x + \alpha_x\gamma_x = 0\). So

\[ J^2 = -I \]

just as \(i^2 = -1\). Since \(I\) commutes with everything, the matrices \(aI + bJ\) multiply among themselves exactly as the numbers \(a + bi\) do. In particular,

\[ \begin{aligned} &(I\cos\mu + J\sin\mu)(I\cos\nu + J\sin\nu) \\ &\quad = I(\cos\mu\cos\nu - \sin\mu\sin\nu) + J(\sin\mu\cos\nu + \cos\mu\sin\nu) \\ &\quad = I\cos(\mu + \nu) + J\sin(\mu + \nu) \end{aligned} \]

and repeating it, \(M^N = I\cos N\mu + J\sin N\mu\). After \(N\) periods a particle has advanced in phase by \(N\mu\) on the same ellipse. With the numbers of exercise 10 (\(\beta_x = 16\) m, \(\alpha_x = -0.5\), \(\gamma_x = 0.078125\) m\(^{-1}\)), the formula gives \(M^3\) with \(M_{12} = -14.876\) m; multiplying the rounded matrix three times gives \(-14.877\) m.

Exercise Z2. Use de Moivre to show that a lattice with \(\mu = 90°\) per cell returns every particle to its start after four cells, and one with \(\mu = 120°\) after three. What does a phase advance of \(360° \times 0.23\) per turn do after 100 turns?

Answer

\((e^{i\pi/2})^4 = e^{2\pi i} = 1\) and \((e^{2\pi i/3})^3 = e^{2\pi i} = 1\), so \(M^4 = I\) and \(M^3 = I\) respectively. For \(\mu = 2\pi\times0.23\), \(100\mu = 2\pi\times23\), a whole number of turns, so \(M^{100} = I\) too: a fractional tune of \(0.23 = 23/100\) repeats after 100 turns. A rational tune makes the motion repeat, which is why the resonances of chapter 18 sit at rational tunes.

5. When is the motion stable? The trace condition

Let \(M\) be the matrix of one period of a periodic lattice, for example one turn of a ring, starting at some point \(s\). After \(N\) periods the state is \(M^N\) times the initial state. The motion stays bounded if and only if the powers of \(M\) stay bounded.

The eigenvalues \(\lambda\) of \(M\) solve \(\det(M - \lambda I) = 0\):

\[ \lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr} M)\,\lambda + \det M = \lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr} M)\,\lambda + 1 = 0 \]

Their product is 1 and their sum is \(\operatorname{tr} M\). There are three cases.

  • If \(\lvert\operatorname{tr} M\rvert < 2\), the roots are complex conjugates on the unit circle, \(\lambda = e^{\pm i\mu}\) with \(\cos\mu = \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr} M\). Any initial state is a combination of the two eigenvectors, and \(M^N\) multiplies them by \(e^{\pm iN\mu}\), which stay of modulus one. The motion is bounded and oscillatory, with phase advance \(\mu\) per period.
  • If \(\lvert\operatorname{tr} M\rvert > 2\), the roots are real, \(\lambda\) and \(1/\lambda\) with \(\lvert\lambda\rvert > 1\). The component along the first eigenvector grows as \(\lvert\lambda\rvert^N\). The beam is lost.
  • If \(\lvert\operatorname{tr} M\rvert = 2\), the motion is marginal: \(\mu\) is 0 or \(\pi\), and the solutions grow linearly. This is the edge of an integer or half-integer resonance.

So the stability criterion is

\[ -2 < \operatorname{tr} M < 2 \]

This is Floquet’s theorem for Hill’s equation in matrix form (G. Floquet, 1883; Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics section 27, where the eigenvalues are written \(\mu_1\mu_2 = 1\)). The trace does not depend on where the period begins: moving the starting point changes \(M\) to \(A M A^{-1}\), which has the same trace. So \(\mu\) is a property of the lattice, not of the place where we look.

6. The FODO cell

The simplest alternating-gradient lattice repeats a cell of a focusing quadrupole (F), a drift (O), a defocusing quadrupole (D) and another drift. In a ring the drifts hold the bending magnets, which focus only weakly, and it is a fair first approximation to treat them as drifts for the transverse optics.

Take the cell of length \(L\) from the centre of one F quadrupole to the centre of the next, with thin lenses of focal length \(f\) for F and \(-f\) for D, a distance \(L/2\) apart. Cutting each F lens in half gives half-lenses of focal length \(2f\) at the ends:

\[ M = M_{\text{lens}}(2f)\; M_{\text{drift}}(L/2)\; M_{\text{lens}}(-f)\; M_{\text{drift}}(L/2)\; M_{\text{lens}}(2f) \]

Multiply the middle three first:

\[ M_{\text{drift}}(L/2)\,M_{\text{lens}}(-f)\,M_{\text{drift}}(L/2) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + \dfrac{L}{2f} & L\left(1 + \dfrac{L}{4f}\right) \\ \dfrac{1}{f} & 1 + \dfrac{L}{2f} \end{pmatrix} \]

Call this matrix \(N\), and write \(q = 1/(2f)\) for the strength of each half-lens, whose matrix is \(\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\-q & 1\end{pmatrix}\). Multiplying \(N\) on the right by it subtracts \(q\) times the second column from the first; multiplying on the left subtracts \(q\) times the first row from the second. Neither touches the top-right element, \(N_{12}\). The top-left becomes \(N_{11} - qN_{12} = 1 + \frac{L}{2f} - \frac{L}{2f}\left(1 + \frac{L}{4f}\right) = 1 - \frac{L^2}{8f^2}\), and the bottom-right \(N_{22} - qN_{12}\) is the same. So

\[ M_{11} = M_{22} = 1 - \frac{L^2}{8f^2}, \qquad M_{12} = L\left(1 + \frac{L}{4f}\right) \]

Section 5 showed that \(\cos\mu = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}M\), so \(\cos\mu = 1 - L^2/(8f^2)\). Using \(\cos\mu = 1 - 2\sin^2(\mu/2)\) (the addition formula of F1, section 3 with both angles \(\mu/2\), and \(\cos^2 = 1 - \sin^2\)), \(2\sin^2(\mu/2) = L^2/(8f^2)\), and

\[ \sin\frac{\mu}{2} = \frac{L}{4f} \]

The cell is stable when \(L/(4f) < 1\), that is \(f > L/4\): the quadrupoles must not be too strong. Too weak and the phase advance is small and the beam large; too strong and the motion becomes unstable. At \(f = L/(2\sqrt{2})\) the phase advance is 90°.

Section 8 shows that at a point of mirror symmetry, such as the centre of a quadrupole, \(M_{12} = \beta_x \sin\mu\). (The betatron function \(\beta_x\), in metres, measures the local size of the beam’s oscillation: the amplitude of each particle is proportional to \(\sqrt{\beta_x}\). Section 7 defines it properly.) The centre of F is where the horizontal betatron function is largest, and the centre of D is where it is smallest; the D-centred cell has the same algebra with \(f \to -f\), so its \(M_{12}\) is \(L(1 - L/4f)\). Put \(L/(4f) = \sin(\mu/2)\) into the two values of \(M_{12}\) and divide by \(\sin\mu\):

\[ \beta_{x,\max} = \frac{L\left(1 + \sin\frac{\mu}{2}\right)}{\sin\mu}, \qquad \beta_{x,\min} = \frac{L\left(1 - \sin\frac{\mu}{2}\right)}{\sin\mu} \]

Vertically the roles swap: \(\beta_y\) is largest at D and smallest at F. These formulas were checked here by multiplying the matrices numerically.

For a 90° cell, \(\beta_{x,\max} = 1.707\,L\) and \(\beta_{x,\min} = 0.293\,L\). For 60°, \(1.732\,L\) and \(0.577\,L\). The maximum is smallest at \(\mu = 76.3°\), where it is \(1.665\,L\), which is why arc cells are usually built with phase advances between about 60° and 90° (figure 16.5).

Weak quadrupoles (small \(\mu\)) give a large beam everywhere; strong ones (towards 180°, where \(f\) approaches \(L/4\)) make \(\beta_{x,\min}\) small but \(\beta_{x,\max}\) large again, and at 180° the cell becomes unstable. A worked cell. Take a cell of about the size of an LHC arc cell, \(L = 106.9\) m, with \(\mu = 90°\). Then \(f = L/(4\sin 45°) = 37.8\) m, \(\beta_{x,\max} = 182.5\) m and \(\beta_{x,\min} = 31.3\) m. Holzer’s CERN Accelerator School lecture gives \(\beta = 180\) m in the LHC arcs; the thin-lens cell reproduces it.

7. Courant-Snyder theory: the betatron function

The FODO cell shows that the beam’s envelope swells and shrinks along the lattice. Courant and Snyder’s theory of 1958 describes that envelope by one function.

Look for a solution of \(x'' + K(s)x = 0\) of the form

\[ x(s) = \sqrt{\varepsilon}\,w(s)\cos\left(\psi(s) + \psi_0\right) \]

with constants \(\varepsilon\) and \(\psi_0\) and functions \(w\) and \(\psi\) to be found. The idea is to let the solution of constant \(K\), a cosine of fixed amplitude and steadily advancing phase, have an amplitude \(w\) and a rate of phase advance \(\psi'\) that vary along the lattice. Differentiate twice, with the product and chain rules (F1, section 2):

\[ \begin{aligned} x' &= \sqrt{\varepsilon}\left[w'\cos(\psi+\psi_0) - w\psi'\sin(\psi+\psi_0)\right] \\ x'' &= \sqrt{\varepsilon}\left[(w'' - w\psi'^2)\cos(\psi+\psi_0) - (2w'\psi' + w\psi'')\sin(\psi+\psi_0)\right] \end{aligned} \]

Put this into Hill’s equation. It must hold for every \(\psi_0\), so the coefficients of the cosine and of the sine vanish separately:

\[ \begin{aligned} w'' - w\psi'^2 + K w &= 0 \\ 2w'\psi' + w\psi'' &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

Multiply the second by \(w\): since \((w^2\psi')' = 2ww'\psi' + w^2\psi''\), it reads \((w^2\psi')' = 0\), so \(w^2\psi'\) is a constant, which we may set to 1 by absorbing it into \(\varepsilon\). Define the betatron function \(\beta_x = w^2\) (m). Then \(\psi' = 1/w^2\), and putting this into the first equation, \(w\psi'^2 = 1/w^3\):

\[ \psi' = \frac{1}{\beta_x}, \qquad w'' + K w - \frac{1}{w^3} = 0 \]

The first says that the phase of the oscillation advances fastest where the beam is narrow. The second, the envelope equation, fixes \(w\); in a periodic lattice we take its periodic solution, which exists when the motion is stable. So the betatron oscillation is

\[ x(s) = \sqrt{\varepsilon\,\beta_x(s)}\,\cos\left(\psi(s) + \psi_0\right) \]

an oscillation whose amplitude \(\sqrt{\varepsilon\beta_x}\) and local wavelength \(2\pi\beta_x\) both follow the lattice. The function \(\beta_x(s)\) is the same for every particle; the amplitude constant \(\varepsilon\) and the phase \(\psi_0\) belong to the particle.

The Twiss parameters and the invariant. Define

\[ \alpha_x = -\frac{\beta_x'}{2}, \qquad \gamma_x = \frac{1 + \alpha_x^2}{\beta_x} \]

(\(\alpha_x\) is dimensionless, \(\gamma_x\) in m\(^{-1}\)). With \(w = \sqrt{\beta_x}\), \(w' = \beta_x'/(2\sqrt{\beta_x}) = -\alpha_x/\sqrt{\beta_x}\) and \(w\psi' = \sqrt{\beta_x}/\beta_x = 1/\sqrt{\beta_x}\), the slope becomes

\[ x' = -\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\beta_x}}\left[\alpha_x\cos(\psi+\psi_0) + \sin(\psi+\psi_0)\right] \]

Solve the two equations for the cosine and the sine:

\[ \cos(\psi+\psi_0) = \frac{x}{\sqrt{\varepsilon\beta_x}}, \qquad \sin(\psi+\psi_0) = -\frac{\alpha_x x + \beta_x x'}{\sqrt{\varepsilon\beta_x}} \]

and add their squares. The phase drops out, because \(\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1\):

\[ x^2 + (\alpha_x x + \beta_x x')^2 = \varepsilon\beta_x \]

Multiply out the bracket, \(x^2(1 + \alpha_x^2) + 2\alpha_x\beta_x xx' + \beta_x^2x'^2 = \varepsilon\beta_x\), and divide by \(\beta_x\), using \(\gamma_x = (1 + \alpha_x^2)/\beta_x\):

\[ \gamma_x x^2 + 2\alpha_x x x' + \beta_x x'^2 = \varepsilon \]

This is the Courant-Snyder invariant. At each \(s\) it is an ellipse in the \((x, x')\) plane, and as the particle goes round it moves on that ellipse. Since \(\beta_x\gamma_x - \alpha_x^2 = 1\) (from the definition of \(\gamma_x\)), the ellipse has area \(\pi\varepsilon\) everywhere (the area of an ellipse \(\mathbf{x}^TG\,\mathbf{x} = \varepsilon\) is \(\pi\varepsilon/\sqrt{\det G}\), from the matrices pre-requisite); its shape changes along the lattice but its area does not. Its half-width is \(\sqrt{\varepsilon\beta_x}\) and its half-height in angle is \(\sqrt{\varepsilon\gamma_x}\). Where \(\alpha_x = 0\) the ellipse is upright: the beam is at a waist or a maximum. Where \(\alpha_x > 0\) the beam is converging; where \(\alpha_x < 0\) it is diverging. (Since \(\alpha_x = -\beta_x'/2\), a positive \(\alpha_x\) means \(\beta_x\), and with it the beam’s width, is shrinking along \(s\).)

Transporting the Twiss parameters. Write the invariant as \(\mathbf{x}^T G\,\mathbf{x} = \varepsilon\) with \(G = \begin{pmatrix}\gamma_x & \alpha_x \\ \alpha_x & \beta_x\end{pmatrix}\), and put \(B = G^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}\beta_x & -\alpha_x \\ -\alpha_x & \gamma_x\end{pmatrix}\) (both have determinant 1; here and in sections 9 and 13 \(B\) is this matrix, not a magnetic field). If \(\mathbf{x}_2 = M\mathbf{x}_1\), the same particles lie on \(\mathbf{x}_2^T M^{-T} G_1 M^{-1}\mathbf{x}_2 = \varepsilon\) (put \(\mathbf{x}_1 = M^{-1}\mathbf{x}_2\) into \(\mathbf{x}_1^TG_1\mathbf{x}_1 = \varepsilon\), with \(M^{-T}\) the transpose of \(M^{-1}\)). So \(G_2 = M^{-T}G_1M^{-1}\), and inverting it, with \((ABC)^{-1} = C^{-1}B^{-1}A^{-1}\) (chapter 8),

\[ B_2 = M\,B_1\,M^T \]

That is how a lattice program carries \(\beta_x\) and \(\alpha_x\) from element to element.

8. The one-turn matrix and the tune

Let \(M\) be the matrix of one full period from \(s\) to \(s + C\). Over one period \(\beta_x\) and \(\alpha_x\) return to their values, and the phase advances by \(\mu = \int_s^{s+C} ds/\beta_x\). Write an initial state in terms of amplitude and phase with the formulas above, advance the phase by \(\mu\), and express the result in terms of the initial \(x\) and \(x'\) again. In steps: section 7 gave \(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\cos\phi = x/\sqrt{\beta_x}\) and \(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\sin\phi = -(\alpha_x x + \beta_x x')/\sqrt{\beta_x}\), with \(\phi = \psi + \psi_0\). One period later the phase is \(\phi + \mu\) and \(\beta_x\), \(\alpha_x\) are back to the same values, so by the addition formula (F1, section 3)

\[ x_2 = \sqrt{\varepsilon\beta_x}\,\big(\cos\phi\cos\mu - \sin\phi\sin\mu\big) = x\cos\mu + (\alpha_x x + \beta_x x')\sin\mu \]

The slope goes the same way: \(x_2' = -\sqrt{\varepsilon/\beta_x}\,[\alpha_x\cos(\phi + \mu) + \sin(\phi + \mu)]\); expanding both and collecting terms, using \(1 + \alpha_x^2 = \beta_x\gamma_x\), gives \(x_2' = -\gamma_x\sin\mu\;x + (\cos\mu - \alpha_x\sin\mu)\,x'\). Read off the coefficients of \(x\) and \(x'\):

\[ M = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\mu + \alpha_x\sin\mu & \beta_x\sin\mu \\ -\gamma_x\sin\mu & \cos\mu - \alpha_x\sin\mu \end{pmatrix} = I\cos\mu + J\sin\mu, \qquad J = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha_x & \beta_x \\ -\gamma_x & -\alpha_x\end{pmatrix} \]

with \(J^2 = -I\), which makes \(J\) play the part of \(i\): \(M^N = I\cos N\mu + J\sin N\mu\).

This is how the Twiss functions are found in practice. Multiply the element matrices to get \(M\) at the point of interest, then read off

\[ \cos\mu = \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr} M, \qquad \beta_x = \frac{M_{12}}{\sin\mu}, \qquad \alpha_x = \frac{M_{11} - M_{22}}{2\sin\mu}, \qquad \gamma_x = -\frac{M_{21}}{\sin\mu} \]

choosing the sign of \(\sin\mu\) so that \(\beta_x > 0\). The trace gives \(\mu\) only modulo \(2\pi\): the matrix cannot tell a tune of 6.23 from 0.23 or 7.23. At a symmetry point \(M_{11} = M_{22}\), so \(\alpha_x = 0\), and \(M_{12} = \beta_x\sin\mu\); that was used for the FODO cell.

For a ring, the phase advance per turn divided by \(2\pi\) is the tune:

\[ Q_x = \frac{1}{2\pi}\oint\frac{ds}{\beta_x} \]

the number of betatron oscillations per turn. A ring of \(N_c\) identical cells has \(Q_x = N_c\mu/(2\pi)\).

The smooth approximation. If \(\beta_x\) did not vary, the tune integral would be \(C/\beta_x = 2\pi Q_x\), so \(\beta_x = C/(2\pi Q_x) = R/Q_x\), with \(R = C/2\pi\) the mean radius, and the oscillation would be \(x \propto \cos(Q_x s/R)\), the motion of a harmonic oscillator of wavenumber \(Q_x/R\). It is the average behaviour of a ring whose cells are short compared with the betatron wavelength. Note what it does not say: the average of \(K\) in a lattice of equal and opposite quadrupoles is about zero, yet the beam is focused. The effective focusing comes from the second-order term found in the doublet: for small \(\mu\), \(\sin(\mu/2) \approx \mu/2\), so \(\sin(\mu/2) = L/(4f)\) gives \(\mu \approx L/(2f)\). An oscillation that advances by \(\mu\) in a length \(L\) has wavenumber \(\mu/L\), and a uniform focusing \(K\) gives wavenumber \(\sqrt{K}\); so the effective focusing strength is \(K_{\text{eff}} = (\mu/L)^2 \approx 1/(4f^2)\), quadratic in the lens strength.

For the CERN PS, \(C = 628.3\) m, so \(R = 100.0\) m. With tunes near 6.2 the smooth approximation gives \(\beta_x \approx 16.1\) m. A recent CERN impedance study (Tables 5 and 6 of arXiv:2505.09328) uses mean betatron functions of 15.8 to 16.5 m for the PS. The weak-focusing ring of the same radius with \(n = 0.5\) would have \(\beta_x \approx R/Q_x = 141\) m.

Pre-requisite: Action-angle variables

Pre-requisite. This section describes an oscillation by two new variables: an action \(J\), which is the area of phase space that the oscillation encloses divided by \(2\pi\) and stays constant, and an angle \(\phi\), which advances steadily round the closed curve. Chapter 12 met the action and angle briefly, for the uniform oscillator of the smooth approximation, to sort resonances. This section builds them properly: for any one-dimensional oscillation, with the check that they are a canonical pair, and then for the betatron oscillation in a lattice whose focusing varies. It shows that the Courant-Snyder invariant of the chapter is twice the action of a betatron oscillation, that the betatron phase is its angle, and that the action in canonical variables is the quantity that stays fixed while a beam is slowly accelerated. Section 9 of the chapter uses this for emittance and adiabatic damping, and the next pre-requisite uses it to describe a whole beam. Taught from Tong, Classical Mechanics, sections 10.5.1 and 10.5.3; Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, sections 49 and 50; and Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, section II.4.

The idea. A particle that oscillates goes round a closed curve in its phase space, \((x, p)\), again and again. Two numbers say everything about it: which curve it is on, and where on that curve it is now. The first does not change; the second goes round. Action-angle variables are the best choice of these two numbers: the “which curve” number is chosen as the area inside the curve divided by \(2\pi\), called the action \(J\), and the “where” number as an angle \(\phi\) that advances at a constant rate. In these variables the motion is as simple as it can be: \(J\) stays put and \(\phi\) grows uniformly. (The chapter’s section 8 uses the letter \(J\) for a matrix; here \(J\) is always the action.)

The harmonic oscillator. Take a mass \(m\) (kg) on a spring, with angular frequency \(\omega\) (rad/s) and energy

\[ H = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac{m\omega^2x^2}{2} \]

At energy \(E\) the curve \(H = E\) is an ellipse in the \((x, p)\) plane with semi-axes \(\sqrt{2E/(m\omega^2)}\) in \(x\) and \(\sqrt{2mE}\) in \(p\). Its area is \(\pi\) times their product:

\[ \oint p\,dx = \pi\sqrt{\frac{2E}{m\omega^2}}\sqrt{2mE} = \frac{2\pi E}{\omega}, \qquad J = \frac{1}{2\pi}\oint p\,dx = \frac{E}{\omega} \]

\(J\) has the units of energy times time, J s, the units of angular momentum. Now write the position and momentum in terms of \(J\) and an angle \(\phi\):

\[ x = \sqrt{\frac{2J}{m\omega}}\cos\phi, \qquad p = -\sqrt{2m\omega J}\,\sin\phi \]

Put these into \(H\): \(p^2/(2m) = \omega J\sin^2\phi\) and \(m\omega^2x^2/2 = \omega J\cos^2\phi\), so

\[ H = \omega J \]

The Hamiltonian no longer contains \(\phi\) at all. If \((\phi, J)\) is a canonical pair, with \(\phi\) as the coordinate and \(J\) as its momentum, Hamilton’s equations give

\[ \dot\phi = \frac{\partial H}{\partial J} = \omega, \qquad \dot J = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial\phi} = 0 \]

The angle advances at the oscillator’s frequency and the action is constant. Is the pair canonical? The test, which the pre-requisite on canonical transformations in section 14 explains, is that the change of variables keeps areas: the Jacobian determinant \(\partial(x, p)/\partial(\phi, J)\) must be 1. (The Jacobian determinant is the determinant of the \(2\times2\) matrix of partial derivatives (F1, section 9) of the old variables with respect to the new; a small rectangle \(d\phi\,dJ\) maps to a small parallelogram whose area is this determinant times \(d\phi\,dJ\), as for any matrix in chapter 8.) Here \(\partial x/\partial\phi = -\sqrt{2J/m\omega}\sin\phi\), \(\partial x/\partial J = \cos\phi/\sqrt{2m\omega J}\), \(\partial p/\partial\phi = -\sqrt{2m\omega J}\cos\phi\), \(\partial p/\partial J = -\sqrt{m\omega/2J}\sin\phi\), and

\[ \frac{\partial x}{\partial\phi}\frac{\partial p}{\partial J} - \frac{\partial x}{\partial J}\frac{\partial p}{\partial\phi} = \sin^2\phi + \cos^2\phi = 1 \]

It is. Tong (section 10.5.1) does the same check through Poisson brackets.

Any one-dimensional oscillation. The same holds for any bounded motion in one dimension: define \(J = \frac{1}{2\pi}\oint p\,dx\) round the orbit; then the energy is a function of \(J\) alone, and the frequency is

\[ \omega = \frac{dE}{dJ} \]

(Tong, section 10.5.3, proves it by differentiating the area with respect to the energy; Landau and Lifshitz, section 50.) For the harmonic oscillator \(E = \omega J\) and \(dE/dJ = \omega\), a constant. For an oscillation in a non-linear force the frequency depends on the action, which is the amplitude-dependent tune of chapter 18.

The betatron oscillation. Use \(s\) as the clock and \((x, x')\) as the canonical pair, which is right while the momentum is constant (the Hamiltonian pre-requisite above). Section 7 of the chapter found that a particle moves on the ellipse \(\gamma_x x^2 + 2\alpha_x xx' + \beta_x x'^2 = \varepsilon\) of area \(\pi\varepsilon\). Its action is therefore

\[ J = \frac{\pi\varepsilon}{2\pi} = \frac{\varepsilon}{2} = \frac12\left(\gamma_x x^2 + 2\alpha_x xx' + \beta_x x'^2\right) \]

in metres. The chapter’s solution, with \(\varepsilon = 2J\) and the angle \(\phi = \psi + \psi_0\), reads

\[ x = \sqrt{2J\beta_x}\cos\phi, \qquad x' = -\sqrt{\frac{2J}{\beta_x}}\left(\sin\phi + \alpha_x\cos\phi\right) \]

The Jacobian of this change is again 1: the four derivatives give \(\sin^2\phi + \alpha_x\sin\phi\cos\phi + \cos^2\phi - \alpha_x\sin\phi\cos\phi = 1\) (a numerical check at \(\beta_x = 50\) m, \(\alpha_x = 1.3\) gives 1.0000). Lee (section II.4) shows that in these variables the Hamiltonian is

\[ \mathcal{H} = \frac{J}{\beta_x(s)} \]

so that \(\phi' = \partial\mathcal{H}/\partial J = 1/\beta_x\) and \(J' = -\partial\mathcal{H}/\partial\phi = 0\). Those are the chapter’s two results, \(\psi' = 1/\beta_x\) and the invariance of \(\varepsilon\), now seen as one statement: in action-angle variables a betatron oscillation is a constant action and a steadily turning angle. The angle turns fastest where \(\beta_x\) is small.

At work. In the LHC-like cell of the chapter, a particle at the centre of the F quadrupole (\(\beta_x = 182.5\) m, \(\alpha_x = 0\)) with amplitude 1 mm has \(J = x^2/(2\beta_x) = 2.74\) nm. At the D quadrupole (\(\beta_x = 31.3\) m) the same particle’s amplitude is \(\sqrt{2J\beta_x} = 0.414\) mm. Its action is 5.5 times the beam’s rms emittance of 0.5 nm.

Conventions. Lee writes the area of the ellipse as \(2\pi J\), as here. Many papers call \(2J\) the “emittance of a particle”, the \(\varepsilon\) of the chapter. For a whole beam the rms emittance turns out to be the average action, \(\varepsilon_{\text{rms}} = \langle J\rangle\) (the next pre-requisite). Read the definitions before comparing numbers.

The canonical action and acceleration. The pair \((x, x')\) is canonical only at fixed \(p\). The true canonical pair is \((x, p_x)\) with \(p_x = p\,x'\), and the action measured in it is

\[ J_p = \frac{1}{2\pi}\oint p_x\,dx = p\,J \]

When the beam is accelerated slowly, slowly meaning that the momentum changes by a small fraction during one betatron oscillation, \(J_p\) is an adiabatic invariant (Landau and Lifshitz, section 49). So \(pJ\) stays constant, \(J \propto 1/p\), and the amplitude \(\sqrt{2J\beta_x}\) shrinks as \(p^{-1/2}\) at a given \(\beta_x\). For the LHC ramp from 450 GeV to 7 TeV, \(J\) falls by \(450/7000 = 0.064\) and every amplitude by \(0.254\). Dividing \(2J_p\) by \(mc\) gives \(\beta\gamma\varepsilon\), the normalised emittance.

Exercise A1. A particle has action \(J = 1\) µm. What is its largest amplitude in a ring with mean \(\beta_x = 16.1\) m (the strong-focusing PS) and in one with \(\beta_x = 141\) m (a weak-focusing ring of the same radius)?

Answer

\(\sqrt{2J\beta_x} = \sqrt{2\times10^{-6}\times16.1} = 5.7\) mm and \(\sqrt{2\times10^{-6}\times141} = 16.8\) mm. The same phase-space area needs three times the aperture in the weak-focusing ring.

Exercise A2. At a point with \(\beta_x = 10\) m and \(\alpha_x = 1\), a particle has \(x = 1\) mm and \(x' = -0.2\) mrad. Find its action and its angle.

Answer

\(\gamma_x = (1 + 1)/10 = 0.2\) m\(^{-1}\). Then \(2J = 0.2\times10^{-6} + 2\times1\times10^{-3}\times(-0.2\times10^{-3}) + 10\times(0.2\times10^{-3})^2 = (0.2 - 0.4 + 0.4)\times10^{-6}\), so \(J = 0.1\) µm. With \(\sqrt{2J\beta_x} = 1.414\) mm: \(\cos\phi = 1/1.414 = 0.707\) and \(\sin\phi = -(\alpha_x x + \beta_x x')/\sqrt{2J\beta_x} = -(1 - 2)/1.414 = 0.707\), so \(\phi = 45°\).

Exercise A3. In the smooth approximation (as in chapter 12, but with \(s\) rather than the azimuth as the clock) a ring is a uniform focusing channel, \(\mathcal{H} = \tfrac12 x'^2 + \tfrac12(Q_x/R)^2x^2\). Find \(J\) in terms of \(\mathcal{H}\), the rate at which \(\phi\) advances, and the advance per turn.

Answer

This is the harmonic oscillator with \(m = 1\) and \(\omega = Q_x/R\), so \(J = \mathcal{H}/(Q_x/R)\) and \(\mathcal{H} = (Q_x/R)J\). Then \(\phi' = d\mathcal{H}/dJ = Q_x/R\), and over a turn of length \(2\pi R\) the angle advances by \(2\pi Q_x\): \(Q_x\) oscillations per turn, as the tune should be. It agrees with \(\phi' = 1/\beta_x\) for \(\beta_x = R/Q_x\).

Pre-requisite (continued): Distribution functions, Vlasov equation, beam temperature

Pre-requisite (continued). Chapter 7 described a beam as a density of points in \((x, x')\), collected its second moments about the centre into the matrix \(\Sigma\) with \(\varepsilon^2 = \det\Sigma\), showed that linear optics keeps that rms emittance, and showed how a nonlinear kick grows it (filamentation). This section adds two things the chapter’s sections 9 and 13 use. First, the Gaussian beam matched to a lattice, written with the action: its actions are spread exponentially, which gives in one line the fractions inside an ellipse behind the emittance conventions of chapter 7, and the size of the beam’s tail. Second, filamentation by phase mixing: particles with slightly different tunes spread round their ellipses, so that a beam injected off centre or with the wrong shape grows to a larger emittance even in a perfectly linear ring. Taught from Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, sections II.5 A, B and D (the emittance of a beam, the \(\sigma\)-matrix and the Gaussian distribution), and his exercise 2.2.16 (the emittance growth of an injected beam whose ellipse does not match the ring’s, worked in the ring’s normalised coordinates).

A matched Gaussian beam. A beam is matched when its distribution does not change shape from turn to turn. Each particle keeps its action \(J\) and turns its angle, so any distribution that depends on \(J\) alone is matched. The usual choice is

\[ f(x, x') = \frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon}\exp\left(-\frac{J}{\varepsilon}\right), \qquad J = \tfrac12\left(\gamma_x x^2 + 2\alpha_x xx' + \beta_x x'^2\right) \]

with \(\varepsilon\) a constant (m) and the Twiss functions those of the lattice at that point. To see what it is, change to normalised coordinates

\[ u = \frac{x}{\sqrt{\beta_x}}, \qquad v = \frac{\alpha_x x + \beta_x x'}{\sqrt{\beta_x}} \]

Then \(u^2 + v^2 = 2J\) (multiply out), and the Jacobian of the change is \((1/\sqrt{\beta_x})\cdot\sqrt{\beta_x} = 1\), so densities and areas carry over unchanged. In \((u, v)\) the distribution is \(\frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon}e^{-(u^2+v^2)/(2\varepsilon)}\): a round Gaussian with variance \(\varepsilon\) in each direction. So \(\langle u^2\rangle = \varepsilon\), which is \(\langle x^2\rangle = \varepsilon\beta_x\), and the rms emittance of this beam is \(\varepsilon\), the chapter’s matched beam.

How the actions are spread. Go to polar coordinates in the \((u, v)\) plane, \(u = r\cos\phi\), \(v = -r\sin\phi\). Then \(J = r^2/2\), so \(dJ = r\,dr\), and the area element \(r\,dr\,d\phi\) (a small patch of radial width \(dr\) and arc length \(r\,d\phi\), F2, section 13) is \(dJ\,d\phi\). Integrating over the angle, the fraction of particles with action between \(J\) and \(J + dJ\) is

\[ P(J)\,dJ = \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,e^{-J/\varepsilon}\,dJ \]

An exponential distribution with mean \(\langle J\rangle = \varepsilon\): the rms emittance of a Gaussian beam is its average action. The fraction of particles inside the ellipse of “emittance” \(\varepsilon_k\), that is with \(2J \le \varepsilon_k\), follows at once:

\[ F(\varepsilon_k) = \int_0^{\varepsilon_k/2}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}e^{-J/\varepsilon}\,dJ = 1 - \exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon_k}{2\varepsilon}\right) \]

This is the formula that chapter 7’s Gaussian pre-requisite found by integrating over a disc: 39.3 per cent inside the rms ellipse, 86.5 per cent inside four times it, 95.0 per cent inside six times. The conventions built on it (rms, 95 per cent, “four times rms”) are explained there; Lee’s Table 2.1 lists the same formula at two to eight times the rms emittance (63, 86, 95 and 98 per cent). What the action adds is a reading of the tail: the fraction of particles whose action exceeds \(J\) is simply \(e^{-J/\varepsilon}\), and an action \(J\) means a betatron amplitude \(\sqrt{2J\beta_x}\) wherever the particle is in the ring.

Phase mixing. No real ring has exactly one tune. Particles of different momentum have different tunes through chromaticity (section 12 of the chapter), and in nonlinear fields the tune depends on the action. Suppose the tunes are spread about \(Q_0\) with an rms width \(\sigma_Q\). A particle’s angle after \(n\) turns is \(\phi_0 + 2\pi Q n\), so two particles that started together differ in angle by \(2\pi\Delta Q\,n\). Once \(2\pi\sigma_Q n\) is of order one radian, the angles are scattered round the ellipse and no memory of the starting angle is left. Nothing in Liouville’s theorem is broken: each particle keeps its action, and the true phase-space area is unchanged. But the rms emittance, which is all an instrument or the next machine sees, is computed from the cloud as it now lies.

The kicked centroid. Give a matched beam a common offset \(\Delta x\) at a point where \(\alpha_x = 0\). Its centre then oscillates, and after \(n\) turns sits at \(\Delta x\,\langle\cos(2\pi Qn)\rangle\), an average over the tunes in the beam. For a Gaussian spread of tunes this average is \(\cos(2\pi Q_0 n)\,\exp[-(2\pi\sigma_Q n)^2/2]\); the exponential is the Fourier transform of the Gaussian (chapter 8). The centroid signal decays to \(e^{-1/2} = 0.61\) of its size in \(n = 1/(2\pi\sigma_Q)\) turns, 159 turns for \(\sigma_Q = 10^{-3}\). This is the decoherence of the chapter’s “At the bench”, item 1.

The emittance it leaves. Each particle’s action after the offset is \(J = [(x + \Delta x)^2/\beta_x + \beta_x x'^2]/2\). Averaged over the beam, the cross term \(\langle x\rangle\Delta x\) vanishes because the beam was centred, so

\[ \langle J\rangle = \varepsilon + \frac{\Delta x^2}{2\beta_x} \]

When the angles have mixed, the cloud is again a function of \(J\) alone, and its rms emittance is \(\langle J\rangle\). The injection error has become emittance:

\[ \varepsilon_f = \varepsilon + \frac{\Delta x^2}{2\beta_x} \]

At work. A beam with \(\varepsilon = 7.78\) nm (the LHC beam at 450 GeV) injected 0.5 mm off the closed orbit where \(\beta_x = 180\) m gains \(0.25\times10^{-6}/360 = 0.694\) nm, a growth of 8.9 per cent. A simulation of a million particles, offset and given random angles, gives 8.475 nm against the predicted 8.474 nm. The same reasoning with a wrongly shaped ellipse instead of an offset gives the mismatch factor of the chapter’s section 13.

Exercise D4. A collimator jaw stands at \(6\,\sigma_x\) from the closed orbit, where \(\sigma_x = \sqrt{\varepsilon\beta_x}\). What fraction of a matched Gaussian beam has betatron amplitudes large enough to reach it, and how many protons is that in a bunch of \(1.15\times10^{11}\)?

Answer

An amplitude \(\sqrt{2J\beta_x}\) reaches \(6\sqrt{\varepsilon\beta_x}\) when \(J \ge 18\,\varepsilon\). The fraction with larger action is \(e^{-18} = 1.5\times10^{-8}\), about 1,750 protons. They touch the jaw only when their angle brings them to the edge, so they are removed over many turns. A real beam has more tail than a Gaussian, which is why collimators are set from measurement, not from this estimate.

Exercise D5. A proton beam of rms emittance 1 µm is injected into a ring 2 mm off the closed orbit at a point where \(\beta_x = 16\) m and \(\alpha_x = 0\). What is its emittance after filamentation?

\(\Delta x^2/(2\beta_x) = (2\times10^{-3})^2/32 = 0.125\) µm, so \(\varepsilon_f = 1.125\) µm, 12.5 per cent more. Halving the error to 1 mm would cut the growth to 3.1 per cent: the growth goes as the square of the error.

\(1/(2\pi\sigma_Q) = 318\) turns. After 1,000 turns, \(\exp[-(2\pi\times5\times10^{-4}\times1000)^2/2] = \exp(-4.93) = 0.0072\): less than 1 per cent. A 1,024-turn record for a tune measurement would be mostly noise in its second half.

9. Emittance and Liouville’s theorem

A beam is many particles, each with its own \(\varepsilon\) and \(\psi_0\). Describe it by its second moments, measured from the centroid (the beam’s average position and slope): \(\langle x^2\rangle\), \(\langle x x'\rangle\), \(\langle x'^2\rangle\), where \(\langle\ \rangle\) is the average over the particles. The first and last are variances, the squares of the beam’s rms width and rms angle (F6, section 3); the middle one, the correlation, says whether particles further out tend to have larger slopes. The rms emittance is

\[ \varepsilon = \sqrt{\langle x^2\rangle\langle x'^2\rangle - \langle x x'\rangle^2} \]

in m rad (often quoted in mm mrad or μm). If the beam is matched to the lattice (its distribution keeps its shape from turn to turn, as the distribution pre-requisite showed for the Gaussian), its second-moment matrix \(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}\langle x^2\rangle & \langle xx'\rangle\\ \langle xx'\rangle & \langle x'^2\rangle\end{pmatrix}\) is \(\Sigma = \varepsilon B\), that is

\[ \langle x^2\rangle = \varepsilon\beta_x, \qquad \langle x x'\rangle = -\varepsilon\alpha_x, \qquad \langle x'^2\rangle = \varepsilon\gamma_x \]

and the rms size is \(\sigma_x = \sqrt{\varepsilon\beta_x}\). The matrix \(\Sigma\) transforms like \(B\), \(\Sigma_2 = M\Sigma_1 M^T\), and since \(\det M = 1\), \(\det\Sigma = \varepsilon^2\) is unchanged. Conventions differ: some laboratories quote the emittance of the ellipse containing 95 per cent of the particles, or four times the rms value, and older papers include the factor \(\pi\) in the unit (“\(\pi\) mm mrad”). Always check which.

The conservation of \(\varepsilon\) is a case of Liouville’s theorem: for a Hamiltonian system, the density of points in phase space is constant along the motion, so a volume of phase space keeps its size (Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, section 46). The theorem is about canonical coordinates. For transverse motion these are \(x\) and the transverse momentum \(p_x\), and \(x' = p_x/p\). Without acceleration \(p\) is constant and the area in \((x, x')\) is conserved, as the determinant showed.

Adiabatic damping. Now accelerate. The cavities push along \(s\), so \(p_x\) is untouched while \(p\) grows. The area in \((x, p_x)\) is conserved, so the area in \((x, x')\) shrinks as \(1/p\):

\[ \varepsilon \propto \frac{1}{p} = \frac{1}{m c\,\beta\gamma} \]

Here \(\beta = v/c\) and \(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}\) are the relativistic factors of the particle, with \(p = \gamma mv = mc\,\beta\gamma\) (chapter 1, section 2.1); they have nothing to do with the Twiss \(\beta_x\) and \(\gamma_x\). The quantity conserved is the normalised emittance

\[ \varepsilon_n = \beta\gamma\,\varepsilon \]

The amplitude of each betatron oscillation shrinks as \(p^{-1/2}\), the result found for the betatron in chapter 11. It is also an adiabatic invariant in the sense of Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, section 49: the action of the oscillation is conserved when the parameters change slowly compared with one oscillation. In the LHC at 7 TeV (\(\gamma = 7460.5\)), the arc emittance \(\varepsilon = 5\times10^{-10}\) m quoted by Holzer corresponds to \(\varepsilon_n = 3.73\) μm. The same beam at the injection energy of 450 GeV (\(\beta\gamma = 479.6\)) has \(\varepsilon = 7.78\) nm. At \(\beta_x = 180\) m its size is 1.18 mm at injection and 0.30 mm at top energy: the beam shrinks by \(\sqrt{7000/450} = 3.94\) during the ramp.

The theorem conserves the six-dimensional volume. The two-dimensional rms emittance is conserved only while the planes are uncoupled and the forces are linear. Nonlinear fields filament the beam and increase its rms emittance; scattering and noise heat it; electrons radiate, which damps and excites it to an equilibrium (chapter 20); cooling reduces it by acting on the beam from outside (chapter 24).

10. Dispersion and momentum compaction

A particle of momentum \(p_0(1+\delta)\) obeys \(x'' + K_x x = \delta/\rho\). Its solution is the sum of a betatron oscillation about a shifted closed orbit and that orbit:

\[ x = x_\beta + D_x(s)\,\delta \]

where the dispersion function \(D_x\) (m) is the periodic solution of

\[ D_x'' + K_x(s)\,D_x = \frac{1}{\rho(s)} \]

It is the closed orbit of a particle with \(\delta = 1\), scaled down. It is found with the \(3\times3\) matrices of section 4: if \(M_3\) is the one-period matrix, the periodic dispersion satisfies \((D_x, D_x', 1)^T = M_3\,(D_x, D_x', 1)^T\), two linear equations. Vertically, in a flat ring, \(D_y = 0\) by design.

For the thin-lens FODO cell with the bending spread evenly along the cell, total bending angle \(\theta\) per cell, the periodic solution is

\[ D_{x,\max} = \frac{L\theta\left(1 + \frac{1}{2}\sin\frac{\mu}{2}\right)}{4\sin^2\frac{\mu}{2}}, \qquad D_{x,\min} = \frac{L\theta\left(1 - \frac{1}{2}\sin\frac{\mu}{2}\right)}{4\sin^2\frac{\mu}{2}} \]

at the F and D quadrupoles. These were checked here by solving the periodic condition numerically; the algebra is in Lee’s or Wiedemann’s textbook. Give the worked cell of section 6 six bending magnets of \(2\pi/1232 = 5.10\) mrad each, the bending angle of one of the LHC’s 1232 dipoles, so \(\theta = 30.6\) mrad. Then \(D_{x,\max} = 2.21\) m and \(D_{x,\min} = 1.06\) m.

Momentum compaction. An off-momentum particle follows a longer or shorter closed orbit. Along a curved reference orbit, a particle at \(x\) travels \(dl = (1 + x/\rho)\,ds\), the outer lane of a running track (figure 16.4: arcs of the same angle have lengths in the ratio of their radii, \((\rho + x)/\rho\)). Averaged over the betatron oscillation, \(x = D_x\delta\), so the circumference changes by

\[ \Delta C = \delta\oint\frac{D_x}{\rho}\,ds, \qquad \alpha_c = \frac{\Delta C/C}{\delta} = \frac{1}{C}\oint\frac{D_x}{\rho}\,ds \]

The momentum compaction \(\alpha_c\) is dimensionless.

In the smooth approximation, average the dispersion equation over the ring: \(K_x \to (Q_x/R)^2\) (the uniform focusing that gives wavenumber \(Q_x/R\), section 8) and \(1/\rho \to 2\pi/C = 1/R\) (the bends add up to one full turn, \(2\pi\), over the length \(C\)). A constant \(D_x\) has \(D_x'' = 0\), so \(D_x(Q_x/R)^2 = 1/R\), \(D_x \approx R/Q_x^2\), and, with \(\oint ds/\rho = 2\pi\) and \(C = 2\pi R\),

\[ \alpha_c \approx \frac{1}{R}\cdot\frac{R}{Q_x^2} = \frac{1}{Q_x^2} \]

For the PS with \(Q_x = 6.2\) this gives \(D_x \approx 2.60\) m and \(\alpha_c \approx 0.026\). The CERN impedance study uses 0.025 at injection and 0.027 at extraction. The estimate is good to a few per cent.

For a weak-focusing ring the same reasoning, with the constant \(K_x = (1-n)/R^2\) of section 3 and \(\rho = R\) everywhere, gives \(D_x(1-n)/R^2 = 1/R\), so \(D_x = R/(1-n)\) and \(\alpha_c = D_x/R = 1/(1-n)\), greater than one (chapter 15, section 6 found the same). With \(R = 100\) m and \(n = 0.5\), \(D_x = 200\) m: a momentum error of \(10^{-3}\) moves the orbit by 0.200 m, against 2.6 mm in the PS. That ratio, nearly 80, is more than the ratio of betatron amplitudes, \(\sqrt{141/16.1} = 2.96\). Most of the aperture saved by strong focusing is saved on the momentum spread.

11. Transition: the price of strong focusing

The revolution frequency of a particle is \(f_0 = \beta c/C\). A particle with more momentum is faster, and it also has a longer path. Chapter 15 (section 7) derives the balance of the two: the speed term is \(\Delta\beta/\beta = \delta/\gamma^2\) and the path term \(\Delta C/C = \alpha_c\delta\), so

\[ \frac{\Delta f_0}{f_0} = -\left(\alpha_c - \frac{1}{\gamma^2}\right)\delta = -\eta\,\delta \]

The phase-slip factor \(\eta = \alpha_c - 1/\gamma^2\) changes sign at the transition energy

\[ \gamma_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_c}} \]

Below transition (\(\gamma < \gamma_t\), \(\eta < 0\)) a particle with more momentum goes round sooner; above it, the longer path wins and it goes round later. At transition all momenta take the same time, the bunch has no longitudinal restoring force, and the stable phase must be moved from \(\phi_s\) between 0 and \(\pi/2\) to \(\phi_s\) between \(\pi/2\) and \(\pi\) in the sine convention of this course. Chapter 12 explains why, and chapter 19 what happens to the bunch while it crosses.

In a weak-focusing ring \(\alpha_c > 1\), so \(\eta > 0\) for every \(\gamma\), and there is no transition at all. In a strong-focusing ring \(\alpha_c \approx 1/Q_x^2\) is small, and

\[ \gamma_t \approx Q_x \]

Proton machines with tunes of a few units therefore have \(\gamma_t\) of a few units, inside their energy range. For the PS, \(\alpha_c\) between 0.025 and 0.027 gives \(\gamma_t\) between 6.32 and 6.09, a transition kinetic energy of 4.8 to 5.0 GeV. The PS injected at 50 MeV and accelerated to 24 GeV, so it had to cross. At 2 GeV it is below transition (\(\gamma = 3.13\), \(\eta = -0.077\)); at 25.4 GeV above (\(\gamma = 28.07\), \(\eta = +0.0257\)). It was feared before the machines were built, and the PS did falter at transition in November 1959 (see “How it was researched and built”). Electron machines are far above transition from the moment of injection and never meet it.

12. Chromaticity

A quadrupole bends a particle of momentum \(p_0(1+\delta)\) less, because its rigidity is larger: \(k \to k/(1+\delta) \approx k(1 - \delta)\) (the binomial series again, F1, section 8). The word chromaticity comes from optics, where a glass lens focuses each colour of light differently; here each momentum is a colour. A particle with more momentum is under-focused and has a lower tune. The tune change from a small change of focusing \(\Delta K(s)\) is, by the result derived at the bench (section “At the bench”, item 2),

\[ \Delta Q_x = \frac{1}{4\pi}\oint\beta_x\,\Delta K\,ds \]

With \(\Delta K = -k\delta\) in the quadrupoles, the natural chromaticity is

\[ Q_x' = \frac{dQ_x}{d\delta} = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\oint\beta_x\,k\,ds \]

and similarly for \(y\) with the sign of \(k\) reversed. (The dipoles’ weak focusing adds a smaller term, neglected here.) For the thin-lens FODO cell, \(\beta_x\) is \(\beta_{x,\max}\) at F and \(\beta_{x,\min}\) at D. A thin lens has \(\int k\,ds = kl = 1/f\), so the F lens (two halves, one at each end of the cell) contributes \(\beta_{x,\max}/f\) to the integral and the D lens \(-\beta_{x,\min}/f\). From section 6, \(\beta_{x,\max} - \beta_{x,\min} = 2L\sin\frac{\mu}{2}/\sin\mu\) and \(1/f = 4\sin\frac{\mu}{2}/L\), and \(\sin\mu = 2\sin\frac{\mu}{2}\cos\frac{\mu}{2}\), so

\[ Q_{x,\text{cell}}' = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\cdot\frac{\beta_{x,\max} - \beta_{x,\min}}{f} = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\cdot\frac{8\sin^2\frac{\mu}{2}}{\sin\mu} = -\frac{\tan\frac{\mu}{2}}{\pi} \]

Since the cell’s tune is \(\mu/(2\pi)\), the ratio is \(Q_x'/Q_x = -2\tan(\mu/2)/\mu\): \(-1.27\) at 90° and \(-1.10\) at 60°. The rule worth remembering is that the natural chromaticity of a strong-focusing ring is about \(-Q_x\) (and \(-Q_y\)). The PS, with combined-function magnets, has bare chromaticities of \(-5.0\) horizontally and \(-7.0\) vertically at injection tunes of 6.07 and 6.31, as used in the parameter tables of the CERN impedance study: ratios of \(-0.82\) and \(-1.11\).

Why it matters: a momentum spread of \(10^{-3}\) with \(Q_x' = -6\) spreads the tunes over 0.006, which can put part of the beam on a resonance, and a negative chromaticity above transition drives the head-tail instability, in which the fields left behind by the head of a bunch drive the oscillation of its tail (chapter 22). It is corrected with sextupoles placed where the dispersion separates the momenta, which is a nonlinear business for chapter 18.

13. Beam size and matching

A particle’s displacement is \(x_\beta + D_x\delta\), and the betatron part and the momentum deviation are uncorrelated in a matched beam, so the variances add, as they do for independent errors (chapter 1; F6, section 8):

\[ \sigma_x = \sqrt{\varepsilon\,\beta_x + D_x^2\,\sigma_\delta^2} \]

with \(\sigma_\delta\) the rms relative momentum spread. In the worked LHC-like cell at 7 TeV with \(\varepsilon = 0.5\) nm, the betatron part at the F quadrupole is \(\sqrt{0.5\times10^{-9}\times 182.5} = 0.30\) mm. If \(\sigma_\delta = 10^{-4}\), the dispersion part is \(2.21\times10^{-4}\) m, and the total is 0.37 mm. In a proton ring at injection, with a larger \(\sigma_\delta\), the dispersion part can dominate. The aperture is sized to some multiple of \(\sigma_x\) plus room for the closed orbit and its errors; Holzer gives a minimum of 18 \(\sigma\) inside the LHC’s final-focus quadrupoles.

Matching. A real machine is a chain of different sections: transfer lines, arcs, straight sections for injection and RF, low-\(\beta\) insertions at collision points (chapter 23). Each periodic section has its own periodic Twiss functions. Where two sections meet, \(\beta_x\), \(\alpha_x\), \(\beta_y\), \(\alpha_y\), \(D_x\) and \(D_x'\) must be continuous, or the beam coming from the first will not fit the second. Matching means choosing the strengths of a few quadrupoles between them so that \(B_2 = M B_1 M^T\) lands on the required values. Four conditions in \(\beta\) and \(\alpha\) need at least four independent quadrupoles, six with the dispersion. A dispersion suppressor at the end of an arc is a matching section that brings \(D_x\) and \(D_x'\) to zero for the straight section.

What does a mismatch cost? Suppose a beam with emittance \(\varepsilon_1\) and ellipse parameters \((\beta_{x1}, \alpha_{x1}, \gamma_{x1})\) is injected into a ring whose own parameters at that point are \((\beta_{x2}, \alpha_{x2}, \gamma_{x2})\). Each particle has, in the ring’s terms, the invariant \(\mathbf{x}^T G_2\mathbf{x}\). Its average over the injected beam is \(\operatorname{tr}(G_2\Sigma_1) = \varepsilon_1\operatorname{tr}(G_2 B_1)\). (Write it out: \(\mathbf{x}^TG_2\mathbf{x} = \gamma_{x2}x^2 + 2\alpha_{x2}xx' + \beta_{x2}x'^2\), whose average is \(\gamma_{x2}\langle x^2\rangle + 2\alpha_{x2}\langle xx'\rangle + \beta_{x2}\langle x'^2\rangle\), and that is the sum of the diagonal elements of \(G_2\Sigma_1\); with \(\Sigma_1 = \varepsilon_1B_1\) it is \(\varepsilon_1(\gamma_{x2}\beta_{x1} - 2\alpha_{x2}\alpha_{x1} + \beta_{x2}\gamma_{x1})\).) The particles keep their invariants but spread in phase, because their tunes differ slightly, until the beam fills the ring’s ellipse: it filaments. For a matched beam, \(\operatorname{tr}(G_2\cdot\varepsilon B_2) = 2\varepsilon\), because \(G_2B_2\) is the unit matrix, of trace 2: the average invariant of a filamented beam is twice its emittance (the average action, as in the distribution pre-requisite). Since the average invariant does not change while the beam filaments, the final emittance is half the average found above:

\[ \varepsilon_f = \varepsilon_1\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\beta_{x1}\gamma_{x2} - 2\alpha_{x1}\alpha_{x2} + \gamma_{x1}\beta_{x2}\right) \]

The factor is at least 1, with equality only when the ellipses agree. A beam with \(\alpha_{x1} = \alpha_{x2} = 0\) and \(\beta_{x1}\) 20 per cent too large loses 1.7 per cent of emittance; a factor of two too large costs 25 per cent. Injection matching is part of chapter 21.

Pre-requisite: Symplectic matrices

Pre-requisite. This section defines a symplectic matrix by the condition \(M^T\mathbf{S}M = \mathbf{S}\), shows that for one plane of motion it says exactly \(\det M = 1\), and that for two or three planes it says much more: it keeps the areas in each plane, not only the total volume, and it allows only the couplings a magnetic field can produce. It shows that symplectic matrices form a group, have determinant 1 and have eigenvalues in reciprocal pairs, so that no symplectic map can damp every motion. It ends with what this means for computing: a tracking code must step with symplectic maps, or it invents damping or growth. Section 14 of the chapter uses all of it. Taught from Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, section II.7; Penrose, The Road to Reality, sections 13.10 and 20.4; Tong, Classical Mechanics, section 10.4; and the Handbook of Accelerator Physics and Engineering, section 2.3.6 (Yoshida on symplectic integration).

The idea. For one plane, the chapter showed that every transfer matrix has \(\det M = 1\), so it keeps areas in \((x, x')\). With two planes the state is \((x, x', y, y')\). Is it enough that a \(4\times4\) matrix keep four-dimensional volume? No. A magnet lattice keeps something finer: for any small patch of particles, the sum of its area projected on the \((x, x')\) plane and its area projected on the \((y, y')\) plane. “Symplectic” is the name for maps that keep that sum, and it is exactly the property of the linear maps that come from a Hamiltonian.

The symplectic product. For two state vectors \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) in one plane, the signed area of the parallelogram they span is

\[ a_x b_{x'} - a_{x'} b_x = \mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{b}, \qquad \mathbf{S} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0\end{pmatrix} \]

(multiply it out). For two or three planes, \(\mathbf{S}\) is this block repeated along the diagonal, with the state ordered \((x, x', y, y', z, \delta)\), and \(\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{b}\) is the sum of the projected areas, one per plane. A matrix \(M\) keeps this for every pair of vectors when \((M\mathbf{a})^T\mathbf{S}(M\mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{b}\), that is \(\mathbf{a}^T(M^T\mathbf{S}M)\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{b}\) for all \(\mathbf{a}\), \(\mathbf{b}\), which means

\[ M^T\mathbf{S}\,M = \mathbf{S} \]

A matrix that satisfies this is called symplectic. Lee writes the same condition with the matrix called \(J\) and the state ordered \((x, y, p_x, p_y)\); the content is the same.

One plane: symplectic means determinant 1. Let \(M = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d\end{pmatrix}\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{S}M &= \begin{pmatrix} c & d \\ -a & -b\end{pmatrix}, \\ M^T\mathbf{S}M &= \begin{pmatrix} a & c \\ b & d\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} c & d \\ -a & -b\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & ad - bc \\ -(ad - bc) & 0\end{pmatrix} = (\det M)\,\mathbf{S} \end{aligned} \]

So in one plane the condition is \(\det M = 1\) and nothing else. Every matrix in section 4 of the chapter passes.

Two planes: more than volume. \(M^T\mathbf{S}M\) is always antisymmetric (its transpose is its negative, because \(\mathbf{S}^T = -\mathbf{S}\)), so the \(4\times4\) condition has six independent entries, against the single condition \(\det M = 1\). Two examples show what the extra conditions forbid.

  • Trading area between planes. Take \(M = \operatorname{diag}(A, D)\), the block-diagonal \(4\times4\) matrix with the \(2\times2\) block \(A\) acting on \((x, x')\), \(D\) on \((y, y')\) and zeros elsewhere, with \(A = \begin{pmatrix}2 & 0\\0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\) and \(D = \begin{pmatrix}0.5 & 0\\0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\). Its determinant is 1, so four-dimensional volume is kept, but it doubles every horizontal area and halves every vertical one. It is not symplectic: for an uncoupled matrix the condition is \(\det A = \det D = 1\), one plane at a time. A magnetic lattice cannot squeeze one plane by puffing up the other.
  • Coupling. A thin element that kicks \(x'\) by \(-a\,y\) and \(y'\) by \(-b\,x\) has the matrix with ones on the diagonal and \(-a\), \(-b\) in the \((x', y)\) and \((y', x)\) places. Its determinant is 1 for any \(a\) and \(b\). Multiplying out \(M^T\mathbf{S}M - \mathbf{S}\) leaves \(\pm(a - b)\) in two places, so it is symplectic only if \(a = b\). That is precisely what Maxwell’s equations require of a skew quadrupole’s field (the first pre-requisite of this chapter: the divergence condition makes the cross terms equal). The symplectic condition and the field equations say the same thing.

Properties. Three follow quickly.

  • A group. If \(M\) and \(N\) are symplectic, so is \(MN\): \((MN)^T\mathbf{S}(MN) = N^T(M^T\mathbf{S}M)N = N^T\mathbf{S}N = \mathbf{S}\). The inverse is symplectic too, and is easy: from \(M^T\mathbf{S}M = \mathbf{S}\), \(M^{-1} = \mathbf{S}^{-1}M^T\mathbf{S}\). So a lattice built of symplectic elements is symplectic, however long.
  • Volume. Taking determinants, \((\det M)^2\det\mathbf{S} = \det\mathbf{S}\), so \(\det M = \pm1\); Lee states that it is \(+1\) (proving the sign takes more than this one line). Symplectic maps keep phase-space volume, which is Liouville’s theorem for linear motion. The converse fails in four or more dimensions, as the first example showed.
  • Eigenvalues in reciprocal pairs. From \(M^{-1} = \mathbf{S}^{-1}M^T\mathbf{S}\), the inverse is similar to the transpose, so it has the same eigenvalues as \(M^T\), which are those of \(M\). But the eigenvalues of \(M^{-1}\) are \(1/\lambda\). So whenever \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue, so is \(1/\lambda\), and for a real matrix so are their complex conjugates. If one eigenvalue lies inside the unit circle, a partner lies outside. A symplectic one-turn map cannot shrink every motion; the most it can do is keep them all, with every eigenvalue on the unit circle, which is the stable case of section 5. Damping must come from outside the Hamiltonian (chapter 20).

Integrators. A tracking code follows particles for millions of turns, so it applies the same one-turn map millions of times, and any error that changes areas grows without limit. Take the oscillation \(x'' = -x\) (a betatron oscillation in units where its wavenumber is 1), stepped with \(h = 2\pi/20\), twenty steps per oscillation. Each method is a \(2\times2\) matrix applied once per step.

  • Explicit Euler (the method of F1, section 11), \(x \to x + h\,x'\) and \(x' \to x' - h\,x\) using the old values: \(M = \begin{pmatrix}1 & h\\-h & 1\end{pmatrix}\), \(\det M = 1 + h^2 = 1.0987\). Areas grow by that factor every step; the amplitude grows by 2.56 per oscillation and by \(7.5\times10^{40}\) in 100.
  • Classical fourth-order Runge-Kutta, which within each step evaluates the slope four times (at the start, twice at the middle and at the end) and averages them with weights 1, 2, 2, 1, so that its error per step is of order \(h^5\); for a linear equation the step is again a matrix. Far more accurate per step: \(\det M = 0.999987\). The amplitude falls by 12 per cent in 1,000 oscillations and to \(5\times10^{-58}\) of itself in a million. A proton beam would appear to cool itself.
  • Kick then drift, \(x' \to x' - h\,x\) and then \(x \to x + h\,x'\) using the new slope: the product of a kick matrix and a drift matrix, each of determinant 1, so \(\det M = 1\) exactly, \(M = \begin{pmatrix}1 - h^2 & h\\-h & 1\end{pmatrix}\). Its eigenvalues lie on the unit circle, and the amplitude stays bounded for ever (figure 16.8). Its error is in the phase: \(\cos\mu = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}M\) gives \(\mu = 0.31547\) per step against the exact 0.31416, a tune error of 0.4 per cent, and a slightly tilted invariant ellipse.

Figure 16.8. The oscillation \(x'' = -x\) stepped twenty times per oscillation by each method, from the per-step factors above: explicit Euler multiplies the amplitude by \((1 + h^2)^{1/2}\) every step, Runge-Kutta by \(0.9999934\), and kick-then-drift keeps it. Euler’s beam has grown \(10^{40}\) times by the hundredth oscillation. Runge-Kutta looks perfect for ten thousand oscillations, a few milliseconds in the PS, and then its beam shrinks away as if cooled: by a million oscillations the amplitude is \(5\times10^{-58}\) of what it was. Only the symplectic method keeps it, for ever.

The Handbook (section 2.3.6, Yoshida) puts it this way: ordinary integrators such as Euler’s and Runge-Kutta’s do not respect the symplectic structure, so after long integration they show damping or excitation that is not there. Symplectic ones keep an invariant close to the true one, so their energy error does not grow steadily. A thin-lens model of a lattice is exactly a kick-drift scheme: drifts between thin kicks, each symplectic. Yoshida’s method composes drifts and kicks with chosen weights to reach fourth or higher order while staying exactly symplectic, which is what tracking codes such as PTC inside MAD-X and Xsuite do.

Exercise S1. Show that the \(4\times4\) matrix of a thick quadrupole, \(M_F\) in \(x\) and \(M_D\) in \(y\) (section 4 of the chapter), is symplectic.

Answer

It is uncoupled, \(\operatorname{diag}(M_F, M_D)\), so the condition is \(\det M_F = \det M_D = 1\). \(\det M_F = \cos^2\phi + \sin^2\phi = 1\) and \(\det M_D = \cosh^2\phi - \sinh^2\phi = 1\). A numerical check with \(K = 0.05\) m\(^{-2}\) and \(l = 1\) m gives \(M^T\mathbf{S}M = \mathbf{S}\) to rounding.

Exercise S2. For the thin coupling kick above, write \(M\) and compute \(M^T\mathbf{S}M - \mathbf{S}\). What must hold, and what field does it correspond to?

Answer

With the state \((x, x', y, y')\), \(M\) is the identity plus \(M_{23} = -a\) and \(M_{41} = -b\). Multiplying out, \(M^T\mathbf{S}M - \mathbf{S}\) has \(b - a\) in place \((1, 3)\) and \(a - b\) in place \((3, 1)\), and zeros elsewhere; with \(a = 0.1\), \(b = 0\) the numerical product shows \(\mp0.1\) there. Exercise S3. With explicit Euler, how many steps per oscillation would keep the amplitude growth below 10 per cent over 1,000 oscillations?

Answer

The amplitude grows by \((1 + h^2)^{1/2}\) per step, and there are \(2\pi\times1000/h\) steps, so \(\ln(\text{growth}) \approx (2\pi\times1000/h)(h^2/2) = 1000\pi h\). Setting this to \(\ln 1.1 = 0.0953\) gives \(h = 3.0\times10^{-5}\): about 207,000 steps per oscillation. The kick-drift method stays bounded with twenty. Symplecticity, not step size, is the cure.

Pre-requisite: Canonical transformations and generating functions

Pre-requisite. This section says what a canonical transformation is, a change of the phase-space variables that keeps Hamilton’s equations in their form, and proves the test that section 14 of the chapter quotes: a change of variables is canonical exactly when its Jacobian matrix is symplectic at every point. It then shows that the motion of a particle through any lossless lattice, linear or not, is itself a canonical transformation, which is why transfer matrices are symplectic and nonlinear maps have symplectic Jacobians. It ends with generating functions, the way such transformations are built in practice, and uses one to produce the action-angle variables that section 9 uses. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, section 45; Tong, Classical Mechanics, sections 10.4, 10.4.1 and 10.4.3; Schwichtenberg, No-Nonsense Classical Mechanics, sections 7.3.1 to 7.3.4; and Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, section II.4.

What it is. In Lagrangian mechanics we may use any coordinates we like; the equations keep their form. Hamilton’s equations treat positions and momenta as independent variables, and this allows a wider freedom: new variables that mix positions and momenta,

\[ X = X(x, p), \qquad P = P(x, p) \]

The change is called canonical if the motion, written in \((X, P)\), again obeys Hamilton’s equations, \(X' = \partial\tilde{\mathcal{H}}/\partial P\) and \(P' = -\partial\tilde{\mathcal{H}}/\partial X\), for some new Hamiltonian \(\tilde{\mathcal{H}}\). Not every change qualifies (Landau and Lifshitz, section 45). We have already met two that do: the action-angle variables \((\phi, J)\) of the action-angle pre-requisite, and, as shown below, the motion itself.

The test. Use one plane and a change that does not depend on \(s\). Collect the variables into \(\mathbf{z} = (x, p)^T\). Hamilton’s equations are then one line,

\[ \mathbf{z}' = \mathbf{S}\,\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\mathcal{H}, \qquad \mathbf{S} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\-1 & 0\end{pmatrix} \]

since the first row gives \(x' = \partial\mathcal{H}/\partial p\) and the second \(p' = -\partial\mathcal{H}/\partial x\). Let \(\mathbf{Z} = (X, P)^T\) and let \(\mathcal{J}\) be the Jacobian matrix, \(\mathcal{J}_{ij} = \partial Z_i/\partial z_j\). By the chain rule the new variables move as \(\mathbf{Z}' = \mathcal{J}\mathbf{z}'\), and the gradient changes as \(\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\mathcal{H} = \mathcal{J}^T\nabla_{\mathbf{Z}}\mathcal{H}\). Put these together:

\[ \mathbf{Z}' = \mathcal{J}\,\mathbf{S}\,\mathcal{J}^T\,\nabla_{\mathbf{Z}}\mathcal{H} \]

This has Hamilton’s form, with the same \(\mathcal{H}\) written in the new variables, exactly when \(\mathcal{J}\mathbf{S}\mathcal{J}^T = \mathbf{S}\), which is equivalent to \(\mathcal{J}^T\mathbf{S}\mathcal{J} = \mathbf{S}\) (the first gives \(\mathcal{J}^T = \mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathcal{J}^{-1}\mathbf{S}\); put that into the second and use \(\mathbf{S}^2 = -I\), \(\mathbf{S}^{-1} = -\mathbf{S}\)). So:

A change of variables is canonical when its Jacobian matrix is symplectic at every point of phase space.

In one plane this says \(\det\mathcal{J} = 1\): the change keeps areas. Tong (section 10.4) states it with Poisson brackets, \(\{X, P\} = 1\), which in one plane is the same determinant. For several planes it is the full symplectic condition. If the change depends on \(s\), the Jacobian test is the same, and the new Hamiltonian gains an extra term, given below by the generating function.

The motion is canonical. Follow the particle for a short step \(h\) along \(s\). To first order, \(\mathbf{z} \to \mathbf{z} + h\,\mathbf{S}\nabla\mathcal{H}\). The Jacobian of this step is \(I + h\,\mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{H}\), where \(\mathbf{H}\) is the matrix of second derivatives of \(\mathcal{H}\), which is symmetric. Test it:

\[ (I + h\mathbf{S}\mathbf{H})^T\,\mathbf{S}\,(I + h\mathbf{S}\mathbf{H}) = \mathbf{S} + h\left(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{S}^T\mathbf{S} + \mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{H}\right) + O(h^2) = \mathbf{S} + h\left(\mathbf{H} - \mathbf{H}\right) + O(h^2) \]

using \(\mathbf{S}^T\mathbf{S} = I\) and \(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S} = -I\). The first-order change vanishes. Each short step is canonical, and a product of symplectic Jacobians is symplectic, so the map from any \(s_1\) to any \(s_2\) is a canonical transformation. Tong (section 10.4.1) makes the same point: the Hamiltonian generates the motion as a continuous family of canonical transformations.

For linear motion the map is the transfer matrix, which is its own Jacobian: so every transfer matrix of a lossless lattice is symplectic, and in one plane has determinant 1. For nonlinear motion the map is not a matrix, but its Jacobian, the matrix that maps a small neighbourhood of one particle, is symplectic at every point.

At work: a sextupole. A thin sextupole of integrated strength \(ml\) kicks the slope by \(\Delta x' = -\tfrac12 ml\,x^2\) (in the midplane). Its Jacobian is

\[ \mathcal{J} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -ml\,x & 1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det\mathcal{J} = 1 \]

a different matrix at every \(x\), but always of determinant 1. With \(ml = 0.5\) m\(^{-2}\), a particle at 1 mm gets a kick of 0.25 µrad, and the Jacobian’s corner element is \(-5\times10^{-4}\) m\(^{-1}\): near that particle the sextupole acts as a weak lens of focal length 2 km. The sextupole kick bends the shape of a beam (the filamentation of chapter 7), but it keeps its area. A tracking code that represents sextupoles by exact kicks between drifts stays symplectic; one that truncates a Taylor expansion of a thick sextupole’s map generally does not, and drifts over long runs.

Generating functions. Checking a Jacobian tells whether a given change is canonical. To build one, use a single function. Landau and Lifshitz (section 45) start from the action principle: the motion makes \(\int(p\,dx - \mathcal{H}\,ds)\) stationary. If the new variables are to obey Hamilton’s equations, the same must hold for \(\int(P\,dX - \tilde{\mathcal{H}}\,ds)\). Both hold if the two integrands differ by the total derivative of some function \(F\), since adding \(dF\) to an integrand changes the integral only by end values, which are fixed:

\[ p\,dx - \mathcal{H}\,ds = P\,dX - \tilde{\mathcal{H}}\,ds + dF \]

Take \(F = F_1(x, X, s)\), a function of the old and new coordinates. Then \(dF = (\partial F_1/\partial x)dx + (\partial F_1/\partial X)dX + (\partial F_1/\partial s)ds\), and matching the coefficients of \(dx\), \(dX\) and \(ds\):

\[ p = \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial x}, \qquad P = -\frac{\partial F_1}{\partial X}, \qquad \tilde{\mathcal{H}} = \mathcal{H} + \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial s} \]

Any function \(F_1\) produces a canonical transformation this way. Other types use other pairs of variables, such as \(F_2(x, P)\); Schwichtenberg (section 7.3.4) and Tong (section 10.4.3) list them.

At work: action-angle variables from one function. Lee (section II.4) uses

\[ F_1(x, \phi, s) = -\frac{x^2}{2\beta_x}\left(\tan\phi + \alpha_x\right) \]

with \(x'\) as the momentum and \(\phi\) as the new coordinate. The first rule gives \(x' = \partial F_1/\partial x = -(x/\beta_x)(\tan\phi + \alpha_x)\), which is the ratio of the chapter’s \(x'\) and \(x\) in terms of the phase. The second gives the new momentum:

\[ J = -\frac{\partial F_1}{\partial\phi} = \frac{x^2}{2\beta_x}\left(1 + \tan^2\phi\right) \]

Use the first rule to write \(\tan\phi = -(\alpha_x x + \beta_x x')/x\); then \(x^2(1 + \tan^2\phi) = x^2 + (\alpha_x x + \beta_x x')^2\), and

\[ J = \frac{x^2 + (\alpha_x x + \beta_x x')^2}{2\beta_x} = \tfrac12\left(\gamma_x x^2 + 2\alpha_x xx' + \beta_x x'^2\right) \]

the action of the action-angle pre-requisite, half the Courant-Snyder invariant. The third rule, with the \(s\)-dependence of \(\beta_x\) and \(\alpha_x\) and Hill’s equation, gives Lee’s \(\tilde{\mathcal{H}} = J/\beta_x\).

Exercise C1. Is the change \(X = x\cos\theta + p\sin\theta\), \(P = -x\sin\theta + p\cos\theta\), with \(\theta\) a constant, canonical? Where have you met it?

Answer

Its Jacobian is a rotation matrix, \(\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & \sin\theta\\-\sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\), of determinant 1, so yes. In the normalised coordinates \((u, v)\) of the distribution pre-requisite, one turn of a ring of tune \(Q\) is exactly this rotation, with \(\theta = 2\pi Q\).

Exercise C2. Is \((x, p_x) \to (x, x')\), with \(x' = p_x/p\), canonical? What follows for the area in \((x, x')\) when \(p\) changes?

Answer

The Jacobian is \(\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\0 & 1/p\end{pmatrix}\), of determinant \(1/p\), so it is canonical only in the units where \(p = 1\). While \(p\) is constant, dividing the Hamiltonian by \(p\) restores Hamilton’s form in \((x, x')\), which is why the chapter can treat \((x, x')\) as a canonical pair between cavities. Areas in \((x, x')\) are areas in \((x, p_x)\) divided by \(p\); the latter are conserved, so the former fall as \(1/p\) when the beam is accelerated: adiabatic damping.

Exercise C3. Which of these maps of \((x, x')\) can come from a lossless lattice: (a) \(x' \to x' - \kappa x^3\); (b) \(x' \to x' - \kappa x'\); (c) \(x \to x + l\,x'\)?

Answer

(b) Jacobian \(\operatorname{diag}(1, 1 - \kappa)\), determinant \(1 - \kappa\): no, it is a damping, which needs a dissipative process such as radiation or cooling. 14. Symplecticity

The determinant condition is the two-dimensional case of a deeper rule. For the full state \((x, p_x, y, p_y, z, \delta)\) the linear map \(M\) of any lossless magnetic lattice satisfies

\[ M^T\mathbf{S}\,M = \mathbf{S}, \qquad \mathbf{S} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0\end{pmatrix}\ \text{repeated along the diagonal} \]

This is the statement that the motion is a canonical transformation (Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, section 45). In two dimensions it is exactly \(\det M = 1\); in four and six dimensions it is a stronger set of conditions, and it implies that phase-space volume is conserved. It holds for nonlinear maps too, in the form that the Jacobian matrix of the map is symplectic at every point.

It matters in computation. A tracking code that follows particles for millions of turns must use maps that are exactly symplectic. A map that is slightly off, as a general-purpose numerical integrator produces, damps or excites the motion artificially, and the long-term behaviour it predicts is wrong. Lattice codes use thin-lens kicks and symplectic integrators for that reason.

There were costs. The machine has a transition to cross. Many more resonances lie near a working point of 6.2 than near 0.7. These were the worries of the 1950s, and they were well founded.

How it was researched and built

The discovery and the priority. The Brookhaven paper of 1952 laid out the principle, the stability condition for alternating sectors of large positive and negative field index, and the example design. In the same issue of Physical Review, John Blewett showed that the same principle would focus a linear accelerator radially, with alternating quadrupole fields; quadrupole focusing has been the norm in linacs since (chapter 14). When Christofilos’s patent application came to light, the four authors acknowledged his priority in a letter, “Origin of the ‘strong-focusing’ principle” (Physical Review 91, 202, 1953, received 27 April 1953). Christofilos was offered a post at Brookhaven and worked there from 1953 to 1956, before moving to Livermore.

The visitors from CERN. Odd Dahl, Frank Goward and Rolf Wideroe visited Brookhaven in the summer of 1952, soon after the idea was found; Livingston had begun the study that led to it in preparation for their visit (Livingston and Blewett, section 15-1). Hearing the new idea, they abandoned their plan for a 10 GeV weak-focusing machine in favour of a 25 GeV alternating-gradient synchrotron of about the same price (Wilson 1996). In 1953 CERN fixed the energy of its machine at 25 GeV. After Goward’s death and Dahl’s departure, John Adams was appointed director of the PS division in 1954 (CERN Archives).

The worries. Three problems had to be faced before anyone would build one. The first was tolerances: with strong lenses, a small misalignment or gradient error gives a large orbit distortion. Adams and Mervyn Hine made precise alignment and magnet measurement central to the PS. The magnets sit on a free-floating concrete ring 200 m across, carried on springs because the soft Molasse rock beneath shifts with the seasons (Livingston and Blewett, section 15-8), in a tunnel whose temperature is held to within about a degree. The second was resonances: John Lawson pointed out that machines with many periods and high tunes are especially susceptible to nonlinear resonances, which pushed designers to moderate tunes. The third was transition: Kjell Johnsen, in Bergen in 1953, showed that it could be crossed. John and Hildred Blewett worked with Dahl and Johnsen in Bergen on the first prototype high-gradient synchrotron magnet before moving to Geneva in the autumn of 1953. Adams, impressed by the difficulty, advised the British laboratory to keep weak focusing for Nimrod, and the cautious built weak-focusing machines for some years after.

The first strong-focusing synchrotron. The first ring to use alternating gradients was an electron synchrotron of Robert Wilson’s group at Cornell, who adopted the principle without hesitation (Wilson 1996). They began a synchrotron of about 1.4 GeV in 1952; planned with a constant gradient, it was given alternating-gradient pole faces, came into operation in 1954, and was developed to deliver 1.1 GeV electrons, 1.2 GeV in research use by 1959. Livingston and Blewett call it the first practical alternating-gradient accelerator (sections 12-1 and 15-9), and Lee the first strong-focusing electron accelerator. Electrons are relativistic from the start, so the machine had no transition to cross, and a small machine was a cheap test of whether the alignment tolerances could be met.

The CERN PS. The PS lattice is made of 100 combined-function magnets, each 4.4 m long, in ten superperiods (a superperiod is the largest section of the ring that repeats exactly, here ten magnets with their straight sections; the ring is ten copies of it). Each magnet consists of two half-units with gradients of opposite sign (figure 16.10), so every magnet bends and the lattice focuses through the alternation of the gradients. Between the magnets are 80 short straight sections of 1.6 m and 20 of 3 m for the RF cavities, correction lenses and injection and extraction. The circumference is \(200\pi\) m, 628.3 m. The bending radius follows: 100 magnets of 4.4 m cover 440 m, so \(\rho = 440/(2\pi) = 70.0\) m, and the ring is 70 per cent bending. At injection the 50 MeV protons of Linac 1 have \(B\rho = 1.04\) T m, a field of 14.8 mT. At 25.4 GeV, \(B\rho = 87.8\) T m and the field is 1.25 T. The total magnet weight is about 4,000 tonnes. The tunes are adjusted with pole-face windings on the main magnets: thin coils laid on the pole surfaces whose currents add a small, adjustable gradient.

In one half-unit the gap closes outward, so the field rises with radius and the half focuses horizontally (F); in the other the gap opens outward, the field falls, and the half defocuses horizontally and focuses vertically (D). Both halves bend: the whole 4.4 m magnet is bending field, and the focusing comes from the alternation.

The linac delivered the first turn of 50 MeV protons round the PS in September 1959. During commissioning that autumn the beam was lost at transition until Wolfgang Schnell built a circuit in a coffee tin: by Wilson’s account, to switch the RF phase at the moment of transition; by Vretenar’s, a radial loop for the RF. On 24 November 1959 the beam was accelerated to a kinetic energy of 24 GeV. The PS was the first strong-focusing proton synchrotron. Over fifty years its intensity per pulse rose from about \(10^{10}\) to \(3\times10^{13}\) protons, and it still runs, at up to 26 GeV, as an injector of the CERN chain (chapter 21).

The Brookhaven AGS. The Alternating Gradient Synchrotron followed. It has 240 magnets whose gradients alternate inward and outward, in 12 superperiods of 20, and a circumference of 807 m. It reached its design energy of 33 GeV in July 1960 and was the highest-energy accelerator in the world until the Soviet U-70 at Serpukhov started in 1967 (Wilson 1996; Brookhaven’s own history says until 1968). Experiments there found the muon neutrino, CP violation and the J particle, each recognised with a Nobel prize.

The theory. Courant and Snyder’s “Theory of the alternating-gradient synchrotron” (Annals of Physics 3, 1, 1958) set out the theory of this chapter: the betatron function, the invariant, the matrix formalism, the effects of errors and the resonances. The Twiss parameters are also called the Courant-Snyder parameters after it.

Separated functions. The PS and the AGS used combined-function magnets. Robert Wilson’s design for the Fermilab Main Ring separated the functions, with pure dipoles for bending and pure quadrupoles for focusing (figure 16.11); this packs more bending field into the ring and lets the optics be tuned independently of the bending. Almost every large ring since has done the same, with FODO arcs and quadrupoles on separate power supplies. The PS Booster, added later as an injector to the PS, used separated functions with quadrupole triplets for that freedom.

Quadrupoles focus and dipoles bend, each on its own supply, so the optics can be tuned without touching the bending.

How a lattice is designed today. The work is done with lattice codes. Karl Brown at SLAC set out the first- and second-order matrix theory of beam lines (Advances in Particle Physics 1, 71, 1968) and wrote TRANSPORT, the standard beam-line program of the 1960s and 1970s. At CERN, MAD (Methodical Accelerator Design) became the standard for rings; MAD-X, its successor after MAD-8, was first released in June 2002, with the Polymorphic Tracking Code of E. Forest inside it for symplectic tracking. CERN now describes MAD-X as frozen and is moving its models to Xsuite, which works with MAD-NG. Elsewhere, Michael Borland’s elegant is widely used for electron machines. A designer chooses the cell (usually FODO for arcs, with phase advance between 60° and 90°), sets the dipole field and the number of cells from the energy and the tunnel, adds the straight sections and their matching sections, then lets the code fit quadrupole strengths to target tunes, \(\beta\) values and dispersion. The code computes the closed orbit and Twiss functions with the matrices of this chapter, and then tracks particles through the nonlinear fields to check that the linear design survives them.

Pre-requisite (continued): Sampling, discrete Fourier transform, FFT, aliasing

Pre-requisite (continued). Chapter 8 showed that a BPM samples the beam once a turn, that the discrete Fourier transform of \(N\) turns resolves the fractional tune only to about one bin, \(1/N\), and that sampling once a turn hides the integer part and cannot tell \(\nu\) from \(1 - \nu\). This section adds the step that item 1 of “At the bench” uses to do much better than one bin: interpolating the peak between bins, from the shape of the transform of a sinusoid, with and without a window. Taught from Lee, Accelerator Physics, appendix B, section I.2 (the discrete Fourier transform, its resolution and aliasing) and chapter 2, section III.5 B (measuring the tune); the Handbook of Accelerator Physics and Engineering, section 4.7.8.1 (tune measurement); and the Fourier transforms of chapter 8.

The shape of one line. Let a BPM record \(x_n = e^{2\pi i\nu n}\) for \(n = 0, 1, \ldots, N - 1\), a pure oscillation of fractional tune \(\nu\) (a cosine is half of this plus half of its mirror at \(-\nu\)). The discrete Fourier transform at bin \(k\) is

\[ X_k = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} e^{2\pi i\nu n}\,e^{-2\pi i kn/N} = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} e^{2\pi i d\,n/N}, \qquad d = N\nu - k \]

where \(d\) is the distance from bin \(k\) to the true line, measured in bins. This is a geometric series with ratio \(e^{2\pi id/N}\), so

\[ |X_k| = \left|\frac{1 - e^{2\pi i d}}{1 - e^{2\pi i d/N}}\right| = \frac{|\sin\pi d|}{|\sin(\pi d/N)|} \approx N\,\frac{|\sin\pi d|}{\pi|d|} \]

using \(|1 - e^{i\theta}| = 2|\sin(\theta/2)|\) and, for \(|d| \ll N\), \(\sin(\pi d/N) \approx \pi d/N\). The line has the sinc shape of chapter 8’s rectangular profile: \(N\) records make a window of length \(N\) in time, and its transform is a sinc of width one bin. If \(\nu\) falls exactly on a bin (\(d = 0\)) all the signal is in that bin; otherwise it leaks into the neighbours.

Two bins tell where the line is. Let \(k\) be the highest bin and write the true position as \(N\nu = k + \delta\), with \(0 \le \delta < 1\) if the right-hand neighbour is the larger one. The two bins sit at distances \(d = \delta\) and \(d = \delta - 1\), and \(|\sin\pi(\delta - 1)| = \sin\pi\delta\), so

\[ |X_k| \approx \frac{N\sin\pi\delta}{\pi\delta}, \qquad |X_{k+1}| \approx \frac{N\sin\pi\delta}{\pi(1 - \delta)}, \qquad r = \frac{|X_{k+1}|}{|X_k|} = \frac{\delta}{1 - \delta} \]

The unknown amplitude and the sine cancel in the ratio. Solving,

\[ \delta = \frac{r}{1 + r}, \qquad \nu = \frac{k + \delta}{N} \]

If the left neighbour is the larger, use it and subtract.

A window. The sinc falls slowly, as \(1/d\), so a strong line leaks into bins far from it, and the mirror line of a cosine at \(-\nu\) and any noise disturb the two bins used. Multiplying the record by a window that goes smoothly to zero at both ends, such as the Hann window \(w_n = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12\cos(2\pi n/N)\), makes the line wider (two bins) but its tails fall as \(1/d^3\). The Hann window is a sum of three exponentials, so its transform is a sum of three shifted sincs; adding them gives a line of shape \(\sin(\pi d)/[\pi d(1 - d^2)]\), up to a constant. The same two-bin ratio then reads \(r = (1 + \delta)/(2 - \delta)\), so

\[ \delta = \frac{2r - 1}{1 + r} \]

At work. Take \(N = 256\) and a cosine with \(\nu = 80.3/256 = 0.313672\). Without a window the four bins from 79 to 82 have magnitudes 25.8, 110.3, 46.7 and 18.9. The peak is bin 80, \(r = 0.423\), \(\delta = 0.297\), and \(\nu = 0.313661\): an error of \(1.1\times10^{-5}\), against \(1.2\times10^{-3}\) for the bin alone. With the Hann window the bins are 18.4, 60.4, 46.2 and 5.1; \(r = 0.765\), \(\delta = 0.30000\), \(\nu = 0.3136719\), right to \(10^{-9}\).

How good is it in general? For clean cosines with random tunes between 0.20 and 0.25, the largest error found in 50 trials was:

turns \(N\) highest bin two bins, no window two bins, Hann

The bin alone is good to half a bin, \(1/(2N)\). The interpolated estimates improve much faster than \(1/N\). Real records are not clean: they decay as the beam decoheres (the distribution pre-requisite of this chapter) and carry BPM noise. In the chapter’s test, 1,024 turns of a decaying, noisy record with true \(\nu = 0.2316\) gave 0.23145 from the highest bin, 0.23176 by the plain two-bin formula and 0.23162 with the Hann window. Decay and noise, not the method, set the limit, at around \(10^{-4}\) to \(10^{-5}\) here. Methods that fit the frequency directly to the record, such as the numerical analysis of fundamental frequencies (NAFF) used in frequency-map analysis (Handbook, section 2.3.7, on dynamic aperture), go further for clean tracking data.

Pre-requisite (continued): Least-squares fitting

Pre-requisite (continued). Chapter 8 taught least squares: why the sum of squared, weighted misfits \(\chi^2\) is minimised, the normal equations \((A^TWA)\mathbf{p} = A^TW\mathbf{y}\), the parameters’ variances and covariances as the elements of \((A^TWA)^{-1}\), why a quadrupole scan must span the minimum, and that three points fit a parabola exactly and leave nothing to check. This section adds what items 4 and 5 of “At the bench” need beyond that: how large \(\chi^2\) should be, so that a fit with more points than unknowns becomes a test of the model; what that test cannot see; and how a model with many parameters that enter nonlinearly, such as a ring’s quadrupole gradients, is fitted to a measured orbit response matrix by repeated linear fits, the method behind LOCO. Taught from the Handbook of Accelerator Physics and Engineering, section 4.7.6 (Safranek, on modelling a storage ring from orbit measurements) and Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, section II.5 C (emittance measurement). The rule for the size of \(\chi^2\) is standard statistics that none of the course’s books develops; the numbers below come from simulations made for this section.

The setting. As in chapter 8, \(N\) measurements \(y_i\) with error bars \(\sigma_i\) are described by a model with \(n\) unknown parameters, linear in them: \(\mathbf{y} \approx A\mathbf{p}\). In the quadrupole scan, \(y_i = \sigma_{\text{scr},i}^2\), the parameters are \((\Sigma_{11}, \Sigma_{12}, \Sigma_{22})\), and row \(i\) of \(A\) is \(\big((1 - dq_i)^2,\ 2(1 - dq_i)d,\ d^2\big)\) with \(q_i = 1/f_i\). The fit minimises \(\chi^2 = \sum_i [(y_i - (A\mathbf{p})_i)/\sigma_i]^2\).

More measurements than unknowns. With \(N = n\) the model can always pass exactly through every point: \(\chi^2 = 0\), whatever the data. The fit then tells you nothing about whether the model is right. With \(N > n\) it cannot, in general, and the leftover misfit is a test. If the model is right and the error bars are honest, \(\chi^2\) is on average equal to the number of degrees of freedom, \(N - n\), with a spread of about \(\sqrt{2(N - n)}\). Much larger means a wrong model or underestimated errors; much smaller means overestimated errors.

At work: the quadrupole scan with seven points. Take the beam of the chapter’s exercise 9 (\(\Sigma_{11} = 5.0\times10^{-8}\) m², \(\Sigma_{12} = 5.0\times10^{-9}\) m rad, \(\Sigma_{22} = 1.0\times10^{-9}\) rad², a 5 m drift) and measure the size at seven values of \(1/f\) from 0 to 0.36 m\(^{-1}\), through the waist, each with a 2 per cent error in size (4 per cent in \(\sigma^2\)). One simulated scan gives \(\Sigma_{11} = (5.28 \pm 0.19)\times10^{-8}\), \(\Sigma_{12} = (5.17 \pm 0.14)\times10^{-9}\), \(\Sigma_{22} = (9.97 \pm 0.22)\times10^{-10}\), so \(\varepsilon = 5.09\) nm, \(\beta_x = 10.4\) m and \(\alpha_x = -1.02\), with \(\chi^2 = 2.0\) for 4 degrees of freedom: a good fit. Repeated 2,000 times, the emittance comes out at \(5.00 \pm 0.10\) nm. Three points would have fitted exactly and given no warning at all of a bad point.

What \(\chi^2\) cannot see. Now let the screen have a resolution of 50 µm that nobody allowed for: every measured \(\sigma^2\) is too large by \((50\ \mu\text{m})^2\). A constant added to \(\sigma^2\) is itself a parabola in \(1/f\): it is exactly a change of \(\Sigma_{22}\) by \(r^2/d^2 = 1.0\times10^{-10}\). So the fit absorbs it perfectly, \(\chi^2\) stays at zero for noise-free data, and the emittance comes out at 5.48 nm, 9.5 per cent too large. A fit checks that the data have the shape of the model, not that the model contains all the physics. Effects that mimic the model must be measured separately.

Many parameters: the response-matrix fit. Item 5 of “At the bench” fits a ring’s model to its measured orbit response matrix: the change of orbit at every BPM for a kick from every corrector. The model’s response \(R_{ij}(\mathbf{k})\) depends on all the quadrupole strengths \(\mathbf{k}\), and not linearly. The method, in Safranek’s description in the Handbook, is to make it linear one step at a time.

  1. Start from the design strengths \(\mathbf{k}_0\) and compute the model matrix \(R(\mathbf{k}_0)\).
  2. Change each parameter \(k_m\) in turn by a small amount and recompute, to get the derivatives \(\partial R_{ij}/\partial k_m\).
  3. Treat every element of the measured matrix as one data point. To first order, \(R^{\text{meas}}_{ij} - R_{ij}(\mathbf{k}_0) \approx \sum_m(\partial R_{ij}/\partial k_m)\,\Delta k_m\), a linear model in the corrections \(\Delta k_m\). Solve it by least squares.
  4. Update \(\mathbf{k}\) and repeat until \(\chi^2\) stops falling (the Gauss-Newton method).

The real programs also fit each BPM’s gain and each corrector’s calibration, since those also scale the matrix.

At work: a small ring. A model ring of eight FODO cells of 10 m (tune 1.80) has 16 thin quadrupoles, each with a BPM and a corrector beside it, so the response matrix has \(16\times16 = 256\) elements and there are 16 unknown gradient errors: 240 degrees of freedom. Give the quadrupoles random gradient errors of 1 per cent rms, and add measurement noise of \(10^{-3}\) m/rad to each element (0.1 µm of orbit for a 0.1 mrad kick, the precision the Handbook quotes for averaged closed orbits). Before fitting, \(\chi^2\) per degree of freedom is in the tens of thousands: errors of 1 per cent move the response by some 0.3 m/rad, hundreds of times the noise. The first linear step brings it down to a few tens, not to 1, because over a 1 per cent change the response is not quite linear in the gradients. The second step brings it to about 0.9, and further steps change nothing. The fitted gradient errors then match the true ones to about 0.006 per cent rms, as the covariance matrix predicts: for example a true error of \(-0.891\) per cent was found as \(-0.893\) per cent. The Handbook’s Table 1 gives an rms uncertainty of 0.04 per cent for the quadrupole gradients of the NSLS ring, with real BPMs and with gains and calibrations also free.

When two parameters affect the data in nearly the same way (two quadrupoles side by side, say), \(A^TWA\) is nearly singular and the fit can return large, opposite corrections. The cure is more independent data, or a constraint that penalises large corrections; the Handbook notes that such constrained fits give a model that reproduces the real machine reliably.

Exercise L1. The seven-point scan has 4 degrees of freedom. What range of \(\chi^2\) is unremarkable, and what would \(\chi^2 = 20\) suggest?

Answer

The mean is 4 and the spread about \(\sqrt{8} = 2.8\), so values from about 1 to 9 are unremarkable. \(\chi^2 = 20\) means the misfit is about \(\sqrt{20/4} = 2.2\) times the stated errors: either the errors are underestimated (beam jitter, for instance) or the model is wrong (a non-Gaussian beam, a thick-lens effect, dispersion at the screen).

Exercise L2. A light source has 100 BPMs, 80 correctors and 120 quadrupoles. Fitting all gradients, BPM gains and corrector calibrations, how many data points and unknowns does the response-matrix fit have?

Answer

Data: \(100\times80 = 8{,}000\) matrix elements (in one plane). Unknowns: \(120 + 100 + 80 = 300\). Degrees of freedom: 7,700. The large surplus is what lets the fit find each gradient separately, and it lets \(\chi^2\) test the model.

Exercise L3. Show that an unknown screen resolution \(r\) is indistinguishable from a change of \(\Sigma_{22}\) in a scan with a single screen, and suggest a way to tell them apart.

Answer

The measured value is \(\sigma^2 + r^2\), and \(r^2 = d^2(r^2/d^2)\) is the \(\Sigma_{22}\) term with \(\Sigma_{22}\) raised by \(r^2/d^2\). Every scan point shifts in the same way as for a beam with a larger divergence, so no fit can separate them. A second screen at a different drift \(d_2\) would see the same \(r^2\) but a \(\Sigma_{22}\) term scaled by \(d_2^2\); or the resolution can be measured directly, for instance with a beam much smaller than \(r\).

At the bench

The linear model is a set of predictions: tunes, betatron functions, phase advances and dispersion at each monitor. Commissioning and operation consist largely of measuring them and making the machine agree. The instruments are those of chapter 8: beam position monitors (BPMs) at most quadrupoles, which read the beam’s centroid on each turn to a fraction of a millimetre; kicker magnets; screens and wire scanners for the beam size; and the controls of the quadrupole and RF power supplies.

1. Measuring the tune. Kick the beam once with a fast dipole and record its position at one BPM on every turn. The record is \(x_n = A\cos(2\pi\nu_x n + \phi)\) for turns \(n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots\), where \(\nu_x\) is the fractional part of \(Q_x\): the BPM sees the oscillation once a turn, so the integer part is invisible. A fast Fourier transform of \(N\) turns shows a peak at \(\nu_x\) with a resolution of \(1/N\): 0.00098 for 1024 turns, which take 2.15 ms in the PS at top energy and 91 ms in the LHC. Interpolating the peak between bins does much better. In a test made here with a simulated record of 1024 turns, true \(\nu_x = 0.2316\), decaying and noisy, the highest bin was at 0.2314 and the interpolated peak at 0.2316 (figure 16.12). A single BPM cannot tell \(\nu_x\) from \(1 - \nu_x\); a second BPM a known phase advance away, or a small change of a quadrupole and the direction in which the peak moves, settles it. The integer part comes from the pattern of the kicked orbit round the whole ring: count the oscillations.

Figure 16.12. Measuring the tune, from the simulated record of the text: a beam kicked once, read at one BPM every turn, decaying and noisy, with true fractional tune 0.2316. Top: the first 60 of 1024 turns. Bottom: the spectrum of all 1024 (a discrete Fourier transform with a Hann window). The highest bin is at 0.2314; interpolating between bins puts the peak at 0.2316.

The signal decays as the particles, with slightly different tunes, drift out of phase (decoherence), and in an electron ring through radiation damping. A kick large enough to see also blows up a hadron beam’s emittance. So operating machines use gentler methods: an AC dipole, a kicker driven with a steady oscillation just off the tune, so that the beam follows it in a large forced oscillation without being blown up (F5, section 5); a phase-locked loop, a feedback circuit that excites the beam very gently and keeps adjusting its own frequency to stay on the beam’s resonance, so tracking the tune continuously (Wenninger quotes an accuracy of \(10^{-5}\) or better); or the Schottky spectrum, the noise signal that an unexcited beam makes because it is a finite number of particles, which has lines at the betatron frequencies (noise and its spectrum: chapter 8).

Failure signatures: a tune near an integer or a half-integer shows as a closed orbit or a beam size that blows up, and the beam is lost in the first turns; a wrong integer tune in the model makes orbit correction fail to converge; two peaks in each plane mean the planes are coupled, and the minimum distance the tunes can approach each other measures the coupling.

2. Measuring \(\beta_x\) by changing one quadrupole. Change the strength of one quadrupole of length \(l\) by \(\Delta k\). As a thin lens it adds \(\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -\Delta k\,l & 1\end{pmatrix}\) at that point. The new one-turn matrix there is this times the old one, \(M' = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -\Delta k\,l & 1\end{pmatrix}M\), and its trace is

\[ \operatorname{tr} M' = \operatorname{tr} M - \Delta k\,l\,M_{12} = 2\cos 2\pi Q_x - \Delta k\,l\,\beta_x\sin 2\pi Q_x \]

Set \(\operatorname{tr} M' = 2\cos 2\pi(Q_x + \Delta Q_x) \approx 2\cos 2\pi Q_x - 4\pi\Delta Q_x\sin 2\pi Q_x\) (to first order, \(\cos(a + b) \approx \cos a - b\sin a\) for small \(b\), F1, section 5). The first form of the trace uses \(\operatorname{tr}M = 2\cos\mu\) and \(M_{12} = \beta_x\sin\mu\) from section 8, with \(\mu = 2\pi Q_x\); and the product adds \(-\Delta k\,l\,M_{12}\) to the bottom-right element of \(M\) and leaves the top-left alone. The sines cancel, and

\[ \Delta Q_x = \frac{\beta_x\,\Delta k\,l}{4\pi} \]

where \(\beta_x\) is the betatron function at the quadrupole (its average over the quadrupole for a thick one). So measuring the tune change gives \(\beta_x\) at that quadrupole. With \(\beta_x = 20\) m and \(\Delta k\,l = 10^{-3}\) m\(^{-1}\), \(\Delta Q_x = 0.0016\), well within reach of a tune measurement. For larger changes, or tunes near a half-integer, use the exact form \(\cos 2\pi(Q_x + \Delta Q_x) = \cos 2\pi Q_x - \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta k\,l\,\beta_x\sin 2\pi Q_x\) and solve for \(\beta_x\); at \(Q_x = 6.23\) the two agree to \(10^{-6}\) for this example. The same sum over all quadrupoles gave the chromaticity formula of section 12. The method, called k-modulation when the strength is varied periodically, needs quadrupoles on their own power supplies, common in light sources and rare in large rings. It is also used to find the offset between a BPM and the magnetic centre of the quadrupole beside it: the orbit moves when the quadrupole strength changes unless the beam passes through its centre.

3. Measuring dispersion by changing the RF frequency. The beam is locked to the RF, so its revolution frequency is \(f_{RF}/h\), with \(h\) the harmonic number, the whole number of RF periods in one turn (chapter 15, section 4). Change \(f_{RF}\) by a small fraction and the beam must change its revolution frequency by the same fraction, which by section 11 it does by changing its momentum:

\[ \delta = -\frac{1}{\eta}\,\frac{\Delta f_{RF}}{f_{RF}} \]

Record the closed orbit at every BPM before and after; the dispersion at each BPM is \(D_x = \Delta x/\delta\). In the PS at 25.4 GeV, with \(\eta = 0.0257\), a change of \(10^{-5}\) in the RF frequency shifts the momentum by \(-3.9\times10^{-4}\) and moves the orbit by about \(-1.0\) mm where \(D_x = 2.6\) m. The method needs \(\eta\), from the model or from the measured synchrotron tune. Measured vertical dispersion, which should be zero in a flat ring, reveals rolled quadrupoles and vertical orbit errors. The same physics works the other way: Wenninger shows the LHC’s circumference changed by about 1 mm peak to peak over 26.7 km with the Earth tides, and the orbit and energy followed.

4. Measuring emittance with a quadrupole scan. In a transfer line, put a screen or wire scanner a drift \(d\) downstream of a quadrupole and record the rms beam size \(\sigma_{\text{scr}}\) while stepping the quadrupole’s strength, written as the inverse focal length \(1/f = kl\). With the thin-lens matrix the transfer to the screen is \(\begin{pmatrix}1 - d/f & d \\ -1/f & 1\end{pmatrix}\), and from \(\Sigma_{\text{scr}} = M\Sigma M^T\):

\[ \sigma_{\text{scr}}^2 = \left(1 - \frac{d}{f}\right)^2\Sigma_{11} + 2\left(1 - \frac{d}{f}\right)d\,\Sigma_{12} + d^2\,\Sigma_{22} \]

a parabola in \(1/f\). Fit a parabola to the measured \(\sigma_{\text{scr}}^2\); its three coefficients give \(\Sigma_{11}\), \(\Sigma_{12}\) and \(\Sigma_{22}\) at the quadrupole, and \(\varepsilon = \sqrt{\Sigma_{11}\Sigma_{22} - \Sigma_{12}^2}\), \(\beta_x = \Sigma_{11}/\varepsilon\), \(\alpha_x = -\Sigma_{12}/\varepsilon\). The curve has a minimum, the waist on the screen, and the scan must go through it. The method assumes no dispersion at the screen (or subtracts \(D_x^2\sigma_\delta^2\)), negligible space charge, a thin quadrupole, and a screen whose resolution is small compared with the smallest beam. Exercise 9 runs an example.

5. Checking the lattice against its model. Three methods are in use, as surveyed by Wenninger for the CERN Accelerator School.

  • Tunes against settings. The first check on a new machine: set the model tunes and measure them. A ring that comes out a few tenths off has a gradient error, a calibration error or a wrong polarity somewhere.
  • Orbit response. Kick the beam with each orbit corrector in turn and record the change of the closed orbit at every BPM. The response of BPM \(i\) to corrector \(j\) is \(\sqrt{\beta_{x,i}\beta_{x,j}}\cos(\lvert\psi_i - \psi_j\rvert - \pi Q_x)/(2\sin\pi Q_x)\) times the kick, so the matrix of responses contains the betatron functions and phases everywhere. A fitting program (LOCO is the best known) adjusts the model’s quadrupole gradients, BPM gains and corrector calibrations until the model reproduces the measured matrix; the fitted gradient errors are then corrected. This is standard in synchrotron light sources, with hundreds of BPMs and correctors.
  • Turn-by-turn phase advance. Excite the beam, record a few thousand turns at all BPMs synchronised to the same turn, and extract the phase of the oscillation at each BPM. The phase advance between neighbours does not depend on BPM calibration. In an LHC example, two consecutive phase advances were 50.9° and 39.1°. From three BPMs the betatron functions can be reconstructed. The ratio \((\beta_{x,\text{meas}} - \beta_{x,\text{model}})/\beta_{x,\text{model}}\) is the beta-beating; a single gradient error makes it oscillate round the ring at twice the betatron frequency, and the pattern points to the error.

The failure of 1959. The first proton strong-focusing machine showed the price in section 11 on its first ramp. In the PS commissioning of November 1959, the beam survived injection and acceleration to transition and was lost there until Schnell’s circuit brought it through. Crossing transition still needs fast, accurately timed changes of the RF phase, and in modern high-intensity operation a fast change of the lattice that moves \(\gamma_t\) past the beam (a gamma jump) and a change of the sign of the chromaticity.

Where it stops

Within its assumptions, linear optics has no limit: any momentum can be bent and focused if the magnets scale with \(B\rho\). The machines stop for other reasons.

The first is the field. The energy of a ring is set by \(p = qB\rho\), and iron saturates at about 2 T (chapter 17). The PS at 25.4 GeV needs 1.25 T on a 70 m bending radius. A 1 TeV/c proton ring at 2 T needs a bending radius of 1.67 km, before allowing for quadrupoles and straight sections. Fermilab’s Main Ring ran at 400 GeV, and reached 500 GeV in 1976, with iron magnets; superconducting magnets, as in Fermilab’s Tevatron, were the way beyond.

The second is the sensitivity that strong focusing brings. Orbit distortion from a misaligned quadrupole grows with the strength of the lenses and with \(1/\sin\pi Q_x\); the beam becomes unstable at integer and half-integer tunes and is disturbed near many higher resonances; the sextupoles that correct chromaticity are themselves nonlinear and limit the stable amplitude, the dynamic aperture. None of this is in the linear theory. Chapter 18 takes it up.

The third is the beam itself. At high intensity the beam’s own fields shift the tunes and drive instabilities (chapter 22), and electrons radiate, so that their emittance is set not by Liouville’s theorem but by a balance of damping and quantum excitation (chapter 20). For colliders the lattice must also squeeze \(\beta\) to centimetres at the collision points (chapter 23).

Exercises

1. On 24 November 1959 the PS accelerated protons to a kinetic energy of 24 GeV. Find the momentum, the rigidity, and the field in the bending magnets, given that 100 magnets of 4.4 m make up the full circle of bending.

Answer

\(E = W + m_pc^2 = 24.938\) GeV, \(pc = \sqrt{E^2 - (m_pc^2)^2} = 24.92\) GeV, so \(p = 24.92\) GeV/\(c\) and \(B\rho = 3.3356 \times 24.92 = 83.1\) T m. The bending radius is \(\rho = 440\ \text{m}/2\pi = 70.0\) m, so \(B = 83.1/70.0 = 1.19\) T.

2. Two thin quadrupoles of focal length \(\pm 2\) m are placed 0.5 m apart. Find the focal length of the pair in each plane, and show that it does not depend on which lens comes first.

Answer

With \(f_1 = f\), \(f_2 = -f\): \(1/F = 1/f - 1/f + d/f^2 = d/f^2\), so \(F = 4/0.5 = 8\) m. In the other plane the order is reversed, \(f_1 = -f\), \(f_2 = f\). The first two terms still cancel, and \(f_1 f_2 = -f^2\) in both orders, so \(-d/(f_1 f_2) = d/f^2\) either way. Both planes have \(F = 8\) m. Multiplying the matrices \(M_{\text{lens}}(-2)\,M_{\text{drift}}(0.5)\,M_{\text{lens}}(2)\) gives \(M_{21} = -0.125\) m\(^{-1}\), so \(F = 8\) m, and the reversed product gives the same.

3. A FODO cell is 106.9 m long and has a phase advance of 90°. Find the focal length of its quadrupoles in the thin-lens approximation, and \(\beta_{x,\max}\) and \(\beta_{x,\min}\). Compare with the 180 m quoted for the LHC arcs.

Answer

\(\sin 45° = L/(4f)\) gives \(f = 106.9/(4 \times 0.7071) = 37.8\) m. \(\beta_{x,\max} = L(1 + 0.7071)/\sin 90° = 182.5\) m and \(\beta_{x,\min} = L(1 - 0.7071) = 31.3\) m. The thin-lens estimate is within 1.4 per cent of 180 m.

4. Show that \(\beta_{x,\max}/L\) for a thin-lens FODO cell is smallest near \(\mu = 76°\), and find its value there, at 60° and at 90°.

Answer

Write \(u = \sin(\mu/2)\); then \(\sin\mu = 2u\sqrt{1-u^2}\) and \(\beta_{x,\max}/L = (1+u)/(2u\sqrt{1-u^2})\). Minimising numerically gives \(\mu = 76.3°\) and \(\beta_{x,\max}/L = 1.665\). At 60° the value is 1.732 and at 90° it is 1.707. The minimum is shallow, which is why cells between 60° and 90° are all reasonable choices.

5. The PS has \(C = 628.3\) m and horizontal tune near 6.2. Use the smooth approximation to estimate the mean \(\beta_x\), the mean dispersion, the momentum compaction, \(\gamma_t\) and the kinetic energy of transition. Compare with the measured \(\alpha_c\) of 0.025 to 0.027.

Answer

\(R = 100.0\) m. \(\beta_x \approx R/Q_x = 16.1\) m; \(D_x \approx R/Q_x^2 = 2.60\) m; \(\alpha_c \approx 1/Q_x^2 = 0.0260\); \(\gamma_t \approx Q_x = 6.2\), so \(W_t = (\gamma_t - 1)m_pc^2 = 4.9\) GeV. From the measured range, \(\gamma_t = 1/\sqrt{\alpha_c} = 6.09\) to 6.32 and \(W_t = 4.8\) to 5.0 GeV. The smooth estimate sits inside the measured range.

6. For a ring of 100 m radius, compare the closed-orbit displacement of a particle with \(\delta = 10^{-3}\) in a weak-focusing machine with \(n = 0.5\) and in a strong-focusing machine with \(Q_x = 6.2\). Which is larger, this ratio or the ratio of betatron amplitudes for equal emittance?

Answer

Weak: \(D_x = R/(1-n) = 200\) m, so \(D_x\delta = 0.200\) m. Strong: \(D_x = R/Q_x^2 = 2.60\) m, so \(2.6\) mm. The ratio is 77. Betatron amplitudes scale as \(\sqrt{\beta_x}\): \(\sqrt{141.4/16.1} = 2.96\). The momentum term is by far the larger gain.

7. A quadrupole where \(\beta_x = 20\) m is strengthened by \(\Delta k\,l = 10^{-3}\) m\(^{-1}\). Find the tune change, and check the approximation against the exact trace formula for \(Q_x = 6.23\).

Answer

\(\Delta Q_x = 20 \times 10^{-3}/(4\pi) = 0.00159\). Exactly: \(\cos 2\pi(Q_x + \Delta Q_x) = \cos 2\pi Q_x - \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta k\,l\,\beta_x\sin 2\pi Q_x\) gives a new fractional tune 0.231591, against \(0.23 + 0.001592 = 0.231592\) from the approximation.

8. The LHC’s normalised emittance is about 3.73 μm (from \(\varepsilon = 0.5\) nm at 7 TeV). Find the geometric emittance and rms size at \(\beta_x = 180\) m at the injection energy of 450 GeV and at 7 TeV.

Answer

\(\beta\gamma = 479.6\) at 450 GeV and 7460.5 at 7 TeV. \(\varepsilon = 3.73\ \mu\text{m}/479.6 = 7.78\) nm at injection, giving \(\sigma_x = \sqrt{7.78\times10^{-9}\times180} = 1.18\) mm; at 7 TeV, \(\varepsilon = 0.50\) nm and \(\sigma_x = 0.30\) mm. The ratio is \(\sqrt{7000/450} = 3.94\).

9. In a quadrupole scan with a drift of 5 m to the screen, the rms beam size is 0.354 mm with the quadrupole off, 0.202 mm at \(1/f = 0.15\) m\(^{-1}\) and 0.112 mm at \(1/f = 0.30\) m\(^{-1}\). Find the emittance and the Twiss parameters at the quadrupole. (The numbers are illustrative, not from a particular machine.)

Answer

Each measurement gives one linear equation \(\sigma^2 = (1 - d/f)^2\Sigma_{11} + 2(1 - d/f)d\,\Sigma_{12} + d^2\Sigma_{22}\). Solving the three: \(\Sigma_{11} = 5.0\times10^{-8}\) m², \(\Sigma_{12} = 5.0\times10^{-9}\) m rad, \(\Sigma_{22} = 1.0\times10^{-9}\) rad². Then \(\varepsilon = \sqrt{\Sigma_{11}\Sigma_{22} - \Sigma_{12}^2} = 5.0\) nm, \(\beta_x = \Sigma_{11}/\varepsilon = 10.0\) m and \(\alpha_x = -\Sigma_{12}/\varepsilon = -1.0\): the beam is diverging as it enters the quadrupole. With only three points the fit has no check; a real scan takes ten or more through the minimum.

10. A one-turn matrix at a point in a ring is measured or computed as

\[ M = \begin{pmatrix} -0.3707 & 15.874 \\ -0.07751 & 0.6214 \end{pmatrix} \]

with lengths in metres. Find the fractional tune, \(\beta_x\) and \(\alpha_x\). What can you not find from \(M\) alone?

Answer

\(\cos\mu = (-0.3707 + 0.6214)/2 = 0.1254\), so \(\mu = 82.8°\) or \(-82.8°\), fractional tune 0.230 or 0.770. Since \(M_{12} > 0\) and \(\beta_x > 0\), \(\sin\mu > 0\), so the fractional tune is 0.230. Then \(\beta_x = 15.874/\sin\mu = 16.0\) m and \(\alpha_x = (-0.3707 - 0.6214)/(2\sin\mu) = -0.50\). The integer part of the tune cannot be found from \(M\): a tune of 6.23 and one of 0.23 give the same matrix. (The matrix was built here from \(Q_x = 6.23\), \(\beta_x = 16\) m, \(\alpha_x = -0.5\); its determinant is 1.)
