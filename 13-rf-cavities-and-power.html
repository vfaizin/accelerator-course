13 RF cavities and power sources

1937 to today: Hansen’s resonator and the Varians’ klystron at Stanford, Randall and Boot’s magnetron at Birmingham, copper cavities fed by klystrons, niobium cavities from Stanford to CEBAF, TESLA and the European XFEL; the wall is that valves and coils could not give megawatts at the gigahertz frequencies an electron linac needs.

In one page

The wall. An electron is near the speed of light within a few MeV, so its resonant cells (chapter 9) are half a wavelength long; cells of centimetres mean gigahertz. In the 1930s nothing gave real power there. A valve’s electrons take about a nanosecond to cross its grid gap, a whole period at 1 GHz, and coils that small radiate and burn their own current.

The idea. Close the circuit into a metal box: the capacitor becomes two end plates, the coil the cylindrical wall joining them. The electric field sits on the axis, where the beam passes; current flows in a skin a micrometre thick. Then make the power with electrons that use their transit time: in the klystron (Russell and Sigurd Varian, with William Hansen, Stanford, 1937) a first cavity ripples the beam’s velocity, fast electrons catch slow ones in a drift, and the bunches drive a second cavity.

The equations to remember. For a pillbox of radius \(a\) and length \(L\) (m), conductivity \(\sigma\) (S/m), frequency \(f\) (Hz), \(\eta_0 = \sqrt{\mu_0/\varepsilon_0} = 376.7\ \Omega\):

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{010} &= \frac{2.405\,c}{2\pi a}, \qquad \delta = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega\mu_0\sigma}}, \qquad R_s = \frac{1}{\sigma\delta} \\ Q_0 &= \frac{\omega U}{P}, \qquad R_{sh} = \frac{\hat{V}^2}{P}, \qquad \frac{R}{Q} = \frac{\hat{V}^2}{\omega U}, \qquad G = Q_0 R_s \\ \frac{1}{Q_L} &= \frac{1}{Q_0} + \frac{1}{Q_{ext}}, \qquad \beta_c = \frac{Q_0}{Q_{ext}}, \qquad \Gamma = \frac{\beta_c - 1}{\beta_c + 1} \\ f &= 1.64\,\mathcal{E}_K^2\,e^{-8.5/\mathcal{E}_K} \quad (f \text{ in MHz}, \mathcal{E}_K \text{ in MV/m, Kilpatrick}) \\ R_{BCS} &\propto \frac{f^2}{T}\,e^{-\Delta/k_B T} \end{aligned} \]

Here \(U\) is the stored energy (J), \(P\) the wall power (W), \(\hat{V}\) the voltage a particle feels (V), \(Q_L\) and \(Q_{ext}\) the loaded and external quality factors, \(\beta_c\) the coupling factor, \(\Gamma\) the reflection coefficient at resonance, \(\Delta\) the superconducting energy gap. In this chapter \(\delta\) is the skin depth and \(\sigma\) the electrical conductivity: no momentum deviation, cross-section or beam size appears.

Numbers worth knowing by heart. Copper at 1.3 GHz: skin depth 1.83 µm, \(R_s = 9.4\) mΩ. A copper pillbox cell at 2856 MHz is 80 mm across, has \(Q_0 = 18\,400\), \(R/Q = 196\ \Omega\), and needs 277 kW to hold 1 MV. A 1.3 GHz nine-cell niobium TESLA cavity has \(R/Q = 1036\ \Omega\) and \(G = 270\ \Omega\); at 2 K, \(Q_0 \approx 10^{10}\), and 23.6 MV/m over its 1.038 m costs 58 W in the walls. The same shape in copper would need 20 MW. The Kilpatrick field is 18.5 MV/m at 352 MHz and 45.8 MV/m at 2856 MHz; clean copper does better by a factor of 1 to 2. Niobium’s surface field cannot exceed about 200 to 240 mT, which in the TESLA shape is 47 to 56 MV/m.

Pre-requisite (continued): Vacuum tubes and amplifiers

Pre-requisite. This continues Vacuum tubes and amplifiers from chapter 6, which gave the triode’s law and its three small-signal numbers, and its continuation in chapter 9, which gave the triode’s gain into a resonator and the two limits on its frequency, the electrons’ transit time across the grid gap and the resonance of the valve’s own leads. Three things are added here: why a tetrode has a second grid, what the classes A, B and C of operation mean and how efficient each can be, and the hydrogen thyratron, the gas-filled switch that fires a pulsed modulator. The next section and section 9 use them. Taught from Brennan, in the Handbook, section 7.3.3 (tetrode amplifiers), and the Handbook’s section 6.18.1 (thyratrons).

The trouble with one grid. In a triode the anode and the control grid face each other across the electron stream, so there is a capacitance \(C_{ag}\) (F) between them. In an amplifier the anode swings in the opposite sense to the grid and much harder: \(\tilde{V}_a = -A\tilde{V}_g\), with \(A\) the voltage gain (chapter 9 found \(A = 18.5\) for an illustrative triode on a resonator). The current through \(C_{ag}\) is driven by the difference of the two voltages:

\[ \tilde{I} = j\omega C_{ag}(\tilde{V}_g - \tilde{V}_a) = j\omega C_{ag}(1 + A)\,\tilde{V}_g . \]

Seen from the grid, \(C_{ag}\) looks \(1 + A\) times larger (the Miller effect). With an illustrative \(C_{ag} = 5\) pF and \(A = 18.5\), the grid circuit sees 97.5 pF, a reactance of 8.2 Ω at 200 MHz: the driver must push large reactive currents just to move the grid. Worse, the same capacitance carries a fraction of the anode’s RF back to the grid. With a tuned circuit at the grid and another at the anode, that is a feedback path, and if its phase is right the stage oscillates by itself (the Barkhausen condition of chapter 9).

The screen grid. The tetrode puts a second grid, the screen, between the control grid and the anode. It is held at a fixed positive DC voltage and tied to the cathode for RF through a capacitor, so its RF voltage is zero. Field lines from the anode now end on the screen instead of on the control grid, and \(C_{ag}\) falls by a large factor (about 20 for high-power tubes, Brennan says). Electrons still pass through the gaps of the screen on their way to the anode. A second gain follows: the field at the cathode, which sets the space-charge-limited current, is now fixed by the control grid and the screen, and the anode hardly reaches it. The anode current barely depends on the anode voltage, so the tetrode acts as a current source controlled by the grid voltage. Brennan writes its law as \(I_a = k(V_g + V_s/\mu_s + V_a/\mu_a)^{3/2}\), with \(V_s\) the screen voltage and \(\mu_a \gg \mu_s\).

Classes of operation. The DC bias on the control grid decides for how much of each RF cycle the tube conducts. Let the anode supply be \(V_{dc}\) and let the anode’s RF voltage swing with amplitude \(V_{a0}\) about it; it cannot swing below zero, so \(V_{a0} \le V_{dc}\). The RF power delivered is half the product of the amplitude of the fundamental (the part of the anode current at \(\omega\)) and \(V_{a0}\); the supply delivers \(V_{dc}\) times the mean current. The fundamental’s amplitude is twice the average of \(I_a(t)\cos\omega t\) over a cycle, because the average of \(\cos^2\omega t\) is \(\tfrac12\) and the averages of \(\cos\omega t\) times any other harmonic vanish (the Fourier series is taught in full before section 7).

  • Class A. The tube conducts the whole cycle: \(I_a = I_q + I_1\cos\omega t\) with \(I_1 \le I_q\). At best \(P_{RF} = \tfrac12I_qV_{dc}\) against \(P_{dc} = I_qV_{dc}\): an efficiency of 50 per cent at full swing, much less below it. It is linear, but it wastes half the power even with no signal.
  • Class B. The grid is biased at cut-off, so the tube conducts for half of each cycle and the current is a train of half-sine pulses of peak \(I_p\). Its mean is \(I_p/\pi\) and its fundamental \(I_p/2\). At full swing the efficiency is \(\tfrac12(I_p/2)V_{dc}/(V_{dc}I_p/\pi) = \pi/4 = 78.5\) per cent.
  • Class C. The grid is biased beyond cut-off, so current flows only near the top of the drive. Take the current pulse as the top of a cosine, \(I_a = I_p(\cos\omega t - \cos\theta)/(1 - \cos\theta)\) for \(|\omega t| < \theta\) and zero otherwise, with \(\theta\) (rad) the half-angle of conduction. With \(x = \omega t\), the mean is \(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\theta}^{\theta}I_a\,dx\), and \(\int_{-\theta}^{\theta}(\cos x - \cos\theta)\,dx = 2\sin\theta - 2\theta\cos\theta\). The fundamental is twice the average of \(I_a\cos x\), \(\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\theta}^{\theta}I_a\cos x\,dx\), and \(\int_{-\theta}^{\theta}(\cos^2x - \cos\theta\cos x)\,dx = (\theta + \sin\theta\cos\theta) - 2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \theta - \sin\theta\cos\theta\), using \(\cos^2x = \tfrac12(1 + \cos2x)\) (F1, section 3). Dividing by \(1 - \cos\theta\) gives

\[ \frac{\bar{I}_a}{I_p} = \frac{\sin\theta - \theta\cos\theta}{\pi(1 - \cos\theta)}, \qquad \frac{I_1}{I_p} = \frac{\theta - \sin\theta\cos\theta}{\pi(1 - \cos\theta)}, \qquad \eta = \frac{1}{2}\frac{I_1}{\bar{I}_a}. \]

For \(\theta = 90°\) this is class B again. For \(\theta = 60°\), \(\eta = 89.7\) per cent; for \(30°\), 97.3 per cent, but the fundamental is then only \(0.215I_p\), so the tube must pass large peak currents for little output.

Why does a short pulse waste less? The power dissipated in the tube at each instant is \(I_aV_a\). The anode voltage is lowest exactly when the current flows, at the bottom of its swing, so a current that flows only then dissipates little. The price is distortion: the current is rich in harmonics. A high-Q cavity filters them out, so for a fixed amplitude this does not matter; but class C responds badly to small drive, and machines whose cavity voltage must be controlled over a wide range run between A and B (class AB). For the chapter, the useful fact is that in class B or C the tube draws current only when driven, so its DC current, and its waste, fall with the RF output. That is the inductive output tube’s advantage over a klystron, which draws its full beam whatever the output.

The hydrogen thyratron. A pulse modulator needs a switch that holds off tens of kilovolts, then closes in nanoseconds and carries kiloamperes. The thyratron is a hot-cathode tube filled with hydrogen (or deuterium) at a fraction of a millibar, with a grid between cathode and anode. Its gap and pressure put it on the left-hand branch of the Paschen curve (chapter 4): an electron crossing the gap meets too few molecules to start an avalanche, so it holds off the voltage while the grid is negative. A positive pulse on the grid lets electrons from the cathode into the anode region; they ionise the gas, a plasma fills the tube in tens of nanoseconds (the Handbook gives 20 to 50 ns for most tubes), and it then conducts with a drop of a few hundred volts. Once the plasma has formed, the grid is wrapped in a sheath and has lost control: the thyratron is a closing switch only. It opens when the current stops and the gas deionises. That suits a line-type modulator, whose pulse-forming network empties itself and ends the current on its own (section 9, and the transmission-line section before section 7). Hydrogen’s light ions clear quickly after the pulse, so the tube recovers fast enough for repetition rates up to the kilohertz range.

Exercise A1. A tetrode reduces \(C_{ag}\) from 5 pF to 0.25 pF. With the same gain of 18.5, what capacitance does the driver see at the grid, and what reactance at 200 MHz?

Answer

\(0.25\ \text{pF}\times19.5 = 4.9\) pF, a reactance of \(1/(2\pi\times2\times10^8\times4.9\times10^{-12}) = 163\ \Omega\), twenty times higher than the triode’s 8.2 Ω. The grid now draws a twentieth of the reactive current, and the anode feeds back a twentieth as much signal.

Exercise A2. Show that half-sine pulses \(I_a = I_p\cos\omega t\) for \(|\omega t| < \pi/2\), zero otherwise, have mean \(I_p/\pi\) and fundamental \(I_p/2\).

Answer

With \(x = \omega t\), the mean is \(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}I_p\cos x\,dx = I_p/\pi\). The fundamental is twice the average of \(I_a\cos x\): \(2\cdot\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}I_p\cos^2x\,dx = \frac{I_p}{\pi}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2} = I_p/2\). (Setting \(\theta = \pi/2\) in the class C formulas gives the same.)

Exercise A3. A pulse-forming network charged to 40 kV discharges through a thyratron into a matched 5 Ω load. What current does the thyratron carry, what power reaches the load, and what fraction is lost in a forward drop of 200 V?

Answer

A matched network delivers half its charging voltage, 20 kV (shown in the transmission-line section before section 7), so the current is \(20\ \text{kV}/5\ \Omega = 4\) kA and the load receives 80 MW. The thyratron dissipates \(200\ \text{V}\times4\ \text{kA} = 0.8\) MW during the pulse, 1 per cent of the power delivered.

What resonance demanded

Chapter 9 showed that a resonant accelerator’s cell must be \(\beta\lambda/2\) long, with \(\lambda = c/f_{RF}\). For a heavy ion at a few MeV, \(\beta\) is small and a few megahertz will do. For an electron, \(\beta \to 1\) after a few MeV, and a cell of \(\lambda/2\) at 10 MHz is 15 m. To build a compact electron accelerator one wants cells of centimetres, so frequencies of gigahertz. Higher frequency also pays in power, as section 5 below shows: the shunt impedance per metre of a copper structure grows as \(\sqrt{f}\).

Three things blocked the way in the early 1930s.

The valve. The triode and tetrode work by letting a grid voltage control a stream of electrons. The electrons must cross from cathode to grid in a small fraction of a period. Chapter 9 estimated a transit of 1 ns for a 2 mm gap at 100 V: 1 per cent of a period at 10 MHz, 20 per cent at 200 MHz, a whole period at 1 GHz. Beyond that the grid no longer controls the current in step with the signal, and power gain collapses.

The circuit. A coil and capacitor resonate at \(1/\sqrt{LC}\). To go up in frequency both must shrink. A small coil carries the same current in a thinner skin of copper (section 3), so its loss rises; its leads become a fair fraction of a wavelength and radiate. The resonator stops being a lumped circuit.

The line. Power had to be carried from source to load. Lord Rayleigh had solved, in 1897, the problem of waves in a hollow conducting tube and shown that they travel only in particular modes above a cutoff frequency. George Southworth at Bell Telephone Laboratories (1932 to 1936) and Wilmer Barrow at MIT (by March 1936) independently made the hollow waveguide practical. This gave a low-loss way to carry gigahertz power, if anyone could make it.

Who stood at the wall

At Stanford, the physicist William Webster Hansen (died 1949) studied closed metal resonators in the 1930s. His paper “A type of electrical resonator” (1938) analysed what amounts to a cavity: a hollow conductor whose resonant mode stores energy in its volume and loses it only in a thin skin of its walls. The Varians called Hansen’s resonator the rhumbatron.

Russell Varian, a former Stanford student, and his brother Sigurd, a pilot, wanted a microwave source to detect aircraft at night and in cloud. In 1936 David Webster, head of the Stanford physics department, gave them laboratory space, USD 100 a year for supplies, and an agreement that Stanford would take half of any patent royalties. They worked with Hansen. Russell found a way to use velocity modulation to bunch an electron stream; A. Arsenjewa-Heil and Oskar Heil had described the principle in 1935. Sigurd built the first tube, which worked in August 1937 (the date usually given is 30 August). Hansen’s resonators were the klystron’s cavities. The Varians published in 1939; Webster published the theory of the bunching the same year.

In Britain, Mark Oliphant at Birmingham had seen the klystron at Stanford and judged its power too small for radar. He set John Randall and Harry Boot to the problem. By late February 1940 they had a cavity magnetron: a cylindrical anode with resonant holes around a central cathode, in a magnetic field. It gave about 400 W at about 10 cm; within a week, about 1 kW. A 10 kW version crossed the Atlantic with the Tizard Mission in September 1940, and wartime radar at 10 cm and 3 cm followed.

After the war the parts were in place: cavities, waveguides, pulsed magnetrons and klystrons, and a generation of physicists who knew microwave engineering; the MIT Radiation Laboratory, which developed microwave radar, had been staffed largely by nuclear physicists, many of them cyclotron builders (Livingston and Blewett 1962, chapter 1). Alvarez’s 200 MHz proton linac (202.5 MHz in Livingston and Blewett’s table 10-1, p. 316) and Hansen’s Stanford electron linacs came out of them (chapter 14). The rest of this chapter is the physics of the cavity and its power source, and of what limits both.

Pre-requisite (continued): Faraday induction, inductance and magnetic energy

Pre-requisite. This continues Faraday induction, inductance and magnetic energy from chapter 1, which gave Faraday’s law in integral form, \(\oint\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = -d\Phi_B/dt\), and its continuation in chapter 9, which gave inductance, the coil’s voltage \(L\,dI/dt\) and the magnetic energy density \(B^2/2\mu_0 = \mu_0H^2/2\). Three things are added: inductances in parallel, the inductance of a one-turn path closed by a wall, and Faraday’s law at a point, the differential form that section 2 starts from. Section 1 uses the first two. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, sections 4.1 and 4.1.2 and appendix C.3 (Stokes’ theorem).

Inductances in parallel. Connect \(n\) identical coils, each of inductance \(L_1\) (H), between the same two points, and suppose the flux of one does not thread the others. They share one voltage, \(V = L_1\,dI_i/dt\) for each, and their currents add, \(I = \sum I_i\). So \(dI/dt = nV/L_1\), and the group behaves as one inductance

\[ L = \frac{L_1}{n}, \]

the same rule as for resistors in parallel. If the loops share flux, each one’s field induces a voltage in its neighbours, and the inductance falls by less than \(n\); but adding paths in parallel always lowers it.

At work. Two discs of radius 20 mm, 10 mm apart, have \(C = \varepsilon_0\pi r^2/g = 1.11\) pF (chapter 1). Join them by one wire loop of, say, 20 nH (an illustrative value for a loop a few centimetres round): the circuit resonates at \(1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC}) = 1.07\) GHz. Two loops give 1.51 GHz, eight give 3.0 GHz, if their mutual flux is ignored. This is the first step of section 1’s picture.

A wall as a single turn. Now replace the loops by a continuous cylinder of radius \(b\) joining the plates, height \(h\) (m). Current \(I\) flows up the wall, in across one plate, through the central region between the plates (as the displacement current of the next section), and out across the other plate. The magnetic field circles the axis in the space between the central region (radius \(r_c\)) and the wall. Ampère’s law round a circle of radius \(r\) in that space encloses the whole current, so \(2\pi rB = \mu_0I\) and \(B = \mu_0I/2\pi r\). The flux through a radial strip of height \(h\) from \(r_c\) to \(b\) is

\[ \Phi = \int_{r_c}^{b}\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}\,h\,dr = \frac{\mu_0hI}{2\pi}\ln\frac{b}{r_c}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad L = \frac{\Phi}{I} = \frac{\mu_0h}{2\pi}\ln\frac{b}{r_c} . \]

This is the toroidal winding of a beam current transformer (chapter 8) with a single turn. With \(h = 10\) mm, \(r_c = 20\) mm and \(b = 40\) mm, \(L = 1.39\) nH, and with the 1.11 pF above the resonance is at 4.05 GHz: a metal box a few centimetres across rings at gigahertz. The estimate is rough, because a real cavity has no sharp line between “capacitor” and “coil” (the electric and magnetic fields overlap); section 2 solves the box exactly.

Faraday’s law at a point. Take a small flat loop of area \(dA\) with unit normal \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\). Lay axes so that the loop is a rectangle \(dx\) by \(dy\) in the \(x\)-\(y\) plane, traversed anticlockwise seen from \(+z\). The two sides along \(y\) contribute \([\mathcal{E}_y(x + dx) - \mathcal{E}_y(x)]\,dy\) and the two along \(x\) contribute \(-[\mathcal{E}_x(y + dy) - \mathcal{E}_x(y)]\,dx\), so

\[ \oint\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \left(\frac{\partial \mathcal{E}_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial \mathcal{E}_x}{\partial y}\right)dx\,dy \equiv (\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}})_z\,dA . \]

The curl is the circulation per unit area, component by component, exactly as F2, section 11 defined it. A large loop can be tiled with small ones; the shared inner edges are traversed in opposite directions and cancel, leaving the outer boundary. That is Stokes’ theorem:

\[ \oint_C\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \int_S(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}})\cdot d\mathbf{A}. \]

Faraday’s law says the left side equals \(-\int_S(\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t)\cdot d\mathbf{A}\) for every surface \(S\), however small, so the integrands are equal:

\[ \nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t} . \]

The two curls the pillbox needs. In cylindrical coordinates \((r, \varphi, z)\) (F2, section 13, where a small step round the axis has length \(r\,d\varphi\)), repeat the small-loop count. A rectangle \(dr\) by \(dz\) in a plane of fixed \(\varphi\) gives \((\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}})_\varphi = \partial \mathcal{E}_r/\partial z - \partial \mathcal{E}_z/\partial r\). A small sector between \(r\) and \(r + dr\), of angle \(d\varphi\), in a plane of fixed \(z\), has sides of length \(r\,d\varphi\) and \((r + dr)\,d\varphi\); for a field \(H_\varphi(r)\) the circulation is \([(r + dr)H_\varphi(r + dr) - rH_\varphi(r)]\,d\varphi\) and the area \(r\,dr\,d\varphi\), so

\[ (\nabla\times\mathbf{H})_z = \frac{1}{r}\frac{d(rH_\varphi)}{dr} . \]

For the field \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = \mathcal{E}_z(r)\hat{\mathbf{z}}\) of section 2, only \((\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}})_\varphi = -d\mathcal{E}_z/dr\) survives.

At work. At 1 MV the 2856 MHz cell of section 4 has a magnetic field of 52 mT at its wall. A pick-up loop of 2 mm² there, facing the field, has an emf of amplitude \(\omega BA = 2\pi\times2.856\times10^9\times0.052\times2\times10^{-6} = 1.87\) kV. That is why field probes are tiny and set back in ports where the field is far weaker.

Pre-requisite (continued): Dielectrics, capacitance and field energy

Pre-requisite. This continues Dielectrics, capacitance and field energy from chapter 1, which gave the parallel-plate capacitor, \(C = \varepsilon_0A/g\), and its continuation in chapter 4, which gave the stored energy \(\tfrac12CV^2\) as an energy density \(\tfrac12\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}^2\) filling the field’s volume. Section 4 integrates that density over the cavity. What is new here is a small conducting sphere placed in a uniform field, electric or magnetic: the dipole it acquires, proportional to its radius cubed. The bead pull at the bench uses both. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, sections 2.2.3 (the dipole) and 2.4 (conductors), and his problem on a sphere in a field quoted in chapter 4.

The set-up. A metal sphere of radius \(R\) (m) sits in a field that, far away, is uniform: \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_0 = \mathcal{E}_0\hat{\mathbf{z}}\). Charges in the metal move until its surface is an equipotential. They pile up negative on the side facing the field’s source and positive on the other, and their field adds to \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_0\) outside. What is the total?

The potential. Outside the sphere there is no charge, so the potential \(\phi\) obeys Laplace’s equation (chapter 2). In spherical coordinates (F2, section 13), with \(\theta\) the angle from \(\hat{\mathbf{z}}\), try \(\phi = f(r)\cos\theta\). The Laplacian of that, worked out term by term for exactly this sphere in chapter 4, is \(\left[(r^2f')'/r^2 - 2f/r^2\right]\cos\theta\), which vanishes for \(f = r\) (check: \(2/r - 2/r = 0\)) and for \(f = 1/r^2\) (check: \(2/r^4 - 2/r^4 = 0\)). The uniform field is \(\phi = -\mathcal{E}_0r\cos\theta\). Add the second solution with a constant chosen so that the sphere, at the potential of the plane through its centre, is at \(\phi = 0\):

\[ \phi = -\mathcal{E}_0\cos\theta\left(r - \frac{R^3}{r^2}\right). \]

By uniqueness this is the answer. The added term is the potential \(p\cos\theta/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)\) of a dipole at the centre, with

\[ p = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3\mathcal{E}_0 \quad (\text{C m}). \]

The field at the surface is \(\mathcal{E}_r = -\partial\phi/\partial r = 3\mathcal{E}_0\cos\theta\): three times the applied field at the poles. Integrating the energy density \(\tfrac12\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}^2\), or using the dipole energy, shows that the sphere changes the field energy around it by an amount of order \(\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}_0^2R^3\): proportional to its volume and to the energy density where it sits. The bead pull reads exactly that.

The magnetic counterpart. In an RF field the skin depth (section 3) is far smaller than a bead, so the field cannot enter the metal and its normal component at the surface must vanish (the boundary condition of the conductor section below). In a region without current, \(\mathbf{H} = -\nabla\psi\) with \(\psi\) obeying Laplace’s equation, and the same two solutions serve. With \(\mathbf{H}_0 = H_0\hat{\mathbf{z}}\),

\[ \psi = -H_0\cos\theta\left(r + \frac{R^3}{2r^2}\right), \qquad \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r}\bigg|_{r=R} = -H_0\cos\theta\left(1 - 1\right) = 0 . \]

The added term is the potential \(m\cos\theta/(4\pi r^2)\) of a magnetic dipole \(m = -2\pi R^3H_0\) (A m²), pointing against the field: the sphere pushes the field lines aside, and the tangential field at its equator rises to \(\tfrac32H_0\). In a slowly varying RF field both dipoles follow the field in step, since a bead of a millimetre is tiny compared with a wavelength of 10 cm.

At work. The bead of the bench section, \(R = 0.5\) mm, on the axis of the 2856 MHz cell at 1 MV (\(\mathcal{E}_0 = 29.9\) MV/m) would carry \(p = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3\mathcal{E}_0 = 4.2\times10^{-13}\) C m and a surface field of \(3\mathcal{E}_0 = 90\) MV/m, which would spark. Bead pulls are done with milliwatts from a network analyser, where the fields are thousands of times weaker and the frequency shift, which depends only on the ratio of the bead’s effect to the stored energy, is the same.

Exercise C1. Check that \(\phi = -\mathcal{E}_0\cos\theta\,(r - R^3/r^2)\) gives zero tangential field on the sphere and a surface charge density \(3\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}_0\cos\theta\).

Answer

On \(r = R\), \(\phi = 0\) for every \(\theta\), so \(\mathcal{E}_\theta = -(1/r)\,\partial\phi/\partial\theta = 0\). The normal field is \(\mathcal{E}_r = -\partial\phi/\partial r = \mathcal{E}_0\cos\theta\,(1 + 2R^3/r^3) = 3\mathcal{E}_0\cos\theta\) at \(r = R\), and the surface charge is \(\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}_r = 3\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}_0\cos\theta\) (chapter 4): negative on the side facing the field’s source, positive opposite.

Exercise C2. A bead of radius 1 mm sits on the axis of a cavity at a point where \(\mathcal{E} = 1\) MV/m. What dipole does it carry, and what does the dipole become if the bead’s radius is halved?

Answer

\(p = 4\pi\varepsilon_0(10^{-3})^3(10^6) = 1.11\times10^{-13}\) C m. Halving the radius divides it by 8, to \(1.39\times10^{-14}\) C m. The frequency shift falls by the same factor 8, which is why the bead is chosen as large as the field’s variation along the axis allows.

1. From a circuit to a box

The picture. Start with a parallel-plate capacitor connected by a single loop of wire. Charge oscillates between the plates through the loop. To raise the frequency, reduce the inductance by adding more loops in parallel, then a whole ring of them, until the plates are joined all round by a cylindrical wall. Now the capacitor is the central region between two end plates, where the electric field points along the axis, and the inductor is the cylindrical wall, around which the magnetic field circulates. Nothing radiates, because the fields are enclosed. Current flows up the wall and across the end plates on the inside surface. A hole on the axis lets a beam through the region of strongest electric field.

This is the pillbox cavity. Real accelerating cavities have rounded shapes, beam pipes, nose cones and ports, but the pillbox can be solved exactly and shows every idea.

Pre-requisite: Maxwell’s equations and the wave equation

Pre-requisite. This section collects the laws of the field into Maxwell’s four equations, adds the one piece the course has not yet needed, the displacement current, and shows that together they make waves travelling at \(c\). It writes the equations for fields varying as \(e^{j\omega t}\), derives the Helmholtz equation \(\nabla^2\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} + k^2\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = 0\) with \(k = \omega/c\), and finds the ratio \(\eta_0 = \sqrt{\mu_0/\varepsilon_0} = 376.7\ \Omega\) between electric and magnetic fields in a wave. Section 2 starts from all of these. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, sections 4.2 (the displacement current) and 4.3 (light); Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 2, §26, §30 and §46 to §48; and Wangler, section 1.8.

What the course has so far. Four laws have appeared, each in integral form:

  • Gauss’s law (chapter 1; F4, section 4): the flux of \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) out of a closed surface is the charge inside over \(\varepsilon_0\).
  • No magnetic charge: the flux of \(\mathbf{B}\) out of any closed surface is zero (F4, section 13).
  • Faraday’s law (section 1’s pre-requisite; F4, section 14): the circulation of \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) round a loop is minus the rate of change of magnetic flux through it.
  • Ampère’s law (chapter 2; F4, section 12): the circulation of \(\mathbf{B}\) round a loop is \(\mu_0\) times the current through it.

The divergence theorem (F2, section 10) turns a flux out of a small closed surface into \(\nabla\cdot\) times the volume, as Stokes’ theorem (section 1’s pre-requisite) turns a circulation into \(\nabla\times\) times the area. Shrinking every surface and loop to a point gives the laws as equations between fields at that point.

The gap in Ampère’s law. (F4, section 16 told this story first; here it is again, in the form the cavity needs.) Charge a capacitor through a wire carrying current \(I\). Draw a loop round the wire. Ampère’s law says the circulation of \(\mathbf{B}\) round it is \(\mu_0I\), where \(I\) is the current through any surface spanning the loop. Choose a flat surface: the wire pierces it, and the answer is \(\mu_0I\). Now choose a surface bulging out to pass between the plates, never touching the wire. No current crosses it, so the answer is zero. Both cannot be right.

What does cross the bulging surface is a changing electric field. Between plates of area \(A\) carrying charge \(Q\), \(\mathcal{E} = Q/\varepsilon_0A\), so \(\varepsilon_0\,d(EA)/dt = dQ/dt = I\). Maxwell’s fix is to add this term to the current: the circulation of \(\mathbf{B}\) is \(\mu_0\) times the conduction current plus \(\varepsilon_0\) times the rate of change of electric flux. The new term is the displacement current, with density \(\varepsilon_0\,\partial\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}/\partial t\) (A/m²). With it, both surfaces give \(\mu_0I\). It is not optional: take the divergence of \(\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{J} + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}/\partial t\). The divergence of a curl is zero (F2, section 11), and \(\varepsilon_0\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = \rho\), so

\[ 0 = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} + \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}, \]

the conservation of charge. Without the displacement current, Ampère’s law would allow charge to pile up only if it did not pile up.

Maxwell’s equations. In vacuum, with charge density \(\rho\) (C/m³) and current density \(\mathbf{J}\) (A/m²), and with \(\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{B}/\mu_0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} &= \rho/\varepsilon_0, &\qquad \nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} &= -\mu_0\frac{\partial\mathbf{H}}{\partial t}, \\ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{H} &= 0, &\qquad \nabla\times\mathbf{H} &= \mathbf{J} + \varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}{\partial t}. \end{aligned} \]

The constants are \(\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\) F/m and \(\mu_0 = 1.2566\times10^{-6}\) H/m.

Displacement or conduction? In a metal both currents are present. With Ohm’s law \(\mathbf{J} = \sigma\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) (the conductor section below) and fields varying at \(\omega\), the ratio of conduction to displacement current is \(\sigma/\omega\varepsilon_0\). For copper (\(\sigma = 5.8\times10^7\) S/m) at 1.3 GHz it is \(8\times10^8\): inside a metal the displacement current is utterly negligible. For stainless steel it is \(2\times10^7\); for sea water, 66. In the vacuum of a cavity there is no conduction current at all, and the displacement current is the only current between the end plates: the “capacitor” of section 1 passes current exactly this way.

Phasor form. For fields varying at one angular frequency, write \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbf{r}, t) = \mathrm{Re}[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbf{r})e^{j\omega t}]\) and the same for \(\mathbf{H}\), with complex amplitudes \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbf{r})\) and \(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{r})\) (the complex-numbers pre-requisite of chapter 9 and F5, section 3 show why \(\partial/\partial t\) becomes \(j\omega\): the derivative of \(e^{j\omega t}\) is \(j\omega e^{j\omega t}\)). In charge-free vacuum the curl equations become

\[ \nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -j\omega\mu_0\mathbf{H}, \qquad \nabla\times\mathbf{H} = j\omega\varepsilon_0\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}. \]

The wave equation. Take the curl of the first and use the second:

\[ \nabla\times(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}) = -j\omega\mu_0\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \omega^2\mu_0\varepsilon_0\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}. \]

A vector identity gives \(\nabla\times(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}) - \nabla^2\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\), where \(\nabla^2\) (F2, section 12) acts on each Cartesian component. To check it, write out the \(x\) component with the curl of F2, section 11:

\[ \begin{aligned} [\nabla\times(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}})]_x &= \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{\partial \mathcal{E}_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial \mathcal{E}_x}{\partial y}\right) - \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(\frac{\partial \mathcal{E}_x}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial \mathcal{E}_z}{\partial x}\right) \\ &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{\partial \mathcal{E}_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial \mathcal{E}_z}{\partial z}\right) - \frac{\partial^2\mathcal{E}_x}{\partial y^2} - \frac{\partial^2\mathcal{E}_x}{\partial z^2} , \end{aligned} \]

and add and subtract \(\partial^2\mathcal{E}_x/\partial x^2\): the first group becomes \(\partial(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}})/\partial x\) and the rest \(-\nabla^2\mathcal{E}_x\). The \(y\) and \(z\) components follow in the same way. With no charge, \(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = 0\), so

\[ \nabla^2\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} + k^2\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = 0, \qquad k = \omega\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0} = \frac{\omega}{c}, \qquad c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}} = 2.998\times10^8\ \text{m/s}. \]

This is the Helmholtz equation, the time-independent form of the wave equation \(\nabla^2\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = c^{-2}\,\partial^2\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}/\partial t^2\) (F5, section 8 met it on a string, F4, section 17 for light): each \(\partial^2/\partial t^2\) becomes \((j\omega)^2 = -\omega^2\). \(\mathbf{H}\) obeys the same equation. Two constants measured with capacitors and coils give the speed of light: that was Maxwell’s discovery.

The plane wave and \(\eta_0\). The simplest solution is \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = \mathcal{E}_0e^{-jkz}\hat{\mathbf{x}}\), which in real form is \(\mathcal{E}_0\cos(\omega t - kz)\), a wave moving in \(+z\) at \(\omega/k = c\). Faraday’s law gives its magnetic field: \((\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}})_y = \partial \mathcal{E}_x/\partial z = -jk\mathcal{E}_x = -j\omega\mu_0H_y\), so

\[ H_y = \frac{k}{\omega\mu_0}\mathcal{E}_x = \frac{\mathcal{E}_x}{\mu_0c} = \frac{\mathcal{E}_x}{\eta_0}, \qquad \eta_0 = \mu_0c = \sqrt{\frac{\mu_0}{\varepsilon_0}} = 376.73\ \Omega. \]

\(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) and \(\mathbf{H}\) are in step, at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel, and their ratio has the unit of a resistance: the impedance of free space. A wave of 1 MV/m carries \(H = 2654\) A/m, or \(B = \mathcal{E}/c = 3.34\) mT.

Standing waves and boxes. Two equal waves running in opposite directions add to a standing wave (F5, section 10): \(\mathcal{E}_0(e^{-jkz} - e^{jkz}) = -2j\mathcal{E}_0\sin kz\). It has fixed nodes where \(\sin kz = 0\), and since a perfect conductor allows no tangential electric field (the next section), a metal wall can sit at a node. Two parallel walls a distance \(d\) apart allow only \(kd = n\pi\), that is \(f = nc/2d\): 1.5 GHz for walls 10 cm apart. A closed box allows a discrete set of patterns, each with its own frequency: its modes. Each mode behaves as a separate oscillator (Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 2, §52), which is why a cavity can be described by an LC circuit (section 6).

At work: the pillbox. For section 2’s field \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = \mathcal{E}_z(r)\hat{\mathbf{z}}\), the two curls of section 1’s pre-requisite turn the phasor equations into

\[ H_\varphi = \frac{1}{j\omega\mu_0}\frac{d\mathcal{E}_z}{dr}, \qquad \frac{1}{r}\frac{d(rH_\varphi)}{dr} = j\omega\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}_z . \]

Substituting the first into the second gives \((1/r)\,d(r\,d\mathcal{E}_z/dr)/dr + k^2\mathcal{E}_z = 0\): section 2’s Bessel equation, reached from two first-order laws. The second equation is Ampère’s law with only the displacement current: integrated over the disc inside radius \(r\), it says the circulation \(2\pi rH_\varphi\) equals the displacement current through that disc. At the wall of the 2856 MHz cell at 1 MV (\(\mathcal{E}_0 = 29.9\) MV/m, \(a = 40.2\) mm) the displacement current through the whole cross-section has amplitude 10.4 kA, and so the wall carries 10.4 kA: the same number reached either way.

\(H = \mathcal{E}/\eta_0 = 3\times10^7/376.73 = 79.6\) kA/m and \(B = \mathcal{E}/c = 0.100\) T. A cavity at that field has walls carrying tens of kiloamperes per metre, the same order (section 2 has \(0.519\mathcal{E}_0/\eta_0\) at the pillbox wall).

Exercise D3. Show that \(\mathcal{E}_z = \mathcal{E}_0J_0(kr)\) and \(H_\varphi = jE_0J_1(kr)/\eta_0\) satisfy Ampère’s law with displacement current round a circle of radius \(r\), using \(\int_0^r J_0(kr')\,r'\,dr' = rJ_1(kr)/k\) (from \(d(xJ_1)/dx = xJ_0\), in the Bessel section below).

Pre-requisite: Fields in conductors, skin depth, surface resistance

Pre-requisite. This section gives the rules a metal wall imposes on an RF field: Ohm’s law \(\mathbf{J} = \sigma\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) with the conductivity \(\sigma\) in S/m; why a metal holds no free charge inside; and the boundary conditions at a perfect conductor, where the tangential electric field and the normal magnetic field vanish and the tangential magnetic field is carried by a surface current \(\mathbf{K} = \hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\mathbf{H}\). Section 2 uses the boundary conditions to fix the pillbox’s frequency and its wall currents; section 3 then lets the conductivity be finite and finds the skin depth and the surface resistance. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, sections 2.4, 3.1.2 (surface currents and discontinuities) and 4.3.3 (reflection off a conductor); Wangler, section 1.9; and Padamsee, Knobloch and Hays, section 4.4.

Ohm’s law at a point. In a metal, a field \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) drives a current density proportional to it:

\[ \mathbf{J} = \sigma\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}, \]

with \(\sigma\) the conductivity in siemens per metre (S/m, that is A V⁻¹ m⁻¹), the inverse of the resistivity \(\rho_e\) (Ω m) (F4, section 8). A wire of length \(l\) and cross-section \(A\) has \(J = I/A\) and \(\mathcal{E} = V/l\), so \(V = Il/(\sigma A)\): the familiar resistance \(R = l/(\sigma A)\). Values used in this chapter: copper \(5.8\times10^7\) S/m (the usual cavity value; pure annealed copper at 20 °C is \(5.96\times10^7\)), niobium at room temperature about \(6.7\times10^6\) S/m (\(\rho_e \approx 1.5\times10^{-7}\) Ω m), stainless steel about \(1.4\times10^6\) S/m. Why the law holds, and why \(\sigma\) grows so much in pure cold metal, is in the section on electrons in metals before section 3.

No charge inside. Put a lump of charge density \(\rho\) inside a metal. Charge conservation, Ohm’s law and Gauss’s law give

\[ \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = -\sigma\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\rho, \]

so \(\rho\) decays as \(e^{-t/\tau}\) with \(\tau = \varepsilon_0/\sigma\): \(1.5\times10^{-19}\) s for copper. (Tong, section 8.6.3, finds the same decay rate \(\sigma/\varepsilon_0\). A fuller treatment, with the electrons’ inertia, shows the charge ringing at the plasma frequency and dying away in about the collision time, of order \(10^{-14}\) s for a good metal, the value Tong gives in section 8.6.1; either way it is far shorter than an RF period.) Any charge a field brings to a metal ends up on its surface, and inside, the field and current satisfy Maxwell’s equations without free charge.

The perfect conductor. Let \(\sigma \to \infty\). A finite current then needs \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = \mathbf{J}/\sigma \to 0\) inside, and Faraday’s law, \(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t\), then says that \(\mathbf{B}\) inside cannot change. An RF magnetic field cannot enter at all. Real metals approach this within a thin skin, whose depth section 3 computes: 1.2 µm for copper at 2856 MHz.

Boundary conditions. Apply the integral laws to shapes straddling the surface, thinner than anything else in the problem. Let \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) be the unit normal pointing out of the metal, into the cavity.

  • Tangential \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\). Take a thin rectangle with long sides of length \(l\) just inside and just outside, and short sides across the surface. Faraday’s law: the circulation is \((\mathcal{E}_{t,out} - \mathcal{E}_{t,in})\,l\), and the flux through the rectangle vanishes as its width shrinks. So the tangential field is continuous; inside a perfect conductor it is zero, so just outside \(\mathcal{E}_t = 0\). The electric field meets a perfect conductor at right angles.
  • Normal \(\mathbf{B}\). A thin pillbox across the surface: no magnetic flux leaves a closed surface, and only the two faces count, so \(B_n\) is continuous; inside it is zero (for an RF field), so outside \(B_n = 0\). The magnetic field runs along the wall.
  • Normal \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\). The same pillbox with Gauss’s law gives \(\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}_n = \sigma_s\), the surface charge per unit area (chapter 4).
  • Tangential \(\mathbf{H}\). The thin rectangle with Ampère’s law: the circulation of \(\mathbf{H}\) is the current through the rectangle (the displacement current through a vanishing area vanishes). A current confined to the surface, \(\mathbf{K}\) in amperes per metre of width, does pass through it however thin it is. So the jump in the tangential field equals \(K\), and with zero field inside,

\[ \mathbf{K} = \hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\mathbf{H} . \]

Check the direction with axes: metal below \(z = 0\), cavity above, \(\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \hat{\mathbf{z}}\), field \(\mathbf{H} = H\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) just above. Then \(\mathbf{K} = \hat{\mathbf{z}}\times H\hat{\mathbf{y}} = -H\hat{\mathbf{x}}\). A sheet of current flowing in \(-\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) makes, by the right-hand rule, a field along \(+\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) above it, as required. The magnetic field at a wall is a direct measure of the current flowing in it, and with a finite conductivity that current is what dissipates power (section 3).

At work. In the 2856 MHz pillbox at 1 MV, section 2’s field at the cylindrical wall is \(|H_\varphi| = 0.519\mathcal{E}_0/\eta_0 = 41.2\) kA/m. The wall therefore carries 41.2 kA per metre of circumference, 412 A in every centimetre, and \(2\pi a \times 41.2\) kA/m \(= 10.4\) kA in all, the displacement current of the previous section’s example. In a real copper wall the tangential electric field is not quite zero: section 3 finds it is \((1 + j)R_s\) times \(H_t\), with \(R_s = 13.9\) mΩ here, so its magnitude is \(\sqrt2\times0.0139\times41\,200 = 810\) V/m. Against 30 MV/m on the axis, the “perfect conductor” is a very good approximation for finding the fields, and only the loss needs the finite \(\sigma\).

Exercise E3. Why can there be no TM\(_{010}\)-like field with \(\mathcal{E}_z \ne 0\) at the cylindrical wall of a pillbox, but \(\mathcal{E}_z\) can be largest on the end plates?

Pre-requisite (continued): Ordinary differential equations (linear, constant and varying coefficients)

Pre-requisite. This continues Ordinary differential equations from chapter 2 and its continuations, which solved linear equations with constant coefficients, first-order relaxation towards a steady value (the response \(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\) to a switched-on drive, which section 7 uses for filling a cavity), and the oscillator. Three equations in this chapter need more: Bessel’s equation, whose coefficients vary and which has a solution that is singular on the axis; \(y'' = \kappa^2y\) with a complex \(\kappa^2\), which needs the square root of \(j\); and an equation of motion driven by a sinusoidal force, integrated twice from rest. Sections 2, 3 and 10 use them. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, appendix B.5.2 (Bessel functions); Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 1, §22 (motion under a given force); and Penrose, §5.1 and §5.4 (the geometry of complex multiplication, and complex powers with their several values).

Bessel’s equation and the solution regular on the axis. Section 2 meets

\[ \frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r\frac{d\mathcal{E}_z}{dr}\right) + k^2\mathcal{E}_z = 0 . \]

Put \(x = kr\) and \(y(x) = \mathcal{E}_z\); the equation becomes \(y'' + y'/x + y = 0\). The coefficient \(1/x\) varies, and is infinite at \(x = 0\), so the constant-coefficient method (try \(e^{\lambda x}\)) fails. Try instead a power series, \(y = \sum_n a_nx^{n+s}\), with \(a_0 \ne 0\) (a Taylor series, F1, section 8, allowed to start at a power \(s\) still to be found). Differentiating term by term,

\[ \begin{aligned} y'' &= \sum_n a_n(n + s)(n + s - 1)\,x^{n+s-2}, \qquad \frac{y'}{x} = \sum_n a_n(n + s)\,x^{n+s-2}, \\ y &= \sum_n a_{n-2}\,x^{n+s-2}, \end{aligned} \]

where the last sum was relabelled (\(n \to n - 2\)) so that all three carry the same power. The first two add to \(\sum_na_n(n + s)^2x^{n+s-2}\). The lowest power, \(x^{s-2}\) (\(n = 0\)), appears only from \(y''\) and \(y'/x\), with coefficient \(a_0s^2\). It must vanish, so \(s = 0\). For each higher power \(x^{n-2}\), the equation needs the total coefficient to vanish, \(a_nn^2 + a_{n-2} = 0\), so

\[ a_n = -\frac{a_{n-2}}{n^2}, \qquad a_1 = 0 \ (\text{since } 1^2a_1 = 0), \]

and with \(a_0 = 1\) the even coefficients are \(-1/4\), \(+1/64\), \(-1/2304\), and in general \((-1)^m/[4^m(m!)^2]\):

\[ J_0(x) = \sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^m}{(m!)^2}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2m} = 1 - \frac{x^2}{4} + \frac{x^4}{64} - \dots \]

This is the Bessel function of order zero. The series converges for every \(x\), because each term is the previous one times \(-(x/2)^2/m^2\). A second-order equation has two independent solutions, and here the index equation \(s^2 = 0\) gave only one root. The second solution, called \(Y_0\), contains \(\ln x\) and is infinite on the axis. A field cannot be infinite in empty space, so on any region that includes the axis only \(J_0\) is allowed. Where the axis is excluded, as in a coaxial line, both enter.

At work. At \(x = 2.405\) the terms are \(1\), \(-1.446\), \(+0.523\), \(-0.0840\), \(+0.0076\), \(-0.0004\), and the partial sums \(1\), \(-0.446\), \(0.077\), \(-0.0072\), \(0.0004\): the series settles at zero. That zero is the pillbox’s resonance condition \(ka = 2.405\).

\(y'' = \kappa^2y\) with complex \(\kappa^2\). Section 3 meets \(d^2H/dz^2 = j\omega\mu_0\sigma H\) in a metal filling \(z > 0\). The solutions are \(e^{\pm\kappa z}\) with \(\kappa^2 = j\omega\mu_0\sigma\), exactly as for a real constant, because the steps (try \(e^{\lambda z}\), get \(\lambda^2 = \kappa^2\)) never used that \(\kappa^2\) was real. But now \(\kappa\) needs the square root of \(j\). Write \(j = e^{j\pi/2}\) (Euler’s formula, F5, section 2: \(e^{j\pi/2} = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{2} + j\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2}\)); its square roots are \(e^{j\pi/4} = (1 + j)/\sqrt2\) and minus that. So

\[ \kappa = \pm\frac{1 + j}{\sqrt2}\sqrt{\omega\mu_0\sigma} = \pm\frac{1 + j}{\delta}, \qquad \delta = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega\mu_0\sigma}}. \]

Which sign? \(e^{+(1 + j)z/\delta}\) grows without limit into the metal, which no finite source at the surface can produce, so the physical solution is \(H = H_te^{-(1 + j)z/\delta} = H_te^{-z/\delta}e^{-jz/\delta}\). The real part of the exponent makes the field decay: down to \(e^{-1} = 37\) per cent at one skin depth and 5 per cent at three. The imaginary part makes it lag: one radian per skin depth, so at \(3\delta\) the field is 172° behind the surface, nearly opposite. Choosing the root by the physical demand of decay is the same reasoning as choosing \(J_0\) over \(Y_0\).

Integrating a driven motion twice. In section 10 an electron of mass \(m\) starts at rest at \(x = 0\) when \(t = 0\), between plates where the force is \(F\sin(\omega t + \phi_e)\), with \(F = eV/d\). The equation \(m\ddot{x} = F\sin(\omega t + \phi_e)\) has no \(x\) in it, so it integrates directly:

\[ \begin{aligned} \dot{x}(t) &= \frac{F}{m\omega}\left[\cos\phi_e - \cos(\omega t + \phi_e)\right], \\ x(t) &= \frac{F}{m\omega^2}\left[\omega t\cos\phi_e - \sin(\omega t + \phi_e) + \sin\phi_e\right], \end{aligned} \]

where each constant of integration was fixed by \(\dot{x}(0) = 0\) and \(x(0) = 0\). The motion has two parts: an oscillation at \(\omega\), and a steady drift \(F\cos\phi_e/(m\omega)\) that the starting phase leaves behind. An electron released at \(\phi_e = 90°\), at the crest of the force, gains no drift at all and only shakes in place; one released at \(\phi_e = 0\) drifts fastest. Landau and Lifshitz (vol. 1, §22) integrate an oscillator under any given force in one general formula; with the restoring force removed, a free particle, it reduces to what was done here: the velocity is the integral of the force over the mass, and the initial conditions add a uniform motion.

At work. For \(\phi_e = 0\), the electron reaches the far plate \(x = d\) at \(\omega t = \pi\) when \(F\pi/(m\omega^2) = d\), that is \(V = m\omega^2d^2/(\pi e)\): 3.0 kV for a 5 mm gap at 1.3 GHz, the number in section 10.

Pre-requisite (continued): Special functions and integrals (Bessel, Gaussian, error function)

Pre-requisite. This continues Special functions and integrals from chapter 2, which gave the Gaussian integrals, and its later continuations, which used Gaussian distributions. Here are the Bessel functions the cavity needs: the shapes of \(J_0\) and \(J_1\), their zeros and maxima (2.405 and 1.841), the rule \(dJ_0/dx = -J_1\), the integrals of \(J_0^2\) and \(J_1^2\) over a disc that give a cavity’s stored energy and wall loss, Bessel’s integral and the expansion it gives for a periodic function (the klystron’s bunched current), and the modified function \(I_0\) that grows off the axis. Sections 2, 4, 5 and 9 use them. The Gaussian spread of heat is taught with diffusion before the pulsed-heating part of section 10. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, appendix B.5.2, with the series of the ODE section above.

\(J_0\) and \(J_1\). The ODE section above built \(J_0(x) = \sum(-1)^m(x/2)^{2m}/(m!)^2\). Differentiate term by term:

\[ \frac{dJ_0}{dx} = \sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^m\,2m}{(m!)^2}\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2m-1} = -\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^m}{m!\,(m + 1)!}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2m+1} \equiv -J_1(x), \]

after shifting \(m \to m + 1\). The new series defines \(J_1\), the Bessel function of order one. The same kind of term-by-term work shows \(d(xJ_1)/dx = xJ_0\) and \(dJ_1/dx = J_0 - J_1/x\).

Their shapes: \(J_0\) starts at 1 with zero slope and swings down, crossing zero at \(x = 2.405\), \(5.520\), \(8.654\), …; \(J_1\) starts at zero with slope \(\tfrac12\), rises to a maximum of 0.5819 at \(x = 1.841\) (where \(dJ_1/dx = 0\), so \(J_0 = J_1/x\)), and crosses zero at 3.832, 7.016, …. Far from the origin both behave like decaying cosines,

\[ J_0(x) \approx \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi x}}\cos\left(x - \frac{\pi}{4}\right), \]

a wave whose amplitude falls as \(1/\sqrt{x}\) because a cylindrical wave spreads its energy over a circumference that grows as \(x\). The approximation is already fair at \(x = 5\) (\(-0.170\) against the true \(-0.178\)) and predicts the first zero at \(3\pi/4 = 2.356\), near the true 2.405.

At work. In the TM\(_{010}\) pillbox, \(\mathcal{E}_z \propto J_0(kr)\) is largest on the axis and zero at the wall (\(ka = 2.405\)), while \(H_\varphi \propto J_1(kr)\) is zero on the axis and largest at \(kr = 1.841\), that is at \(r = 0.766a\): 67.6 mm from the axis in the 88.3 mm radius 1.3 GHz pillbox. At the wall it has fallen to \(J_1(2.405) = 0.519\).

Integrals over a disc. Section 4 needs \(\int_0^aJ_0^2(kr)\,r\,dr\) for the stored energy and \(\int_0^aJ_1^2(kr)\,r\,dr\) for the loss in the end plates. With \(x = kr\) these are \(k^{-2}\int_0^{ka}xJ_0^2\,dx\) and the same with \(J_1\). Differentiate a guess and use the two rules above:

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\left(J_0^2 + J_1^2\right)\right] = x(J_0^2 + J_1^2) + x^2\left[-J_0J_1 + J_1\left(J_0 - \frac{J_1}{x}\right)\right] = xJ_0^2 . \]

So \(\int_0^XxJ_0^2\,dx = \tfrac12X^2[J_0^2(X) + J_1^2(X)]\), and in the radius,

\[ \int_0^aJ_0^2(kr)\,r\,dr = \frac{a^2}{2}\left[J_0^2(ka) + J_1^2(ka)\right]. \]

In the same way, with \(J_2 = 2J_1/x - J_0\), \(\int_0^aJ_1^2(kr)\,r\,dr = \tfrac12a^2[J_1^2(ka) - J_0(ka)J_2(ka)]\). These are Lommel’s integrals. At the wall of the TM\(_{010}\) pillbox \(J_0(ka) = 0\), and both reduce to \(\tfrac12a^2J_1^2(2.405) = 0.1348a^2\): the electric energy and the end-plate loss involve the same number, 0.2695. A numerical integration agrees to all digits.

Bessel’s integral. A third way to write \(J_n\), for any whole number \(n\), is

\[ J_n(x) = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi}\cos(n\tau - x\sin\tau)\,d\tau . \]

(For \(n = 0\), expand the cosine of \(x\sin\tau\) in powers and use \(\frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\pi\sin^{2m}\tau\,d\tau = (2m)!/[4^m(m!)^2]\); the series of \(J_0\) comes back term by term.) The integral says that \(J_n(x)\) is the \(n\)-th Fourier coefficient of the periodic function \(e^{jx\sin\tau}\) (building a repeating function from harmonics is F5, section 16; the Fourier section before section 7 does it in full):

\[ e^{jx\sin\tau} = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}J_n(x)\,e^{jn\tau}, \]

the Jacobi-Anger expansion. Whenever a phase is modulated sinusoidally, \(\sin(\omega t + X\sin\omega t)\), its spectrum has lines at every harmonic with amplitudes given by Bessel functions. The klystron’s bunched current is such a case, turned inside out: the arrival time is \(t_0\) minus \(X\sin\omega t_0\), and the Fourier section before section 7 shows that its \(n\)-th harmonic is \(2I_0J_n(nX)\). Numbers to check against: \(J_1(1.841) = 0.5819\), \(J_2(3.682) = 0.4315\), \(J_3(5.523) = 0.2502\).

The modified Bessel function \(I_0\). If the sign in Bessel’s equation is reversed, \((1/r)(ry')' - \kappa^2y = 0\), the same series method gives

\[ I_0(x) = \sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(m!)^2}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2m} = 1 + \frac{x^2}{4} + \frac{x^4}{64} + \dots, \]

with all signs positive: \(I_0\) does not oscillate but grows, 1.266 at \(x = 1\) and 2.280 at \(x = 2\). It describes the field of a wave travelling with a particle slower than light. Such a field varies along the axis as \(e^{-jk_zz}\) with wavenumber \(k_z = \omega/\beta c\), larger than \(k = \omega/c\). Put \(\mathcal{E}_z = R(r)e^{-jk_zz}\) into the Helmholtz equation: \(\partial^2/\partial z^2\) gives \(-k_z^2\), and what is left is \((1/r)(rR')' - (k_z^2 - k^2)R = 0\), Bessel’s equation with the sign reversed and

\[ \kappa^2 = k_z^2 - k^2 = \frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\left(\frac{1}{\beta^2} - 1\right) = \frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\cdot\frac{1 - \beta^2}{\beta^2} = \frac{\omega^2}{\beta^2\gamma^2c^2} , \]

so the radial dependence is \(I_0(\kappa r)\) with \(\kappa = \omega/(\beta\gamma c) = 2\pi/(\beta\gamma\lambda)\): the field grows away from the axis. That is why a beam hole of radius \(r_b\) divides the transit-time factor by \(I_0(2\pi r_b/\beta\gamma\lambda)\) in section 5: for a fixed field at the bore, the axis sees less. For a proton of 45 MeV (\(\beta = 0.3\)) at 352.2 MHz (\(\lambda = 0.851\) m) and a 15 mm bore radius, the argument is 0.352 and \(I_0 = 1.031\): 3 per cent. For an electron near \(c\), \(\gamma\) is large, the argument tends to zero, and the field is flat across the bore.

2. The TM010 mode of the pillbox

Set-up. A perfectly conducting cylinder of radius \(a\) and length \(L\), axis along \(z\), end plates at \(z = 0\) and \(z = L\), filled with vacuum. Fields vary as \(e^{j\omega t}\) (complex amplitudes; physical fields are the real parts). In vacuum, Maxwell’s equations give

\[ \nabla \times \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -j\omega\mu_0\mathbf{H}, \qquad \nabla \times \mathbf{H} = j\omega\varepsilon_0\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}, \]

and taking the curl of the first, with \(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = 0\), the Helmholtz equation \(\nabla^2\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} + k^2\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = 0\) with \(k = \omega/c\) (Landau and Lifshitz vol. 2, §46 to §48 for waves in vacuum; §52 for the field in a closed box as a set of oscillators).

The accelerating mode. Look for the simplest mode useful to a beam: \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = \mathcal{E}_z(r)\,\hat{\mathbf{z}}\), independent of \(z\) and of the angle \(\varphi\). In cylindrical coordinates the Helmholtz equation becomes

\[ \frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r\frac{d\mathcal{E}_z}{dr}\right) + k^2\mathcal{E}_z = 0, \]

Bessel’s equation of order zero. The solution regular on the axis is \(\mathcal{E}_z = \mathcal{E}_0J_0(kr)\), with \(\mathcal{E}_0\) the field on axis (V/m). At the cylindrical wall the tangential electric field, here \(\mathcal{E}_z\) itself, must vanish: \(J_0(ka) = 0\). The first zero of \(J_0\) is at 2.405, so

\[ k = \frac{2.405}{a}, \qquad f_{010} = \frac{2.405\,c}{2\pi a}. \]

The end plates impose nothing more, since \(\mathcal{E}_z\) is normal to them. The frequency does not depend on \(L\). The mode is called TM\(_{010}\): transverse magnetic (no \(H_z\)), no variation in \(\varphi\), one radial half-cycle, none along \(z\). The diameter is \(2a = 0.765\lambda\), so at 1.3 GHz \(a = 88.3\) mm and at 2856 MHz \(a = 40.2\) mm.

The magnetic field. The \(\varphi\) component of Faraday’s law gives \(-\partial \mathcal{E}_z/\partial r = -j\omega\mu_0H_\varphi\), and with \(dJ_0/dx = -J_1\),

\[ H_\varphi = \frac{j \mathcal{E}_0}{\eta_0}J_1(kr), \qquad \eta_0 = \sqrt{\mu_0/\varepsilon_0} = 376.73\ \Omega . \]

The factor \(j\) says that \(H\) is a quarter period out of step with \(\mathcal{E}\) (it leads by \(90^\circ\)): when \(\mathcal{E}\) is greatest, \(H\) is zero, and the other way round. Energy swings between the electric field on the axis and the magnetic field near the wall, as in the LC circuit. \(H_\varphi\) is zero on the axis, largest at \(kr = 1.841\) where \(J_1 = 0.582\), and at the wall equals \(0.519\mathcal{E}_0/\eta_0\).

Figure 13.2. The TM\(_{010}\) fields across the pillbox, from \(\mathcal{E}_z = \mathcal{E}_0J_0(kr)\) and \(|H_\varphi| = (\mathcal{E}_0/\eta_0)J_1(kr)\) with \(ka = 2.405\). The electric field is largest on the axis, where the beam passes, and zero at the wall; the magnetic field is zero on the axis, peaks at \(r = 0.766a\) and is still 0.519 of \(\mathcal{E}_0/\eta_0\) at the wall, where it drives the wall current. The two are a quarter period apart in time.

Wall currents. At a good conductor the magnetic field just outside is carried by a surface current \(\mathbf{K} = \hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\mathbf{H}\) (A/m), with \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) the normal pointing into the cavity. On the cylinder the current runs along \(z\) with magnitude \(|H_\varphi(a)|\); on the end plates it runs radially. It flows from one end plate along the wall to the other and back across the plates, charging and discharging the “capacitor” around the axis.

Pre-requisite: Poynting’s theorem, energy and power flow

Pre-requisite. This section gives the field’s energy budget: the energy density, the power a field gives to currents (\(\mathbf{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) per unit volume, which in a resistive metal is the Ohmic loss \(|\mathbf{J}|^2/2\sigma\) on average), and the Poynting vector \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\times\mathbf{H}\) that carries energy from place to place, here from the cavity’s volume into its walls. It also shows how to average these quantities over a cycle when the fields are written as complex amplitudes. Section 3 uses all three to find the power a wall absorbs per unit area. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, section 4.4; Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 2, §31; and Wangler, section 1.9.

Energy in the field. Chapters 4 and 9 showed that a capacitor’s energy sits in its field at \(\tfrac12\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}^2\) per unit volume and a coil’s at \(\tfrac12\mu_0H^2\) (F4, sections 6 and 15). In general the energy density is

\[ u = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}^2 + \tfrac12\mu_0H^2 \quad (\text{J/m}^3). \]

Work done on charges. A charge \(q\) moving at \(\mathbf{v}\) feels \(q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})\); the magnetic part is perpendicular to \(\mathbf{v}\) and does no work, so the field gives it power \(q\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\mathbf{v}\) (power is force dotted with velocity, F3, section 4). Summed over the charges in a unit volume this is \(\mathbf{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) (W/m³). In a metal with \(\mathbf{J} = \sigma\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) it is \(\sigma \mathcal{E}^2 = J^2/\sigma\), all turned into heat. In a beam it is the power the beam gains (positive) or gives back (negative, as in a klystron’s output cavity).

Poynting’s theorem. Take the two curl equations of the Maxwell section, dot the first with \(\mathbf{H}\) and the second with \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\), and subtract:

\[ \mathbf{H}\cdot(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}) - \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{H}) = -\mu_0\mathbf{H}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{H}}{\partial t} - \varepsilon_0\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}{\partial t} - \mathbf{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}. \]

The left side is \(\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\times\mathbf{H})\) (a vector identity, checked by writing out components), and the first two terms on the right are \(-\partial u/\partial t\). So

\[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{S} = -\mathbf{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}, \qquad \mathbf{S} = \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\times\mathbf{H} \quad (\text{W/m}^2). \]

Integrated over a volume, with the divergence theorem: the rate at which field energy inside grows, plus the flux of \(\mathbf{S}\) out through the surface, equals minus the work done on the currents inside. Energy is conserved locally, and \(\mathbf{S}\), the Poynting vector, is the flow of energy per unit area per unit time (F4, section 18 met it for a wave). For the plane wave of the Maxwell section, \(S = \mathcal{E}^2/\eta_0\) and \(u = \varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}^2\) (half electric, half magnetic), so \(S = cu\): the energy travels at \(c\).

Averages with complex amplitudes. RF fields are written \(\mathrm{Re}[\mathbf{A}e^{j\omega t}]\), with \(\mathbf{A}\) a complex amplitude. Chapter 9 showed that the average over a cycle of a product of two such quantities is \(\tfrac12\mathrm{Re}[AB^*]\). Applied here:

\[ \langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = \tfrac12\mathrm{Re}\left[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\times\mathbf{H}^*\right], \qquad \langle u\rangle = \tfrac14\varepsilon_0|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}|^2 + \tfrac14\mu_0|\mathbf{H}|^2, \qquad \langle\mathbf{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\rangle = \frac{|\mathbf{J}|^2}{2\sigma}, \]

the last because in a metal \(\mathbf{J}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) are in phase, so \(\tfrac12\mathrm{Re}[\mathbf{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^*] = \tfrac12|\mathbf{J}|^2/\sigma\). The amplitudes here are peak values. The stored energy \(U\) of the chapter, the energy at the instant the electric field peaks, equals the time-averaged total \(\int\langle u\rangle\,dV\): at that instant all of it is electric, \(\tfrac12\varepsilon_0\int|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}|^2dV\), and on average the electric and magnetic parts each hold half of that.

Why electric and magnetic share equally at resonance. Write Poynting’s theorem with complex amplitudes, keeping the imaginary part. With the phasor curl equations, \(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -j\omega\mu_0\mathbf{H}\) and \(\nabla\times\mathbf{H}^* = \mathbf{J}^* - j\omega\varepsilon_0\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^*\) (the conjugate of \(\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J} + j\omega\varepsilon_0\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\)), the same vector identity gives

\[ \nabla\cdot\tfrac12(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\times\mathbf{H}^*) = \tfrac12\left[\mathbf{H}^*\cdot(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}) - \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{H}^*)\right] = -\tfrac12\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\mathbf{J}^* - 2j\omega\left(\tfrac14\mu_0|\mathbf{H}|^2 - \tfrac14\varepsilon_0|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}|^2\right). \]

The real part is the time-averaged balance of the previous paragraph; the imaginary part is new. Integrate over the cavity. For a closed cavity with perfectly conducting walls and no currents, no energy flows through the walls (\(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) there is normal, \(\mathbf{H}\) tangential, so \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\times\mathbf{H}\) is tangential), and what is left reads \(2j\omega(\langle W_m\rangle - \langle W_e\rangle) = 0\): in a mode the time-averaged magnetic energy equals the time-averaged electric energy. That is the field’s version of the LC circuit’s resonance condition (chapter 9), where the coil’s peak energy equals the capacitor’s.

Power into a wall. At a good conductor, the field just outside is nearly that of a perfect conductor: \(\mathbf{H}\) tangential, \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) almost normal. But a small tangential \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_t\) remains, because a current \(\mathbf{J} = \sigma\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) needs a field to drive it. The tangential parts of \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) and \(\mathbf{H}\) give a Poynting vector pointing into the metal, and in steady state the energy that flows in each cycle is exactly the energy the currents dissipate inside:

\[ \frac{dP}{dA} = \tfrac12\mathrm{Re}\left[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_t\times\mathbf{H}_t^*\right]\cdot(-\hat{\mathbf{n}}) = \int_0^\infty\frac{|\mathbf{J}(z)|^2}{2\sigma}\,dz , \]

with \(z\) the depth into the metal and \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) the normal out of it. Write the ratio at the surface as \(\mathcal{E}_t = Z_sH_t\), with \(Z_s\) (Ω) the surface impedance. Then \(dP/dA = \tfrac12\mathrm{Re}(Z_s)|H_t|^2\). Section 3 computes \(Z_s = (1 + j)R_s\) for a normal metal, so the loss is \(\tfrac12R_s|H_t|^2\), and section 11 does the same for a superconductor. The energy does not travel along the wire as in a circuit; it comes in from the field, through the surface.

At work. At the wall of the 2856 MHz copper pillbox at 1 MV, \(|H_t| = 41.2\) kA/m and \(R_s = 13.9\) mΩ, so \(dP/dA = \tfrac12\times0.0139\times(4.12\times10^4)^2 = 1.18\times10^7\) W/m², or 1.2 kW/cm², the figure in section 5. At the equator of a niobium TESLA cell at 23.6 MV/m, \(|H_t| = 80.0\) kA/m; with \(R_s = 13.9\) nΩ at 2 K the flux is 44 W/m², while copper there (\(R_s = 9.4\) mΩ) would take 30 MW/m². The ratio is the ratio of surface resistances, about 700 000.

\(\langle S\rangle = \mathcal{E}^2/(2\eta_0) = (10^6)^2/(2\times376.73) = 1.33\times10^9\) W/m². Into a perfect conductor the flux is zero: the reflected wave carries the same power back, and at the surface \(\mathcal{E}_t = 0\) so \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\times\mathbf{H}\) has no normal component.

Exercise H2. Show that for fields \(\mathcal{E}_0\cos\omega t\) and \(H_0\cos(\omega t + \alpha)\) at right angles, the average of \(EH\) is \(\tfrac12\mathcal{E}_0H_0\cos\alpha\), and so that a field and a current a quarter period apart carry no average power.

Answer

\(\cos\omega t\cos(\omega t + \alpha) = \tfrac12[\cos\alpha + \cos(2\omega t + \alpha)]\). The second term averages to zero, leaving \(\tfrac12\mathcal{E}_0H_0\cos\alpha\), which is \(\tfrac12\mathrm{Re}[\mathcal{E}_0(H_0e^{j\alpha})^*]\). For \(\alpha = 90°\) it vanishes: energy sloshes back and forth but none is delivered, as in the reactive current of a capacitor.

Pre-requisite (continued): Electrons in metals, Fermi-Dirac statistics, work function

Pre-requisite. This continues Electrons in metals, Fermi-Dirac statistics, work function from chapter 2, which described a metal’s conduction electrons as a gas filling a well up to the Fermi level, and its continuations on emission from the surface. Here the same electrons carry current inside the metal. The section gives Drude’s picture of conduction, which explains Ohm’s law and the size of \(\sigma\); the electrons’ mean free path and why it grows in pure, cold metal; the residual resistivity ratio (RRR) used to grade niobium; and secondary electron emission, the yield of electrons knocked out when one strikes a surface. Section 3 (the anomalous skin effect), section 10 (multipacting) and section 11 (thermal conductivity of niobium) use them. Taught from Padamsee, Knobloch and Hays, sections 3.2.1, 4.3 and 10.4, and Tong, Electromagnetism, section 8.6.1.

Drude’s picture. Treat each conduction electron as a free particle of mass \(m_e\) and charge \(-e\) that is knocked into a random direction by a collision, on average every \(\tau\) seconds (the relaxation time). Between collisions the field accelerates it. Averaged over many electrons, the random velocities cancel and only the drift \(\mathbf{v}\) given by the field survives, so

\[ m_e\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} = -e\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} - \frac{m_e\mathbf{v}}{\tau}, \]

where the last term says that collisions destroy the drift at the rate \(1/\tau\): Newton’s second law with a drag force proportional to the velocity, as in F3, section 10. In a steady field, \(\mathbf{v} = -e\tau\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}/m_e\). With \(n\) electrons per cubic metre, the current density \(\mathbf{J} = -ne\mathbf{v}\) is

\[ \mathbf{J} = \frac{ne^2\tau}{m_e}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}, \qquad \sigma = \frac{ne^2\tau}{m_e}. \]

That is Ohm’s law, with a conductivity fixed by the density of carriers and how long they run between collisions. For an RF field, write \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) as \(e^{j\omega t}\) amplitudes: \(j\omega m_e\mathbf{v} = -e\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} - m_e\mathbf{v}/\tau\), so \(\sigma(\omega) = \sigma/(1 + j\omega\tau)\).

At work. Copper has one conduction electron per atom, \(n = 8.49\times10^{28}\) m⁻³. With \(\sigma = 5.8\times10^7\) S/m, \(\tau = m_e\sigma/(ne^2) = 2.4\times10^{-14}\) s. At 1.3 GHz, \(\omega\tau = 2\times10^{-4}\): the electrons collide tens of thousands of times per RF period, \(\sigma(\omega)\) equals the DC value, and the local Ohm’s law of section 3 holds.

The mean free path. How far does an electron go between collisions? Drude used the classical thermal speed, about \(10^5\) m/s, which with this \(\tau\) gives about 3 nm, ten atomic spacings, a plausible distance between collisions with the ions. That picture was wrong in an instructive way. The electrons that carry current are those near the Fermi level (chapter 2), and they move at the Fermi speed \(v_F = \sqrt{2E_F/m_e}\), \(1.57\times10^6\) m/s for copper (\(E_F = 7.0\) eV). The mean free path is

\[ \ell = v_F\tau = 38\ \text{nm} \]

at room temperature: about 150 atomic spacings. A perfect lattice does not scatter electrons at all; they scatter from its vibrations (phonons) and from defects and impurities.

Why pure cold metal conducts so much better. The two kinds of scattering add their rates: \(1/\tau = 1/\tau_{phonon}(T) + 1/\tau_{defect}\), so the resistivities add (Matthiessen’s rule): \(\rho_e(T) = \rho_{phonon}(T) + \rho_{res}\). Lattice vibrations die away as the metal is cooled, so \(\rho_{phonon}\) falls steeply; the scattering from impurities and defects does not depend on temperature, and leaves a residual resistivity \(\rho_{res}\). The ratio

\[ \text{RRR} = \frac{\rho_e(293\ \text{K})}{\rho_e(\text{low temperature, normal state})} \]

measures purity directly: a pure, well-annealed metal has a small \(\rho_{res}\) and a large RRR. Oxygen-free copper has an RRR of order 100 or more; cavity-grade niobium, about 300, while the purity limit for niobium is near 35 000 (Padamsee, Knobloch and Hays). Because the conduction electrons also carry heat, a long mean free path means a high thermal conductivity as well: in the normal state the two conductivities are tied together (the Wiedemann-Franz law, \(k_{th} = L_0\sigma T\) with \(L_0 = 2.44\times10^{-8}\) W Ω K⁻²). In superconducting niobium most electrons are paired and carry no heat, so the rule changes, but the dependence on purity remains; that is the origin of section 11’s rule of thumb, 0.25 × RRR W/(m K) at 4.2 K.

The anomalous skin effect. Cool copper of RRR 100 to 4 K. Its conductivity rises a hundredfold, to \(5.8\times10^9\) S/m, and its mean free path to 3.8 µm. The normal skin depth at 1.3 GHz would then be 0.18 µm, twenty times shorter than the path. An electron now crosses the whole field layer between collisions, so the current at a point no longer depends only on the field at that point, and the local law \(\mathbf{J} = \sigma\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) of section 3 fails. Only the electrons moving nearly parallel to the surface stay long in the field, and they alone carry the current: the loss stops falling with \(\sigma\) (Padamsee, Knobloch and Hays, section 4.3). Cooling a copper cavity from room temperature to a few kelvin improves its surface resistance by at most a factor of about six, though the DC conductivity rises a hundredfold; the refrigeration does not pay.

Secondary electron emission. An electron striking a surface with kinetic energy \(W\) shares that energy with the electrons of the solid along its track. Some of them reach the surface with enough energy to cross the work-function barrier (chapter 2) and leave: secondary electrons, mostly with a few electronvolts. The secondary emission yield \(\delta_{se}(W)\) is the average number leaving per incident electron. Its shape follows from two lengths. Secondaries can escape only from a thin layer, of the order of nanometres, but the primary deposits its energy along a range that grows with \(W\). At low \(W\) all the energy is deposited inside the escape layer, and the yield grows in proportion to \(W\). At high \(W\) the primary goes deep and most of its energy is deposited where the secondaries cannot get out, so the yield falls. In between, the yield peaks at \(\delta_{max}\) at an energy \(W_{max}\) of a few hundred electronvolts, and exceeds one between two crossover energies, \(W_1\) (from a few tens of eV to about 200 eV) and \(W_2\) (a thousand eV or more). Padamsee, Knobloch and Hays (table 10.1) list for clean surfaces copper 1.3 at 600 eV, crossing one at 200 and 1500 eV, and niobium 1.2 at 375 eV, crossing at 150 and 1050 eV; insulating oxides are far higher, alumina 2 to 9 and magnesium oxide 3 to 15. A real cavity surface, oxidised and covered with adsorbed gas, gives more than the clean metal: section 10 quotes 1.4 to 1.6 near 200 eV for niobium as prepared. Because the secondaries come from the outermost atomic layers, the yield depends on the surface’s last few nanometres, which is why cleaning by electron bombardment (“processing”) lowers it.

At work. Multipacting needs impacts between \(W_1\) and \(W_2\), where each electron returns more than one. With a yield of 1.5, one electron becomes \(10^9\) after \(\ln10^9/\ln1.5 = 51\) impacts. In a two-surface multipactor of order 1 at 1.3 GHz, with one impact each half period, that is 20 ns: the avalanche reaches the space-charge limit within a small fraction of a microsecond.

\(\tau = m_e\sigma/(ne^2) = 9.11\times10^{-31}\times3.77\times10^7/(1.81\times10^{29}\times2.57\times10^{-38}) = 7.4\times10^{-15}\) s, and \(\ell = v_F\tau = 15\) nm. More carriers but more frequent collisions than in copper.

Exercise I2. A niobium sample has a resistivity of \(1.5\times10^{-7}\) Ω m at room temperature and \(5\times10^{-10}\) Ω m just above \(T_c\). What is its RRR, and what thermal conductivity does section 11’s rule give at 4.2 K?

Exercise I3. A secondary yield of 1.2 at the impact energy in a multipacting gap: how many impacts multiply one electron to \(10^6\)? And what happens if processing lowers the yield to 0.9?

Answer

\(\ln10^6/\ln1.2 = 76\) impacts. At a yield of 0.9 the number falls by 10 per cent each impact: one electron leaves, on average, fewer than one, and the avalanche dies. Multipacting is a threshold effect in the yield.

3. Skin depth and surface resistance

The picture. A real wall has finite conductivity \(\sigma\). The field penetrates a little, drives a current in a thin layer, and that current dissipates. The layer is thin because the induced currents screen the field.

Derivation. Take a conductor filling \(z > 0\) (a local coordinate here, with \(z\) into the metal). Inside, \(\mathbf{J} = \sigma\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) and the displacement current is negligible (\(\omega\varepsilon_0 \ll \sigma\); for copper \(\sigma/\varepsilon_0\omega = 1.0\times10^{9}\) at 1 GHz). Then \(\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \sigma\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) and \(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -j\omega\mu_0\mathbf{H}\). Let the field at the surface be \(\mathbf{H} = H_y(z)\hat{\mathbf{y}}\), driving a current \(\mathbf{J} = J_x(z)\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) with \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = \mathcal{E}_x(z)\hat{\mathbf{x}}\); nothing varies along \(x\) or \(y\), because the wall is flat on the scale of \(\delta\). Writing out the curls (F2, section 11), only one component of each survives:

\[ -\frac{dH_y}{dz} = \sigma \mathcal{E}_x, \qquad \frac{d\mathcal{E}_x}{dz} = -j\omega\mu_0H_y . \]

Differentiate the first and use the second: \(-d^2H_y/dz^2 = \sigma\,d\mathcal{E}_x/dz = -j\omega\mu_0\sigma H_y\). This is the diffusion equation, solved in the differential-equations section before section 2:

\[ \frac{d^2H_y}{dz^2} = j\omega\mu_0\sigma H_y \quad\Longrightarrow\quad H_y = H_t\,e^{-(1+j)z/\delta}, \qquad \delta = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega\mu_0\sigma}}, \]

where \(H_t\) is the tangential field at the surface (taken along \(y\)) and \(\delta\) the skin depth (m). The current density follows from Ampère’s law, \(J_x = -dH_y/dz = J_we^{-(1+j)z/\delta}\) with \(J_w = (1+j)H_t/\delta\) the current density at the surface (A/m²). The total current per unit width is \(\int_0^\infty J_x\,dz = H_t\), the surface current \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\mathbf{H}\) of section 2 (here \(\hat{\mathbf{n}} = -\hat{\mathbf{z}}\)). The time-averaged power per unit area (the Poynting section) is the loss \(|J_x|^2/2\sigma\) summed over depth; since \(|e^{-(1+j)z/\delta}|^2 = e^{-2z/\delta}\), whose integral from 0 to \(\infty\) is \(\delta/2\), and \(|J_w|^2 = |1 + j|^2|H_t|^2/\delta^2 = 2|H_t|^2/\delta^2\),

\[ \frac{dP}{dA} = \int_0^\infty \frac{|J_x|^2}{2\sigma}\,dz = \frac{|J_w|^2\delta}{4\sigma} = \frac{|H_t|^2}{2\sigma\delta} \equiv \frac{1}{2}R_s|H_t|^2, \qquad R_s = \frac{1}{\sigma\delta} = \sqrt{\frac{\omega\mu_0}{2\sigma}} . \]

\(R_s\) is the surface resistance (Ω): the wall behaves as a sheet of thickness \(\delta\) carrying the whole current uniformly. (Tong, Electromagnetism, §8.6.2, derives the skin depth from the Drude model.)

Figure 13.3. The field inside a good conductor, \(H = H_te^{-z/\delta}\cos(\omega t - z/\delta)\), at four instants an eighth of a period apart (red), inside the envelope \(\pm e^{-z/\delta}\) (dashed). The field falls to 37 per cent at one skin depth and is nearly gone by five; it also lags by one radian per skin depth, so at any instant the deeper layers swing behind the surface. The current, \(J_x = -dH_y/dz\), lives in the same few skin depths.

Numbers. For copper with \(\sigma = 5.8\times10^7\) S/m (the usual cavity value; Jensen 2014): at 10 MHz, \(\delta = 20.9\) µm and \(R_s = 0.83\) mΩ; at 352.2 MHz, 3.52 µm and 4.90 mΩ; at 1.3 GHz, 1.83 µm and 9.41 mΩ; at 2856 MHz, 1.24 µm and 13.9 mΩ; at 12 GHz, 0.60 µm and 28.6 mΩ. The surface finish must be smooth on the scale of a micrometre, or the current path lengthens and the loss rises.

Limits. This is the normal skin effect. When the electrons’ mean free path exceeds \(\delta\), as in very pure copper at cryogenic temperatures, the local relation \(\mathbf{J} = \sigma\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) fails and \(R_s\) stops falling with \(\sigma\) (the anomalous skin effect). A superconductor needs a different theory (section 11).

Pre-requisite (continued): Driven oscillator, resonance and quality factor

Pre-requisite. This continues Driven oscillator, resonance and quality factor from chapter 7 and its continuation in chapter 9, which defined \(Q_0 = \omega_0U/P\) as \(2\pi\) times the energy stored over the energy lost per cycle (first met in F5, sections 4 and 5), derived the Lorentzian resonance curve and the half-power width \(f_0/Q_0\), and showed how a fixed drive current off resonance gives a smaller voltage. Two things are added: when energy leaves by several routes, their \(1/Q\)s add; and the power needed to hold a fixed voltage when the drive is off resonance. Section 4 defines \(Q_0\) for the walls, and section 7 uses both results for the coupler, the beam and the tuner. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 1, §26 (forced oscillations under friction); Wangler, sections 5.1 and 5.4; and Padamsee, Knobloch and Hays, section 8.2.

Losses add. A cavity holding energy \(U\) at \(\omega_0\) may lose it at once to the walls (power \(P_0\)), out through a coupler into a line (power \(P_{ext}\)), and to a beam (\(P_b\)). Give each route its own quality factor, \(Q_i = \omega_0U/P_i\), as though it were the only one. The powers add, so

\[ \frac{1}{Q_L} = \frac{P_0 + P_{ext} + P_b}{\omega_0U} = \frac{1}{Q_0} + \frac{1}{Q_{ext}} + \frac{1}{Q_b} . \]

The loaded \(Q_L\) is set by the leakiest route. It is what a measurement sees: the stored energy decays as \(e^{-\omega_0t/Q_L}\) and the resonance curve has width \(f_0/Q_L\). In the equivalent circuit of section 6 each route is a resistance in parallel with the LC pair, and \(1/R = \sum1/R_i\) is the same statement. A small \(Q\) means a big loss: adding a heavily loaded coupler to a superconducting cavity changes the decay time from seconds to a millisecond.

At work. The TESLA cavity at 25 MV/m stores \(U = \hat{V}^2/[(R/Q)\omega_0] = 79.6\) J. Its walls at \(Q_0 = 10^{10}\) take 65 W, the 8 mA beam takes 208 kW (\(Q_b = 3.1\times10^6\)), and the coupler is set to \(Q_{ext} = 3.13\times10^6\). With no beam, \(1/Q_L = 10^{-10} + 1/(3.13\times10^6)\) and \(Q_L = 3.129\times10^6\): the walls change it by one part in 3000. A critically coupled copper cell has \(Q_{ext} = Q_0\) and so \(Q_L = Q_0/2\): 9200 for the 2856 MHz cell of section 4.

Detuning costs power. Chapter 9 showed that near resonance the circuit’s impedance is \(Z = R/(1 + jy)\), with the detuning parameter

\[ y = Q\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_0} - \frac{\omega_0}{\omega}\right) \approx 2Q\frac{\Delta\omega}{\omega_0} = 2Q\frac{\Delta f}{f_0}, \]

where \(Q\) is the loaded value when the source and its line are part of the circuit. The voltage is \(\tilde{V} = \tilde{I}_gZ\), with \(\tilde{I}_g\) the drive current. To hold a given \(|\tilde{V}|\), the drive must supply \(|\tilde{I}_g| = |\tilde{V}|\sqrt{1 + y^2}/R\): the in-phase current \(|\tilde{V}|/R\) that feeds the loss, plus a reactive current \(y|\tilde{V}|/R\) that only sloshes energy in and out of the detuned resonator. A generator delivering a forward wave into a matched line supplies a current proportional to the square root of its forward power, so the forward power needed goes as

\[ \frac{P_f(\Delta f)}{P_f(0)} = 1 + y^2, \]

and the cavity’s voltage lags the drive by \(\arctan y\). At \(y = 1\), half the loaded bandwidth off resonance, twice the power is needed and the phase is 45° off. The same arithmetic, with the beam’s current added to the in-phase part, gives section 7’s version with beam.

At work. The TESLA cavity without beam (\(Q_L = 3.13\times10^6\)) detuned by 215 Hz has \(y = 2\times3.13\times10^6\times215/1.3\times10^9 = 1.04\): 2.07 times the power and a 46° phase shift. The 2856 MHz copper cell critically coupled (\(Q_L = 9200\)), warmed by 1 K so that it drifts 47.5 kHz, has \(y = 0.31\): 9 per cent more power and 17° of phase, which the low-level RF must correct.

Pre-requisite: Coupled oscillators and normal modes

Pre-requisite. This section shows how a chain of \(N\) identical resonators, each coupled to its neighbours, behaves: it has exactly \(N\) normal modes, patterns in which every cell oscillates at one common frequency, and their frequencies fill a band whose width is set by the coupling. The mode in which neighbouring cells swing in opposite phase, the \(\pi\) mode, accelerates a particle that crosses a cell in half a period. Section 4’s cell is one link of such a chain; sections 7 and 11 and the bench (the nine modes of a nine-cell cavity, field flatness) use the rest. F5, section 7 met two coupled pendulums and chapter 7 the two-cell case. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 1, §23; Tong, Classical Mechanics, chapter 8 (small oscillations and normal modes; section 8.1.4, a lattice of atoms, is a chain); Wangler, sections 3.6 and 4.10; and Padamsee, Knobloch and Hays, section 7.2.

Normal modes. Two identical pendulums joined by a weak spring can swing together, the spring never stretched, at the pendulum’s own frequency; or against each other, the spring working on both, at a higher frequency. Each of these is a normal mode: a pattern of amplitudes that keeps its shape while the whole oscillates at one frequency. Any motion of the pair is a sum of the two. Start one pendulum alone, and the sum of two modes at slightly different frequencies beats: the motion passes to the other pendulum and back, with the beat period set by the difference of the mode frequencies. For any set of oscillators coupled linearly, Landau and Lifshitz (§23) show the same structure: \(n\) degrees of freedom give \(n\) normal modes, found by assuming every coordinate varies as \(e^{j\Omega t}\) and asking for which \(\Omega\) the equations have a solution that is not zero.

A chain of resonators. Let cell \(n\) have field amplitude \(X_n\), and let each cell alone resonate at \(\omega_0\). Model each as an LC circuit coupled to its neighbours, as Wangler does. In the simplest such model each cell is a loop of inductance \(L\) and capacitance \(C\), so \(\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}\), and neighbouring loops share a little magnetic flux: a current \(I\) changing in one loop induces a voltage \(M\,dI/dt\) in its neighbour, with \(M\) (H) the mutual inductance, the flux one loop sends through its neighbour per ampere. For a mode, every current varies as \(I_ne^{j\Omega t}\), and Kirchhoff’s voltage law round loop \(n\) (the coil, the capacitor, and the voltages induced by the two neighbours, F5, section 6) reads

\[ j\Omega L\,I_n + \frac{I_n}{j\Omega C} + j\Omega M\,(I_{n-1} + I_{n+1}) = 0 . \]

Divide by \(j\Omega L\), use \(1/(j\Omega)^2 = -1/\Omega^2\), write \(k = 2M/L\), and let \(X_n\) stand for the field amplitude in cell \(n\), proportional to \(I_n\):

\[ X_n\left(1 - \frac{\omega_0^2}{\Omega^2}\right) + \frac{k}{2}\left(X_{n-1} + X_{n+1}\right) = 0, \]

with \(k\) the coupling constant, a small pure number (here \(2M/L\); a few per cent for the cells of an elliptical cavity, coupled through their irises). Chapter 7’s two-cell equations have the same form, except that each cell’s single neighbour enters as \(k_cX\) rather than \((k/2)X\). Since the equation is the same for every interior cell, try a wave of fixed phase step \(\phi\) from cell to cell, \(X_n = \cos(n\phi + \alpha)\). Then \(X_{n-1} + X_{n+1} = 2\cos\phi\,X_n\), and the equation is satisfied for every \(n\) if

\[ \Omega = \frac{\omega_0}{\sqrt{1 + k\cos\phi}} . \]

This is the dispersion relation: frequency against phase advance per cell. At \(\phi = 0\) (all cells in phase, the 0 mode) \(\Omega = \omega_0/\sqrt{1 + k}\); at \(\phi = \pi\) (neighbours opposite, the \(\pi\) mode) \(\Omega = \omega_0/\sqrt{1 - k}\). All modes lie in a band of width about \(k\omega_0\), the passband, whatever the number of cells. A drive at a frequency outside it excites the chain only weakly.

The ends pick the modes. In an infinite chain any \(\phi\) would do. A finite chain of \(N\) cells has ends, and they decide which phases fit, as the walls of a box decide which wavelengths fit. In an accelerating cavity the end cells are shaped, together with the beam pipes, so that the \(\pi\) mode has equal fields in every cell. In the model this tuning makes each end behave as though a fictitious neighbour beyond it always swung opposite to the end cell: \(X_0 = -X_1\) and \(X_{N+1} = -X_N\) (Padamsee, Knobloch and Hays, section 7.2.1). Try \(X_n = \sin[(n - \tfrac12)\phi]\). The first condition holds for any \(\phi\), since \(\sin(-\phi/2) = -\sin(\phi/2)\). The second needs \(\sin[(N + \tfrac12)\phi] + \sin[(N - \tfrac12)\phi] = 2\sin(N\phi)\cos(\phi/2) = 0\), so

\[ \phi_m = \frac{m\pi}{N}, \qquad m = 1, 2, \dots, N . \]

\(N\) cells, \(N\) modes, as Landau and Lifshitz’s count requires. For \(m = N\), \(X_n = \sin[(n - \tfrac12)\pi] = \pm1\): the flat \(\pi\) mode. For \(m = 1\) the field rises from the ends to a maximum in the middle, half a sine wave along the cavity. (Wangler’s model has half-cells at the ends instead, and gets \(N + 1\) modes including the 0 mode; the counting changes with the end design, the band does not.)

Why the \(\pi\) mode. A particle at \(\beta \approx 1\) crossing a cell of length \(\lambda/2\) takes half a period. In the \(\pi\) mode the next cell’s field has just reversed when it arrives, so it is accelerated in every cell (chapter 9’s \(\beta\lambda/2\) rule). With the ends tuned as above, its fields are also equal in every cell, so every cell does the same work.

The cost: crowding near \(\pi\). At the edge of the band the dispersion curve is flat, so the modes crowd together there. The gap between the \(\pi\) mode and its neighbour, \(\phi = \pi - \pi/N\), is, for small \(k\),

\[ \frac{\Delta\Omega}{\omega_0} \approx \frac{k}{4}\left(\frac{\pi}{N}\right)^2, \]

falling as \(1/N^2\). To see it, write the phase as \(\phi = \pi - \epsilon\) with \(\epsilon\) small. For small \(k\), \(\Omega \approx \omega_0(1 - \tfrac12k\cos\phi)\) (the Taylor series of \((1 + u)^{-1/2} \approx 1 - u/2\), F1, section 8), and \(\cos(\pi - \epsilon) = -\cos\epsilon \approx -1 + \epsilon^2/2\). So \(\Omega \approx \omega_0(1 + k/2 - k\epsilon^2/4)\): near \(\pi\) the frequency departs from the band edge only as \(\epsilon^2\), the flatness of the curve’s top. The neighbour of the \(\pi\) mode has \(\epsilon = \pi/N\), which gives the gap above. Two harms follow. If the gap shrinks towards the bandwidth \(f/Q_L\), the drive excites the neighbour too. And a small error in one cell’s frequency mixes the neighbouring modes into the \(\pi\) mode in proportion to the error divided by the gap, so a long chain is hard to keep flat (Wangler, section 4.10). This is one reason superconducting cavities are made of a handful of cells, and why the bead pull at the bench is used to tune each cell until the \(\pi\) mode is flat.

At work: the TESLA cavity. Nine cells, coupling \(k = 1.87\) per cent (Aune and others 2000), \(\pi\) mode at 1300 MHz. Then \(\omega_0/2\pi = 1300\sqrt{1 - k} = 1287.8\) MHz, and the nine modes are at

\(m\) 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8 9
\(f\) (MHz) 1276.62 1278.66 1281.81 1285.70 1289.88 1293.85 1297.11 1299.25 1300.00

(the same nine numbers come from solving the \(9\times9\) matrix of the chain directly). The \(8\pi/9\) mode is 0.75 MHz below the \(\pi\) mode, against a loaded bandwidth of 415 Hz in operation: well apart. Twenty cells with the same coupling would leave 0.15 MHz.

4. Stored energy, wall power and \(Q_0\)

Stored energy. At the instant the electric field peaks, the magnetic field is zero and all the energy is electric:

\[ U = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int|\mathcal{E}_z|^2\,dV = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\mathcal{E}_0^2 L\,2\pi\int_0^a J_0^2(kr)\,r\,dr = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\mathcal{E}_0^2\,\pi a^2 L\,J_1^2(2.405), \]

with the volume element \(dV = 2\pi r\,dr\,dz\) of a field that does not depend on \(\varphi\) or \(z\) (F2, section 13), and Lommel’s integral \(\int_0^a J_0^2(kr)\,r\,dr = \tfrac{a^2}{2}[J_0^2(ka) + J_1^2(ka)]\) with \(J_0(ka) = 0\). \(J_1^2(2.405) = 0.2695\).

Wall power. Integrate \(\tfrac12R_s|H_t|^2\) over the walls. On the cylinder, \(|H_t| = 0.519\mathcal{E}_0/\eta_0\) over the area \(2\pi aL\). On each end plate, \(|H_t| = (\mathcal{E}_0/\eta_0)J_1(kr)\), and \(\int_0^aJ_1^2(kr)\,r\,dr = \tfrac{a^2}{2}J_1^2(ka)\) when \(J_0(ka) = 0\). Adding,

\[ P = \pi R_s\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_0J_1(2.405)}{\eta_0}\right)^2 a\,(L + a). \]

Quality factor. \(Q_0 = \omega U/P\), \(2\pi\) times the energy stored over the energy lost per cycle (the same definition as the LC circuit’s in chapter 9; Landau and Lifshitz vol. 1, §25 and §26). Divide the stored energy by the wall power; \(\mathcal{E}_0^2\), \(\pi\) and \(J_1^2(2.405)\) cancel:

\[ Q_0 = \frac{\omega\,\tfrac12\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}_0^2\pi a^2LJ_1^2}{\pi R_s(\mathcal{E}_0J_1/\eta_0)^2a(L + a)} = \frac{\omega\varepsilon_0\eta_0^2\,aL}{2R_s(L + a)} = \frac{\omega\mu_0a}{2R_s}\cdot\frac{L}{L + a} . \]

The middle step used \(\varepsilon_0\eta_0^2 = \varepsilon_0\mu_0/\varepsilon_0 = \mu_0\). Now \(\omega\mu_0a = (\omega a/c)\,\mu_0c = 2.405\,\eta_0\), since \(ka = \omega a/c = 2.405\) and \(\mu_0c = \eta_0\); and with \(R_s = 1/\sigma\delta\) and \(\delta^2 = 2/\omega\mu_0\sigma\), \(\omega\mu_0a/2R_s = \omega\mu_0\sigma\delta\,a/2 = a/\delta\). The result simplifies to

\[ Q_0 = \frac{2.405\,\eta_0}{2R_s(1 + a/L)} = \frac{a}{\delta}\cdot\frac{L}{L + a}. \]

The second form is the picture: \(Q_0\) is roughly the cavity’s size over the skin depth, because the field fills the volume but loses energy only in a skin \(\delta\) deep.

Worked number: a copper cell at 2856 MHz. 2856 MHz is the frequency of the SLAC linac (its klystrons, below). Take an idealised pillbox cell of length \(L = \lambda/2 = 52.5\) mm, the length of one cell of a \(\pi\)-mode standing-wave structure for \(\beta = 1\) (chapter 14), and no beam hole. Then \(a = 40.2\) mm, \(a/\delta = 32\,480\), and \(Q_0 = 18\,400\). The free bandwidth is \(f/Q_0 = 155\) kHz, and the field rings down with time constant \(2Q_0/\omega = 2.05\) µs.

5. Voltage, transit-time factor, shunt impedance and \(R/Q\)

The voltage a particle sees. A particle moving at \(\beta c\) along the axis crosses the cavity while the field changes. Measure \(z\) from the centre of the cell for the moment. With \(\mathcal{E}_z(z,t) = \mathcal{E}_0\sin(\omega t + \phi)\) inside the cell and the particle at the centre at \(t = 0\), so that it is at \(z = \beta ct\), the energy gain is

\[ \Delta W = q\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}\mathcal{E}_0\sin\!\left(\frac{\omega z}{\beta c} + \phi\right)dz = qE_0L\,T_t\sin\phi, \qquad T_t = \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}, \quad \theta = \frac{\omega L}{2\beta c} = \frac{\pi L}{\beta\lambda}. \]

To do the integral, put \(u = \omega z/\beta c\): then \(dz = (\beta c/\omega)\,du\), \(u\) runs from \(-\theta\) to \(+\theta\), and \(\int_{-\theta}^{\theta}\sin(u + \phi)\,du = \cos(\phi - \theta) - \cos(\phi + \theta) = 2\sin\theta\sin\phi\). So \(\Delta W = q\mathcal{E}_0(\beta c/\omega)\,2\sin\theta\sin\phi = qE_0L(\sin\theta/\theta)\sin\phi\), since \(\beta c/\omega = L/2\theta\). The cavity acts as a thin gap of voltage \(\hat{V} = \mathcal{E}_0LT_t\), and \(\Delta W = q\hat{V}\sin\phi\) in the course’s convention, with \(\phi = 90^\circ\) the crest. (Chapter 9 derived \(T_t\) for a gap; the linac texts write \(\Delta W = qE_0T L\cos\phi\) with \(\phi\) measured from the crest, so their phase is ours minus \(90^\circ\).) For \(L = \beta\lambda/2\), \(\theta = \pi/2\) and \(T_t = 2/\pi = 0.637\). A longer cell gains more field length but loses more to the oscillation; \(\mathcal{E}_0LT_t \propto \sin\theta\) is greatest at \(L = \beta\lambda/2\). A beam hole of radius \(r_b\) lowers \(T_t\) further, because the field off axis grows as a modified Bessel function. Taking the field as uniform across a gap \(g\) at the bore radius, Wangler (2008, section 2.4, Eq. 2.43, p. 40) finds \(T_t = J_0(2\pi r_b/\lambda)\,[\sin(\pi g/\beta\lambda)/(\pi g/\beta\lambda)]/I_0(2\pi r_b/\beta\gamma\lambda)\), with \(T_t\) referred to the voltage along the axis; he notes that the factor \(J_0(2\pi r_b/\lambda)\) is usually close to 1.

Shunt impedance. The figure of merit for a copper cavity is how much voltage a watt buys:

\[ R_{sh} = \frac{\hat{V}^2}{P} \quad (\Omega). \]

This is the linac definition, used throughout this course; circuit and synchrotron texts define \(\hat{V}^2/2P\), half of it (Jensen 2014, section 3.9.1). For the pillbox, \(R_{sh} = \eta_0^2L^2T_t^2/[\pi R_sJ_1^2\,a(L+a)]\).

\(R/Q\). Divide out the losses:

\[ \frac{R}{Q} \equiv \frac{R_{sh}}{Q_0} = \frac{\hat{V}^2}{\omega U} = \frac{2\eta_0LT_t^2}{2.405\,\pi a\,J_1^2(2.405)} . \]

(Put \(\hat{V} = \mathcal{E}_0LT_t\) and section 4’s \(U\) into \(\hat{V}^2/\omega U\): \(\mathcal{E}_0^2\) cancels, leaving \(2LT_t^2/(\omega\varepsilon_0\pi a^2J_1^2)\), and \(\omega\varepsilon_0a = 2.405\,\varepsilon_0c = 2.405/\eta_0\).) \(R/Q\) depends only on the shape, not on the material or, for a shape scaled with \(\lambda\), on the frequency: every length in it scales together, and only the ratio \(L/a\) survives. It says how much voltage a joule of stored energy gives. For the \(\lambda/2\) pillbox, \(R/Q = 196\ \Omega\) at any frequency.

Worked number, continued. The 2856 MHz cell has \(R_{sh} = 196 \times 18\,400 = 3.61\) MΩ, or 68.7 MΩ/m. To give 1 MV it needs \(\mathcal{E}_0 = 29.9\) MV/m on axis, stores 0.284 J, and dissipates 277 kW. The magnetic field at the cylindrical wall is 52 mT and the heat flux there 1.2 kW/cm² (the end plates, where \(J_1\) peaks at 0.582, see 12 per cent more field). No one runs such a cell continuously at 1 MV; it is pulsed for microseconds. The same cell scaled to 1.3 GHz has \(Q_0 = 27\,300\) and \(R_{sh}/L = 46.3\) MΩ/m. The ratio, 68.7/46.3 = 1.48, is \(\sqrt{2856/1300}\): for copper, \(R/Q\) per metre grows as \(f\) (the same \(R/Q\) per cell, and cells of length \(\lambda/2 \propto 1/f\), so more cells per metre) and \(Q_0 \approx a/\delta\) falls as \(f^{-1/2}\) (the size \(a \propto 1/f\), the skin depth \(\delta \propto f^{-1/2}\)), so the shunt impedance per metre grows as \(f\cdot f^{-1/2} = \sqrt{f}\). This is one reason electron linacs went to S-band and higher. (The radar bands, named in the war, are still used for RF: L-band is 1 to 2 GHz, S-band 2 to 4 GHz, C-band 4 to 8 GHz and X-band 8 to 12 GHz; 1.3 GHz is L-band, 2856 MHz S-band, and the 11.4 and 12 GHz of the copper high-gradient work are counted as X-band.)

Nose cones. Re-entrant noses on the end plates around the beam hole shorten the gap and concentrate the electric field on the axis, raising \(T_t\) and \(R/Q\). A rounded outer wall carries the current along the shortest path over the largest area, lowering the loss. Copper cavities are shaped this way to maximise \(R_{sh}\), at the price of a higher peak surface field for a given voltage (Gerigk 2011; Jensen 2014). Superconducting cavities are shaped the other way, to minimise peak surface fields (section 11).

6. The equivalent circuit

Near one mode, a cavity behaves as a parallel resistor, inductor and capacitor seen across the gap. Match the circuit’s stored energy, loss and resonance to the cavity’s: with \(\hat{V}\) the gap voltage, a parallel resistance \(R_p = R_{sh}/2\) dissipates \(\hat{V}^2/2R_p = \hat{V}^2/R_{sh}\); the energy \(\tfrac12C\hat{V}^2 = U\), with \(U = \hat{V}^2/[\omega_0(R/Q)]\) from the definition of \(R/Q\), gives \(C = 2/[\omega_0(R/Q)]\); and the resonance \(\omega_0^2 = 1/L_cC\) (F5, section 6) gives \(L_c = 1/(\omega_0^2C) = (R/Q)/(2\omega_0)\). (\(L_c\) here is an inductance, not the cell length.) For the 2856 MHz cell, \(R_p = 1.80\) MΩ, \(C = 0.57\) pF and \(L_c = 5.5\) nH. The circuit is driven by the generator through a coupler and by the beam as a current source (Jensen 2014, section 3.9). Its impedance near resonance,

\[ Z(\omega) = \frac{R_p}{1 + jQ_0\left(\dfrac{\omega}{\omega_0} - \dfrac{\omega_0}{\omega}\right)} \approx \frac{R_p}{1 + 2jQ_0\,\Delta\omega/\omega_0}, \]

falls to \(1/\sqrt{2}\) of its peak at \(\Delta\omega = \pm\omega_0/2Q_0\), so the full width is \(f_0/Q_0\).

Pre-requisite (continued): Transmission lines, matching, S-parameters

Pre-requisite. This continues Transmission lines, matching, S-parameters from chapter 8, which used a 50 Ω line and a matched termination, and its continuation in chapter 9, which derived the characteristic impedance \(Z_0 = \sqrt{L'/C'}\) and wrote the voltage on a line as a forward and a backward wave, \(\tilde{V}(z) = V_+e^{-jkz} + V_-e^{jkz}\), with current \((V_+e^{-jkz} - V_-e^{jkz})/Z_0\). This section adds what section 7 and the bench need: the reflection coefficient of any load and the power it sends back; the ideal transformer that lets a coupler present a cavity to a line; the circle a resonance draws on the Smith chart; S-parameters as a network analyser measures them; the directional coupler and the circulator; and the pulse-forming network of a modulator, a line discharged into its own impedance. Taught from Wangler, sections 5.3 to 5.7; Padamsee, Knobloch and Hays, section 8.2; and the Handbook, section 7.3.2.2 (klystron systems, directional couplers).

The reflection coefficient. End a line of impedance \(Z_0\) at \(z = 0\) with a load \(Z_L\). The load forces its own ratio of voltage to current, \(\tilde{V}(0)/\tilde{I}(0) = Z_L\), so

\[ Z_L = Z_0\frac{V_+ + V_-}{V_+ - V_-} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Gamma \equiv \frac{V_-}{V_+} = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0}. \]

A matched load, \(Z_L = Z_0\), gives \(\Gamma = 0\): the forward wave is absorbed entirely and nothing returns (chapter 8; F5, section 11 found the same rule for waves on a string). A short gives \(\Gamma = -1\), an open end \(+1\). Each wave carries power \(|V|^2/2Z_0\) (amplitudes are peak values), so if the forward wave carries \(P_f\), the reflected one carries \(|\Gamma|^2P_f\) and the load takes \((1 - |\Gamma|^2)P_f\). A 75 Ω load on a 50 Ω line has \(\Gamma = 0.2\) and reflects 4 per cent; a 25 Ω load has \(\Gamma = -1/3\) and reflects 11 per cent. Where the two waves add in phase and in opposition along the line, the voltage swings between \(|V_+|(1 + |\Gamma|)\) and \(|V_+|(1 - |\Gamma|)\); the ratio, the voltage standing-wave ratio \((1 + |\Gamma|)/(1 - |\Gamma|)\), was how mismatch was measured with a probe slid along a slotted line.

The ideal transformer. Two windings of \(n_1\) and \(n_2\) turns on one lossless core share the same flux, so their voltages are in the ratio of turns, \(V_2 = V_1/n\) with \(n = n_1/n_2\), and power conservation makes the currents go the other way, \(I_2 = nI_1\). A load \(Z_L\) on the second winding is seen from the first as

\[ Z_{in} = \frac{V_1}{I_1} = \frac{nV_2}{I_2/n} = n^2Z_L . \]

A coupling loop or an iris does the same job for a cavity: near resonance it presents the cavity’s equivalent circuit to the line through an effective turns ratio set by the loop’s size and orientation, or the iris’s size. At resonance the cavity is a resistance \(R_p\) (section 6), and the line sees \(R_p/n^2\). Define the coupling factor as that resistance in units of \(Z_0\), \(\beta_c = R_p/(n^2Z_0)\); it equals section 7’s \(Q_0/Q_{ext}\), because the power leaving through the port with the generator off is the cavity voltage, transformed, across a matched \(Z_0\). Then

\[ \Gamma = \frac{\beta_cZ_0 - Z_0}{\beta_cZ_0 + Z_0} = \frac{\beta_c - 1}{\beta_c + 1}, \qquad \frac{P_c}{P_f} = 1 - \Gamma^2 = \frac{4\beta_c}{(1 + \beta_c)^2}, \]

section 7’s formulas. Undercoupled (\(\beta_c < 1\)), \(\Gamma\) is negative; overcoupled, positive; critically coupled, zero.

A resonance on the Smith chart. Off resonance the cavity’s impedance is \(R_p/(1 + jy)\), with \(y = 2Q_0\Delta f/f_0\) (the driven-oscillator section above), so the line sees \(\beta_cZ_0/(1 + jy)\) and

\[ \Gamma(y) = \frac{\beta_c - 1 - jy}{\beta_c + 1 + jy} = -1 + \frac{2\beta_c}{1 + \beta_c}\cdot\frac{1}{1 + jy/(1 + \beta_c)} . \]

As \(y\) runs from \(-\infty\) to \(+\infty\), \(1/(1 + jy')\) traces a circle of diameter 1 through 0 and 1 in the complex plane (its real and imaginary parts, \(1/(1 + y'^2)\) and \(-y'/(1 + y'^2)\), satisfy \((u - \tfrac12)^2 + v^2 = \tfrac14\)). So \(\Gamma\) traces a circle that starts and ends at \(-1\), far off resonance where the detuned cavity looks like a short at the coupler, and reaches \((\beta_c - 1)/(\beta_c + 1)\) at resonance. Its diameter is \(2\beta_c/(1 + \beta_c)\). The Smith chart is this plane of \(\Gamma\), the unit disc, overprinted with lines of constant normalised resistance and reactance so that impedances can be read off directly. The circle encloses the centre of the chart, \(\Gamma = 0\), only if \((\beta_c - 1)/(\beta_c + 1) > 0\): overcoupled. That is section 7’s rule for telling the two cases apart. (In practice a length of line between coupler and analyser rotates the circle about the centre, which the analyser’s “electrical delay” setting undoes.)

At work. With \(\beta_c = 2\), \(\Gamma\) at resonance is \(+1/3\); at \(y = \pm1\) it is \(0.2 \mp 0.4j\) (magnitude 0.45); at \(y = \pm10\), \(-0.89 \mp 0.37j\), nearly back at \(-1\).

S-parameters. A network analyser (the bench section has it at work) is an instrument that sends a sine wave of known frequency into a device and measures, in amplitude and phase, the waves that come back and come through. It describes a device with ports by the waves that go in and come out. Normalise each port’s waves so that \(|a_i|^2/2\) and \(|b_i|^2/2\) are the powers going in and coming out of port \(i\). For a linear two-port,

\[ b_1 = S_{11}a_1 + S_{12}a_2, \qquad b_2 = S_{21}a_1 + S_{22}a_2 . \]

\(S_{11}\) is the reflection coefficient at port 1 with port 2 matched; \(S_{21}\) is the transmission from 1 to 2. For a cavity with an input coupler \(\beta_1\) and a pick-up \(\beta_2\), both matched: the cavity seen from port 1 has \(R_p\) in parallel with port 2’s load, which appears as \(R_p/\beta_2\), so its normalised resistance is \(\beta_1/(1 + \beta_2)\) and \(S_{11} = (\beta_1 - 1 - \beta_2)/(\beta_1 + 1 + \beta_2)\). The power absorbed is \((1 - |S_{11}|^2)P_f = 4\beta_1(1 + \beta_2)P_f/(1 + \beta_1 + \beta_2)^2\), and the fraction \(\beta_2/(1 + \beta_2)\) of it leaves through port 2. So \(|S_{21}|^2 = 4\beta_1\beta_2/(1 + \beta_1 + \beta_2)^2\), the bench formula. A weak pick-up with \(\beta_2 = 0.01\) on a critically coupled cavity transmits \(|S_{21}| = 0.0995\), \(-20\) dB. (RF engineers count power ratios in decibels: a ratio \(P_2/P_1\) is \(10\log_{10}(P_2/P_1)\) dB, so \(-10\) dB is a tenth, \(-3\) dB a half and \(-60\) dB a millionth. An amplitude ratio is squared first, which makes it \(20\log_{10}\): \(|S_{21}| = 0.0995\) is \(20\log_{10}0.0995 = -20.0\) dB, a hundredth of the power.)

The directional coupler. To protect a klystron and to measure a cavity, one needs the forward and reflected waves separately, not their sum. A directional coupler samples a waveguide through two small holes a quarter guide-wavelength apart into a parallel guide. A forward wave reaches the second hole a quarter period after the first, and so do the waves it couples through them into the side guide, travelling forward: they add. In the backward direction the two coupled waves differ by a half period and cancel. The side arm’s two ends therefore carry samples of the forward and the reflected wave, attenuated by the coupling factor: \(-60\) dB on a 5 MW line is 5 W, enough for a detector.

The circulator. A circulator is a three-port junction containing magnetised ferrite (a ceramic of iron oxides, magnetic like iron but an electrical insulator, so RF fields pass through it), whose response to a rotating RF magnetic field depends on its sense of rotation relative to the static field. Power entering port 1 leaves at port 2, power entering 2 leaves at 3, and power entering 3 leaves at 1: \(|S_{21}| = |S_{32}| = |S_{13}| \approx 1\) while \(S_{12} \approx 0\). Ordinary components are reciprocal (\(S_{12} = S_{21}\)); the static magnetic field breaks that symmetry. With the source on port 1, the cavity on port 2 and a matched load on port 3, everything the cavity reflects goes to the load, and the source sees a match whatever the cavity does. That is how a TESLA cavity can reflect almost all of its forward power during filling (section 7’s exercise) without harm to the klystron.

The pulse-forming network. Charge a lossless line of impedance \(Z_0\) and one-way delay \(T\) to a voltage \(V_c\), with its far end open, and at \(t = 0\) close a switch connecting the near end to a load \(R = Z_0\). The load’s voltage \(V\) and current \(V/Z_0\) must be those of the line at its end. The voltage at the end changes from \(V_c\) to \(V\) by launching a step of size \(V - V_c\) that runs away from the load into the line. A step travelling one way along a line carries a current equal to its voltage over \(Z_0\) in its own direction of travel; this one runs away from the load, so it brings the load a current \((V_c - V)/Z_0\). The load needs \(V/Z_0\). Equating the two gives \(V = V_c/2\). The step runs to the open far end, reflects with \(\Gamma = +1\) and comes back; when it arrives, at \(2T\), it lowers the line to zero, and the pulse ends. So a matched line delivers

\[ V = \frac{V_c}{2} \quad\text{for a time}\quad 2T . \]

A chain of \(n\) lumped sections of \(L_s\) and \(C_s\) is a line with \(Z_0 = \sqrt{L_s/C_s}\) and delay \(\sqrt{L_sC_s}\) per section (chapter 9), so it delivers a pulse of length \(2n\sqrt{L_sC_s}\). Energy checks: the network stores \(\tfrac12nC_sV_c^2\), and the load receives \((V_c/2)^2/Z_0\times2n\sqrt{L_sC_s} = \tfrac12nC_sV_c^2\), all of it.

At work. An illustrative modulator for a klystron gun of 400 kV and 300 A (a load of 1333 Ω) with a 1:15 pulse transformer: the network sees \(1333/15^2 = 5.93\ \Omega\) (the transformer rule above) and must be charged to \(2\times400/15 = 53.3\) kV. For a 3 µs pulse from ten sections, \(\sqrt{L_sC_s} = 0.15\) µs, so \(C_s = 0.15\ \mu\text{s}/5.93\ \Omega = 25.3\) nF and \(L_s = 0.15\ \mu\text{s}\times5.93\ \Omega = 0.89\) µH. It stores 360 J, exactly the \(400\ \text{kV}\times300\ \text{A}\times3\ \mu\text{s}\) the klystron takes.

Pre-requisite (continued): Fourier series and transforms

Pre-requisite. This continues Fourier series and transforms from chapter 8, which took the Fourier transform of a signal, and its continuation in chapter 9, which took the transform of a field profile and showed that a Gaussian’s transform is a Gaussian, and its continuation in chapter 12, which gave the Fourier series of a function that repeats round a ring. Here the periodic signal is in time: a train of bunches arriving once per RF period. The section recalls the Fourier series and its coefficients; shows that a train of short bunches carries an RF current of twice its average current at every harmonic; and derives the klystron’s harmonics \(2I_0J_n(nX)\) by a change of variable. Section 7 uses the first result for beam loading, section 9 the second. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 2, §49 (spectral resolution), with Bessel’s integral from the Bessel section before section 2.

The Fourier series. (F5, section 16 gave the idea; here are the working formulas.) A function \(f(t)\) repeating every \(T\) seconds, with \(\omega = 2\pi/T\), can be written as a constant plus harmonics of \(\omega\):

\[ f(t) = a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[a_n\cos n\omega t + b_n\sin n\omega t\right]. \]

To find a coefficient, multiply both sides by \(\cos m\omega t\) and average over one period. Every product of two different harmonics averages to zero (each product is half the sum of two sinusoids at the sum and difference frequencies, both non-zero, so both average to zero), and \(\cos^2m\omega t\) averages to \(\tfrac12\). So

\[ \begin{aligned} a_0 &= \langle f\rangle, \qquad a_n = 2\langle f(t)\cos n\omega t\rangle = \frac{2}{T}\int_0^Tf(t)\cos n\omega t\,dt, \\ b_n &= \frac{2}{T}\int_0^Tf(t)\sin n\omega t\,dt . \end{aligned} \]

The amplitude of the \(n\)-th harmonic is \(\sqrt{a_n^2 + b_n^2}\); for a function even about \(t = 0\) all \(b_n\) vanish.

A train of short bunches. Bunches of charge \(q_b\) (C) pass a point once every period, at \(t = 0, \pm T, \pm2T, \dots\). Their average current is \(I_b = q_b/T\). If each bunch is short compared with the period, all its charge passes at essentially one instant, and within one period only the bunch at \(t = 0\) contributes to the integrals, with \(\cos(n\omega\cdot0) = 1\):

\[ a_n = \frac{2}{T}\int(\text{current of one bunch})\,dt = \frac{2q_b}{T} = 2I_b . \]

Every harmonic, the fundamental included, carries an RF current of amplitude twice the DC current. A bunch of finite length spreads its charge over a range of times, and each part enters with its own phase \(\cos n\omega t\). For a Gaussian bunch of rms duration \(\sigma_t\) (s), the cosine-weighted average of a Gaussian (chapter 9) gives \(a_n = 2I_be^{-(n\omega\sigma_t)^2/2}\): the harmonics fall off once \(n\omega\sigma_t\) approaches one.

At work. The TESLA beam of 8 mA in bunches of rms length \(\sigma_z = 1\) mm (\(\sigma_t = 3.3\) ps) has \(\omega\sigma_t = 0.027\) at 1.3 GHz; the factor is 0.9996, and the RF current is 16.0 mA. Crossing the cavity on crest, it takes \(\tfrac12\times2I_b\times\hat{V} = I_b\hat{V}\), section 7’s beam power. Even a 10 mm bunch would keep 96 per cent.

The klystron’s bunched current. Section 9 finds that an electron leaving the buncher at time \(t_0\) arrives at the catcher at a time \(t\) with

\[ \omega t' = \omega t_0 - X\sin\omega t_0, \qquad t' = t - L_d/v_0, \]

where \(X\) is the bunching parameter. Charge is conserved: the charge leaving in \(dt_0\), \(I_0\,dt_0\), arrives in the corresponding \(dt\), so \(I(t)\,dt = I_0\,dt_0\). The arriving current \(I(t)\) is awkward to write down, because when \(X > 1\) fast electrons overtake slow ones and several departure times map to one arrival time. But the Fourier coefficients never need \(I(t)\) itself. In the integral for \(a_n\), replace \(I(t)\,dt\) by \(I_0\,dt_0\) and express the cosine through \(t_0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} a_n &= \frac{2}{T}\int_0^TI(t)\cos n\omega t'\,dt = \frac{2I_0}{T}\int_0^T\cos\left[n(\omega t_0 - X\sin\omega t_0)\right]dt_0 \\ &= 2I_0\cdot\frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\pi\cos(n\theta - nX\sin\theta)\,d\theta . \end{aligned} \]

The last step put \(\theta = \omega t_0\) and used the symmetry of the integrand about \(\theta = \pi\). The integral is Bessel’s integral for \(J_n(nX)\) (the Bessel section before section 2), so

\[ a_n = 2I_0J_n(nX), \qquad b_n = 0, \]

where \(b_n\) vanishes because its integrand is odd about \(\theta = 0\). The change of variable counts every electron once, whether or not it has overtaken another, so the result holds for any \(X\). This is Webster’s 1939 result quoted in section 9.

At work. At \(X = 1.841\) the harmonics are \(2I_0J_1 = 1.164I_0\), \(2I_0J_2(3.682) = 0.863I_0\), \(2I_0J_3(5.523) = 0.500I_0\): strong harmonics, as expected of sharp bunches. At \(X = 1\) the fundamental is only \(0.88I_0\); past the optimum, at \(X = 2.5\), it has fallen back to \(0.99I_0\) because the bunches have passed through their tightest point and spread again. A direct numerical average of \(\cos n(\theta - X\sin\theta)\) over many departure phases gives the same numbers.

Exercise M1. A proton beam of 30 mA average current is bunched at 352.2 MHz with an rms bunch length of 30 ps. What is the amplitude of the RF current at the fundamental and at the fifth harmonic?

\(\omega\sigma_t = 2\pi\times3.522\times10^8\times3\times10^{-11} = 0.0664\). Fundamental: \(2\times30\times e^{-0.0664^2/2} = 59.9\) mA. Fifth harmonic: \(n\omega\sigma_t = 0.332\), \(e^{-0.055} = 0.946\), so 56.8 mA. A pick-up tuned to the fifth harmonic still sees almost the full \(2I_b\).

Exercise M2. Show that the DC term of the klystron’s current, \(a_0\), is \(I_0\) whatever \(X\).

Answer

\(a_0 = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^TI(t)\,dt = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^TI_0\,dt_0 = I_0\). Bunching rearranges the charge in time but neither creates nor destroys it, so the average current is unchanged.

Exercise M3. Why does the RF current amplitude \(2I_0J_1(X)\) not keep growing as the bunches get tighter?

Answer

\(2J_1(X)\) is largest, 1.164, at \(X = 1.841\). Beyond it, electrons that started on one side of the bunch overtake the centre and arrive on the other side, so part of the charge arrives at phases where it adds less to the fundamental. The infinitely sharp bunch, with \(a_1 = 2I_0\), cannot be reached by ballistic bunching from a sinusoidal velocity modulation, because the electrons from the two ends of the period never arrive together with those from the centre.

7. Coupling, filling and beam loading

Couplers. Power enters through a loop that links the magnetic field near the wall, an antenna that reaches into the electric field, or an iris, a hole into a waveguide (figure 13.6). Through the same port, energy stored in the cavity also leaks back out to the line. Define the external quality factor \(Q_{ext} = \omega U/P_{ext}\), where \(P_{ext}\) is the power that would leave through the port if the generator were switched off and the line ended in a matched load. Losses add, so

\[ \frac{1}{Q_L} = \frac{1}{Q_0} + \frac{1}{Q_{ext}}, \qquad \beta_c = \frac{P_{ext}}{P} = \frac{Q_0}{Q_{ext}}, \qquad Q_L = \frac{Q_0}{1 + \beta_c}. \]

\(\beta_c\) is the coupling factor (written \(\beta\) in RF texts; subscripted here to keep it from the particle’s speed). \(Q_L\) is the loaded quality factor, which sets the bandwidth \(f_0/Q_L\) and the field decay time \(2Q_L/\omega_0\).

Figure 13.6. Three ways in, each a pillbox in side section, schematic. In the TM\(_{010}\) mode the electric field runs along the axis and the magnetic field circles it, strongest near the outer wall. (a) A loop at the outer wall links the magnetic flux. (b) An antenna reaches into the electric field. (c) An iris, a hole in the wall, opens into a waveguide.

Reflection. Seen from the line through an ideal transformer, a cavity at resonance is a resistance \(\beta_c\) times the line impedance \(Z_0\). A wave of forward power \(P_f\) meets the reflection coefficient

\[ \Gamma = \frac{\beta_c - 1}{\beta_c + 1}, \qquad P_r = \Gamma^2P_f, \qquad P_c = P_f\frac{4\beta_c}{(1 + \beta_c)^2}. \]

At critical coupling, \(\beta_c = 1\), nothing is reflected and \(Q_L = Q_0/2\). With \(\beta_c = 0.5\) or 2, 11 per cent of the power comes back. Since \(P_c = \hat{V}^2/R_{sh}\), the voltage in steady state is \(\hat{V} = [2\sqrt{\beta_c}/(1+\beta_c)]\sqrt{P_fR_{sh}}\). A circulator between the source and the cavity sends the reflected wave into a load, so the source always sees a match.

Beam loading, in outline. A bunched beam passing through the cavity is a current at the RF frequency. Crossing at the crest, it takes power \(P_b = I_b\hat{V}\), where \(I_b\) is the average beam current during the pulse (bunches short compared with a period carry an RF current of amplitude \(2I_b\), and \(\tfrac12 \cdot 2I_b \cdot \hat{V} = I_b\hat{V}\)); it acts as an extra resistance in parallel with the walls. The generator sees a match when \(\beta_c = 1 + P_b/P\): the coupler must be designed for the beam current. Off crest, the beam current also has a reactive part, which detunes the cavity; and the beam’s own induced voltage, the wakefield, can act back on later bunches. The steady state for accelerating structures is in chapter 14; the instabilities it can drive in chapter 22.

Worked number: the TESLA cavity. The nine-cell 1.3 GHz niobium cavity of the TESLA Test Facility (Aune and others 2000) has an active length of 1.038 m and \(R/Q = 518\ \Omega\) in the circuit convention, so \(1036\ \Omega\) in ours. At 25 MV/m it gives \(\hat{V} = 25.95\) MV, and an 8 mA beam on crest takes \(P_b = 208\) kW. The wall loss is tens of watts (section 11), so \(\beta_c \gg 1\) and \(P_b\) is essentially all the generator must supply. Matching means \(P_{ext} = \hat{V}^2/[(R/Q)Q_{ext}] = P_b\):

\[ Q_{ext} = \frac{\hat{V}}{(R/Q)\,I_b} = \frac{25.95\times10^6}{1036\times0.008} = 3.13\times10^6 . \]

Aune and others give \(Q_{ext} = 3\times10^6\), a bandwidth of 430 Hz and a peak power of 208 kW. With \(\beta_c \gg 1\), the steady-state voltage formula above becomes \(\hat{V} \approx 2\sqrt{P_fR_{sh}/\beta_c} = 2\sqrt{P_f(R/Q)Q_{ext}}\); with no beam and \(P_f = P_b\) this is 51.9 MV, exactly twice the working voltage. So the cavity is filled with full power and no beam; the field rises as \(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\) with \(\tau = 2Q_L/\omega\), here \(Q_L \approx Q_{ext} = 3.13\times10^6\) and \(\tau = 767\) µs, and reaches half its asymptote, the working value, after \(\tau\ln2 = 531\) µs. The beam is then injected and absorbs exactly the power that would otherwise have pushed the field higher. Aune and others quote a filling time of 530 µs, then an 800 µs flat top.

Figure 13.7. One RF pulse of a TESLA cavity, computed with \(\tau = 2Q_L/\omega = 767\) µs. With full forward power and no beam, the voltage heads for twice the working value along \(2\hat{V}(1 - e^{-t/\tau})\) and reaches \(\hat{V}\) at \(\tau\ln2 = 531\) µs. When the RF and the beam stop, the stored energy drains out through the coupler and the voltage decays as \(e^{-t/\tau}\).

Tuning. A cavity’s frequency drifts with temperature, pressure and its own fields, and must be pulled back with a tuner: a plunger, a squeezing frame or, in ferrite-loaded cavities for proton synchrotrons, a magnetic bias that changes \(\mu\) (chapter 19). Copper expands by 16.6 ppm per kelvin, so the 2856 MHz cell drops by 47.5 kHz for each kelvin it warms (the Handbook’s table of materials, section 3.3.12, gives 16.5 ppm, or 47.1 kHz), against a loaded bandwidth of 310 kHz at critical coupling; cooling water is regulated to a fraction of a kelvin. In the TESLA cavity the field’s own pressure on the walls (Lorentz-force detuning) shifts the frequency by about 600 Hz at 25 MV/m, more than the 430 Hz bandwidth (Aune and others 2000); the tuner moves the cavity length at 315 kHz per mm, so 600 Hz is 1.9 µm. A detuning \(\Delta f\) costs power. With \(\beta_c \gg 1\) and no beam, holding the voltage needs \(1 + (2Q_L\Delta f/f)^2\) times the power at resonance, twice as much at half the bandwidth, about 215 Hz. With the matched beam on crest, the generator must supply the beam as well and the factor is \(1 + (Q_L\Delta f/f)^2\), twice as much only when \(\Delta f\) reaches the full bandwidth. (In the circuit, the generator current must drive the in-phase current \(\hat{V}/R_L\) of the loaded cavity, \(R_L\) its loaded resistance, plus an equal beam current, and the detuning adds a reactive current \(2Q_L(\Delta f/f)\hat{V}/R_L\); the forward power goes as the squared magnitude of the sum, \(4 + (2Q_L\Delta f/f)^2\) against 4.) Aune and others estimate about 15 per cent extra power for the TESLA cavity’s detuning during the pulse.

Pre-requisite: Waveguides, TE and TM modes, cutoff, phase and group velocity

Pre-requisite. This section treats a hollow metal pipe as a carrier of waves. It shows that the fields in a pipe split into two families, transverse electric (TE) and transverse magnetic (TM), labelled by two whole numbers; that each mode travels only above its own cutoff frequency, fixed by the pipe’s size; that below cutoff a mode does not travel but decays along the pipe (it is evanescent); and that energy and signals move at the group velocity \(d\omega/dk\), not the phase velocity. Section 8 derives the lowest mode of a rectangular guide and uses the rest; beam pipes, couplers and the higher-order modes of chapter 22 rely on cutoff and evanescence. Taught from Wangler, sections 1.10 and 1.12 to 1.14, with the Maxwell and conductor sections before section 2.

The problem. A long pipe with perfectly conducting walls runs along \(z\). Look for fields travelling along it at one frequency, \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}(x, y)e^{j(\omega t - k_gz)}\) and the same for \(\mathbf{H}\), with \(k_g\) the guide wavenumber. Each Cartesian component obeys the Helmholtz equation; with \(\partial^2/\partial z^2 \to -k_g^2\) it becomes an equation in the cross-section,

\[ \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial y^2} + k_c^2\psi = 0, \qquad k_c^2 = \frac{\omega^2}{c^2} - k_g^2 , \]

where \(\psi\) stands for \(\mathcal{E}_z\) or \(H_z\). Writing out the two curl equations component by component, with \(\partial/\partial z \to -jk_g\), shows that once \(\mathcal{E}_z\) and \(H_z\) are known, all the transverse components follow from their derivatives (the algebra is in Wangler, section 1.10; the TE\(_{10}\) case is worked just below). So there are two independent families. In TM modes \(H_z = 0\) and everything follows from \(\mathcal{E}_z\); in TE modes \(\mathcal{E}_z = 0\) and everything follows from \(H_z\). (A wave with neither, a TEM wave, needs two separate conductors, as in a coaxial line; a single hollow pipe cannot carry one.)

Section 8’s mode, worked out. Take a rectangular guide, broad wall \(a_w\) along \(x\), narrow wall \(b_w\) along \(y\), and try \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = \mathcal{E}_y(x)\hat{\mathbf{y}}\). Faraday’s law in phasor form, \(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -j\omega\mu_0\mathbf{H}\), gives two components:

\[ H_x = -\frac{k_g}{\omega\mu_0}\mathcal{E}_y, \qquad H_z = \frac{j}{\omega\mu_0}\frac{d\mathcal{E}_y}{dx} . \]

\(\mathcal{E}_y\) is tangential to the narrow walls at \(x = 0\) and \(x = a_w\), so it must vanish there; it is normal to the broad walls, which is allowed. \(\mathcal{E}_y = \mathcal{E}_0\sin(\pi x/a_w)\) fits, with \(k_c = \pi/a_w\). Then \(H_z \propto \cos(\pi x/a_w)\), whose slope vanishes at the narrow walls: zero normal \(H\) there, as the conductor requires. There is no \(\mathcal{E}_z\) and there is an \(H_z\): this is a TE mode, TE\(_{10}\).

The general modes and their names. For TE modes in the rectangle, \(H_z = H_0\cos(m\pi x/a_w)\cos(n\pi y/b_w)\), whose normal slope vanishes on all four walls. For TM modes, \(\mathcal{E}_z = \mathcal{E}_0\sin(m\pi x/a_w)\sin(n\pi y/b_w)\), which vanishes on all four walls. In both,

\[ k_c^2 = \left(\frac{m\pi}{a_w}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{n\pi}{b_w}\right)^2, \qquad f_{c,mn} = \frac{c}{2}\sqrt{\left(\frac{m}{a_w}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{n}{b_w}\right)^2} . \]

The indices count half-cycles of the field pattern across the broad wall (\(m\)) and the narrow wall (\(n\)). For TE, one of them may be zero; for TM neither can be, since \(\sin0 = 0\) everywhere. So in a guide with \(a_w > b_w\) the lowest mode is TE\(_{10}\), with \(f_c = c/2a_w\): the broad wall must be at least half a free-space wavelength. In a round pipe the same reasoning in cylindrical coordinates gives Bessel functions across the radius, and the indices count the field’s variation round the angle and along the radius (Wangler, sections 1.13 and 1.14). The two lowest modes of a pipe of radius \(R\) are TE\(_{11}\), cut off at \(1.841c/2\pi R\), and TM\(_{01}\), at \(2.405c/2\pi R\), from the maximum of \(J_1\) and the zero of \(J_0\). The cavity labels of section 2, TM\(_{010}\), use the same scheme with a third index for the variation along \(z\).

At work. For WR-284 (72.14 by 34.04 mm) the cutoffs are 2.078 GHz (TE\(_{10}\)), 4.156 GHz (TE\(_{20}\)), 4.404 GHz (TE\(_{01}\)) and 4.869 GHz (TE\(_{11}\) and TM\(_{11}\) together). At 2856 MHz only TE\(_{10}\) can travel, so the field pattern is unique and power cannot leak into other modes at a bend. WR-650 (165.1 by 82.55 mm), at 1.3 GHz, has TE\(_{10}\) at 0.908 GHz and the next two together at 1.816 GHz. Rectangular guides are used between about 1.25 and 1.9 times the TE\(_{10}\) cutoff for this reason.

Below cutoff: evanescent fields. If \(\omega/c < k_c\), then \(k_g^2 = \omega^2/c^2 - k_c^2\) is negative, and \(k_g = -j\alpha\) with

\[ \alpha = \sqrt{k_c^2 - \frac{\omega^2}{c^2}} \quad (\text{m}^{-1}). \]

The factor \(e^{-jk_gz}\) becomes \(e^{-\alpha z}\): the field does not travel but dies away exponentially, with the same phase everywhere. Its electric and magnetic fields are a quarter period apart, so it carries no average power. A pipe below cutoff is a seal. The TESLA cavity’s irises, 35 mm in radius, cut off TE\(_{11}\) at 2.51 GHz and TM\(_{01}\) at 3.28 GHz. The 1.3 GHz accelerating field leaking into a beam pipe of that radius in the TM\(_{01}\) pattern decays with \(\alpha = 63\) m⁻¹: by \(1/e\) in 16 mm, and to a thousandth in 110 mm. A few pipe radii are enough to contain the field. Modes of the cavity above the pipe’s cutoff, its higher-order modes, can travel out, which is how they are extracted to loads.

Phase velocity and group velocity. (F5, section 12 introduced both; here they are for the guide.) Above cutoff the guide’s dispersion relation is \(\omega^2 = \omega_c^2 + c^2k_g^2\), the definition of \(k_c^2\) above multiplied by \(c^2\), with \(\omega_c = ck_c\). A crest moves at the phase velocity \(v_{ph} = \omega/k_g\), which exceeds \(c\). A signal or a burst of energy is not a single wave but a group of waves at nearby frequencies. Add two of equal amplitude at \(\omega_1, k_1\) and \(\omega_2, k_2\):

\[ e^{j(\omega_1t - k_1z)} + e^{j(\omega_2t - k_2z)} = 2\cos\left[\frac{(\omega_1 - \omega_2)t - (k_1 - k_2)z}{2}\right]e^{j(\bar{\omega}t - \bar{k}z)}, \]

with \(\bar{\omega}\) and \(\bar{k}\) the means. The fast factor is a wave at the mean frequency moving at \(\bar{\omega}/\bar{k}\); the cosine is an envelope, and it moves at \((\omega_1 - \omega_2)/(k_1 - k_2)\), which for close frequencies is

\[ v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk_g} = \frac{c^2k_g}{\omega} = c\sqrt{1 - (f_c/f)^2} < c . \]

So \(v_{ph}v_g = c^2\). Information and energy travel with the envelope. A direct check of the energy: the TE\(_{10}\) wave carries a power \(P = (\mathcal{E}_0^2a_wb_w/4\eta_0)\sqrt{1 - (f_c/f)^2}\) (from \(\tfrac12\mathrm{Re}[\mathcal{E}_yH_x^*]\) integrated over the cross-section), and stores per metre of guide a time-averaged energy \(\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}_0^2a_wb_w/4\) (electric and magnetic equal). Their ratio, power over energy per length, is \(c\sqrt{1 - (f_c/f)^2}\): the group velocity is the speed of the energy.

At work. In WR-284 at 2856 MHz, \(v_g = 0.686c\); a numerical check, \((\omega_2 - \omega_1)/(k_2 - k_1)\) for 2855 and 2857 MHz, gives the same to six figures. The 21 MW pulses of SLAC’s first klystrons give, in this guide, \(\mathcal{E}_0 = \sqrt{4\eta_0P/(a_wb_w\sqrt{1 - (f_c/f)^2})} = 4.3\) MV/m at the centre of the broad wall, above the 3 MV/m at which air breaks down: high-power guides must be evacuated or filled with an insulating gas under pressure.

8. Waveguides: the TE10 mode

The picture. A rectangular metal pipe, broad side \(a_w\) and narrow side \(b_w\), carries a wave that bounces between the narrow walls at an angle. The wave fits only if the walls are far enough apart: at least half a wavelength. Below that frequency it is evanescent and does not travel.

Derivation. Let the wave travel along \(z\) as \(e^{j(\omega t - k_gz)}\), where \(k_g\) is the guide wavenumber. Try \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = \mathcal{E}_y\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) with \(\mathcal{E}_y = \mathcal{E}_0\sin(\pi x/a_w)\), which vanishes at the narrow walls \(x = 0\) and \(x = a_w\) and is normal to the broad walls. In the Helmholtz equation, \(\partial^2/\partial x^2\) of the sine gives \(-(\pi/a_w)^2\) times it, \(\partial^2/\partial z^2\) of \(e^{-jk_gz}\) gives \(-k_g^2\), nothing depends on \(y\), and \(k^2 = \omega^2/c^2\); dividing by \(\mathcal{E}_y\),

\[ -\left(\frac{\pi}{a_w}\right)^2 - k_g^2 + \frac{\omega^2}{c^2} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k_g = \sqrt{\frac{\omega^2}{c^2} - \left(\frac{\pi}{a_w}\right)^2}. \]

The magnetic field has a transverse \(x\) component and a longitudinal \(z\) component, so this is a transverse electric mode, TE\(_{10}\). \(k_g\) is real only above the cutoff \(f_c = c/2a_w\). The phase velocity and group velocity are

\[ v_{ph} = \frac{\omega}{k_g} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{1 - (f_c/f)^2}} > c, \qquad v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk_g} = c\sqrt{1 - (f_c/f)^2} < c, \qquad v_{ph}v_g = c^2 . \]

The phase velocity exceeds \(c\), so a smooth waveguide cannot accelerate a particle: no particle keeps step with the wave. Loading the guide with irises slows the wave to \(c\), which is the travelling-wave linac (chapter 14). Energy and signals travel at \(v_g\).

Numbers. Rectangular guides are named WR plus the broad inside width in hundredths of an inch. WR-284 is 72.14 by 34.04 mm: TE\(_{10}\) cuts off at 2.078 GHz, the next modes (TE\(_{20}\), TE\(_{01}\)) at 4.156 and 4.404 GHz, so at 2856 MHz only TE\(_{10}\) travels. There the guide wavelength is 153.0 mm against 105.0 mm in free space, \(v_{ph} = 1.458c\) and \(v_g = 0.686c\). WR-650 (165.1 by 82.55 mm), used at 1.3 GHz, cuts off at 0.908 GHz and has \(v_g = 0.716c\).

9. Power sources

Triodes and tetrodes

Below a few hundred megahertz the gridded valve still works and is the cheapest source of large continuous power. A tetrode adds a screen grid that shields the control grid from the anode. The electrons’ transit time (see The wall) sets the upper end, together with the resonance of the valve’s own lead inductance and electrode capacitance (chapter 9). Valve amplifiers drive the cavities of cyclotrons (chapter 10) and of proton synchrotrons, whose frequency must sweep as the protons speed up (chapter 15).

Pre-requisite (continued): Debye shielding and plasma frequency

Pre-requisite. This continues Debye shielding and plasma frequency from chapter 8, which used the plasma frequency of a metal’s electrons as the upper limit of its reflection. Here the plasma is an electron beam. The section derives the plasma frequency of a beam, with its relativistic correction, and shows why space charge undoes ballistic bunching after a quarter of a plasma wavelength. The klystron section that follows uses both. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, section 8.6.3 (plasma oscillations), and Wangler, sections 9.12 and 9.13 (the beam plasma frequency).

The oscillation. Take a uniform cloud of electrons, \(n\) per cubic metre, with their average charge neutralised or, in a beam, simply taken as the background about which the density varies. Shift a slab of electrons sideways by a small distance \(\xi\) along \(z\). One face of the slab is left with a sheet of uncovered charge \(+ne\xi\) per unit area and the other gains \(-ne\xi\). Between the two sheets, as between the plates of a capacitor, the field is \(\mathcal{E} = ne\xi/\varepsilon_0\), and it pulls the electrons back:

\[ m_e\ddot{\xi} = -eE = -\frac{ne^2}{\varepsilon_0}\xi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega_p = \sqrt{\frac{ne^2}{\varepsilon_0m_e}} . \]

Displaced charge oscillates at the plasma frequency \(\omega_p\), whatever the displacement’s shape, as long as it is small.

In a moving beam. Now let the electrons move together at \(\beta c\) along \(z\), with \(\gamma = (1 - \beta^2)^{-1/2}\), and consider the bunching oscillation along the motion. Go to the frame moving with the beam. There the electrons are at rest and the density is lower, \(n/\gamma\), because the lab sees the beam contracted along \(z\) by \(\gamma\) (length contraction and time dilation are taught in chapter 1); the plasma frequency there is \(\sqrt{ne^2/\gamma\varepsilon_0m_e}\). Back in the lab, time runs \(\gamma\) times slower on the beam’s clocks, so the frequency the lab sees is smaller by \(\gamma\) again:

\[ \omega_p = \sqrt{\frac{ne^2}{\varepsilon_0m_e\gamma^3}} . \]

The same \(\gamma^3\) appears as in the longitudinal mass of chapter 1: a fast beam is stiff against being bunched by its own field. The density follows from the current, \(n = I/(e\beta c\pi r_b^2)\) for a round beam of radius \(r_b\). In a beam of finite width inside a metal pipe, the field of a density ripple is weaker than between infinite sheets, because its lines also reach sideways to the pipe; the effective frequency is \(\omega_q = F_q\omega_p\) with a plasma reduction factor \(F_q < 1\) (written \(R_p\) in klystron texts; not section 6’s parallel resistance) that depends on the beam and pipe radii and on the length of the ripple.

Space-charge waves and the quarter wavelength. A velocity modulation imposed on the beam, as by a klystron’s buncher, turns into a density modulation as the beam drifts. Ballistically, the bunching would keep growing in proportion to the drift distance. With space charge, the density ripple and the velocity ripple exchange energy like the displacement and velocity of an oscillator at \(\omega_q\), travelling with the beam. Starting from pure velocity modulation, the density ripple is greatest a quarter of an oscillation later, after a drift time \(\pi/2\omega_q\), a distance

\[ \frac{\lambda_q}{4} = \frac{\pi\beta c}{2\omega_q}, \qquad \lambda_q = \frac{2\pi\beta c}{\omega_q}, \]

the quarter plasma wavelength. Drifting further, space charge pushes the bunch apart again. The ballistic theory of section 9 holds only if its optimum drift is much shorter than \(\lambda_q/4\).

At work. Take the 420 kV, 310 A beam of section 9’s X-band klystron (\(\gamma = 1.822\), \(\beta = 0.836\)) and assume a beam radius of 3 mm (an illustrative value). Then \(n = 2.7\times10^{17}\) m⁻³ and \(\omega_p/2\pi = 1.9\) GHz, so \(\lambda_p/4 = 3.3\) cm before the reduction factor; with \(F_q\) of a few tenths it is perhaps 10 cm. The ballistic optimum that section 9 finds for this beam with \(V_1 = 0.05V_0\) is 2.6 m at 2856 MHz, and still 0.6 m at 12 GHz. Space charge, not the drift, sets the bunching distance in a high-perveance tube, which is why such tubes use several intermediate cavities, each bunching over a short distance. A non-relativistic estimate would have made \(\omega_p\) larger by \(\gamma^{3/2} = 2.46\).

Exercise O2. At fixed current and radius, how does \(\omega_p\) scale with beam voltage for a non-relativistic beam?

Answer

\(n \propto I/v \propto V^{-1/2}\), so \(\omega_p \propto n^{1/2} \propto V^{-1/4}\), and \(\lambda_p/4 \propto v/\omega_p \propto V^{3/4}\). A higher voltage beam is more dilute and faster, so space charge takes longer to act: one reason klystron voltages rise with power.

The klystron

The picture. A steady electron beam of voltage \(V_0\) and current \(I_0\) passes through a first cavity, the buncher, whose gap holds a small RF voltage \(V_1\sin\omega t\). Electrons crossing on one half-cycle are sped up slightly; on the other, slowed. Then the beam drifts. Fast electrons catch the slow ones ahead of them, and the beam gathers into bunches, one per period. The bunched beam crosses a second cavity, the catcher, and induces a large RF voltage in it, in a phase that decelerates the bunches. The beam’s DC energy becomes RF energy. No grid has to keep up with the electrons: the transit time is the mechanism.

Ballistic bunching. Non-relativistically, an electron crossing the buncher at time \(t_0\) has gained energy \(e(V_0 + V_1\sin\omega t_0)\) in all, so \(\tfrac12mv^2 = e(V_0 + V_1\sin\omega t_0)\); dividing by \(\tfrac12mv_0^2 = eV_0\), it leaves with speed \(v = v_0\sqrt{1 + (V_1/V_0)\sin\omega t_0} \approx v_0[1 + (V_1/2V_0)\sin\omega t_0]\), for \(V_1 \ll V_0\) and a thin gap (using \(\sqrt{1 + u} \approx 1 + u/2\), F1, section 8). It reaches the catcher, a distance \(L_d\) away, at

\[ t = t_0 + \frac{L_d}{v} \approx t_0 + \frac{L_d}{v_0} - \frac{L_d}{v_0}\frac{V_1}{2V_0}\sin\omega t_0 . \]

where the travel time \(L_d/v\) was expanded in the same way, \(1/(1 + u) \approx 1 - u\). Multiply by \(\omega\): \(\omega t - \omega L_d/v_0 = \omega t_0 - X\sin\omega t_0\), with the bunching parameter \(X = (V_1/2V_0)(\omega L_d/v_0)\). Charge is conserved, \(I(t)\,dt = I_0\,dt_0\), and the arriving current is periodic. Its Fourier series, worked out in the Fourier section before section 7 by changing the variable of integration from the arrival time \(t\) to the departure time \(t_0\) (the relation between the two times has the form of Kepler’s equation for a planet’s position along its orbit, \(M = E - e\sin E\), and Bessel’s functions first arose in solving that problem), has harmonics

\[ I(t) = I_0 + 2I_0\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}J_n(nX)\cos\left[n\left(\omega t - \frac{\omega L_d}{v_0}\right)\right] \]

(Webster 1939). The fundamental, \(2I_0J_1(X)\), is largest at \(X = 1.841\), where \(J_1 = 0.582\): the RF current is 1.16 times the DC current. If the catcher’s voltage could swing to the full \(V_0\), the power out would be \(\tfrac12(2I_0J_1)V_0\), an efficiency of \(J_1(1.841) = 58\) per cent for this two-cavity tube.

Figure 13.9. Top: a two-cavity klystron, not to scale. A steady beam from the gun crosses the buncher, whose small RF voltage speeds some electrons and slows others, drifts a length \(L_d\), and gives up energy in the catcher. Bottom: an Applegate diagram, distance along the drift against time, computed from \(v = v_0(1 + \tfrac{V_1}{2V_0}\sin\omega t_0)\) for electrons leaving the buncher every sixteenth of a period. The drift takes three periods and \(V_1/2V_0 = 0.098\), so \(X = 1.841\) at the catcher. Electrons leaving as the buncher voltage rises through zero (slow ones first, fast ones after) converge, and arrive at the catcher as one bunch per period.

Where the model fails. A relativistic beam is stiff: since \(dp = \gamma^3m\,dv\), a voltage \(V_1\) changes the speed by \(\Delta v/v = qV_1/(\gamma^3\beta^2mc^2)\), not \(V_1/2V_0\). (The energy gained is \(dW = qV_1\), and \(dW = v\,dp\), the work-energy rule of F3, section 4 with \(F = dp/dt\); so \(qV_1 = v\gamma^3m\,\Delta v\), and dividing by \(v^2 = \beta^2c^2\) gives the result.) At 420 kV (\(\gamma = 1.822\), \(\beta = 0.836\)) and \(V_1 = 0.05V_0\), \(\Delta v/v = 0.0097\) instead of the non-relativistic 0.025, and at 2856 MHz the optimum drift would be 2.6 m. The bunch’s own space charge pushes it apart, with the beam’s plasma frequency \(\omega_p\) setting a maximum useful drift, a quarter of a plasma wavelength. Real high-power klystrons therefore use a cascade of several intermediate cavities, tuned slightly off the drive frequency, to bunch hard over a shorter distance, and shape the output cavity to slow the electrons as evenly as possible.

Efficiency and perveance. A gun’s space-charge limit (chapter 7) gives \(I_0 = KV_0^{3/2}\), with \(K\) the perveance (A V\(^{-3/2}\); \(10^{-6}\) A V\(^{-3/2}\) is one microperv; not the surface current \(\mathbf{K}\) of section 2). A low perveance means weak space charge and tighter bunches, so higher efficiency, but needs a higher voltage for the same power. The multi-beam klystron divides the current among several beams, each of low perveance, in one tube.

Real tubes. The SLAC linac opened in 1966 with klystrons giving pulses of about 21 MW, a few microseconds long, at 2856 MHz; the tube developed for the SLC in the 1980s gave 65 MW (Neal 1968; Caryotakis 1998). The X-band klystron used in CLIC’s test work gives 50 MW in 1.5 µs pulses at 50 Hz with 44 per cent efficiency, from a 420 kV modulator, with a perveance of 1.15 µperv: \(1.15\times10^{-6} \times (4.2\times10^5)^{3/2} = 313\) A, against the peak current of 310 A in the table, printed there as 2 × 310 A (CLIC Project Implementation Plan 2018, table 5.8). Taken at face value, 420 kV and 310 A is a beam of 130 MW, and 50 MW out of it is 38 per cent, not the 44 per cent the same table lists; the table’s figures do not all refer to one operating point. For the CLIC drive beam, a six-beam multi-beam klystron from Toshiba at 1 GHz reached 21 MW with 71.5 per cent efficiency in factory tests, and a ten-beam tube from Thales 73 per cent (CLIC Project Implementation Plan 2018). The specified tube runs at 160 to 170 kV, 180 A and 2.8 µperv: 165 kV and 180 A is a beam of 29.7 MW, and 20 MW out is 67 per cent.

The magnetron

In the cavity magnetron the electrons circle a central cathode in crossed electric and magnetic fields and drift past a ring of resonant cavities cut into the anode (figure 13.10). The cavities’ fields bunch the electrons into rotating spokes, which deliver energy to the cavities as they pass. A magnetron is an oscillator, not an amplifier: its frequency and phase drift with current and load. That suits radar, ovens and compact medical linacs, where one magnetron drives one accelerating structure (chapter 28). It does not suit a long linac whose many sources must hold a common phase, which is why such linacs use klystrons.

Figure 13.10. A cavity magnetron end on, schematic: Randall and Boot’s eight-hole form. Each hole, joined to the central bore by a slot, is a resonator. The cathode is on the axis and the magnetic field along it; the electrons drift round the cathode and are gathered into rotating spokes, four for eight cavities in the \(\pi\) mode, which give energy to the cavities as they pass. Power leaves through a loop in one cavity.

The inductive output tube

Andrew Haeff invented the inductive output tube (IOT) at RCA in 1938 or 1939 (sources give both years). It puts a control grid, as in a valve, on the gun of a linear beam tube, so the beam leaves the gun already bunched; the bunches then drive an output cavity as in a klystron. It is a cross between a klystron and a tetrode (Eimac’s trade name was Klystrode). Because the grid, not a drift, does the bunching, the tube is short, and like a valve it draws beam current only when driven: it runs in class B or C, so its DC current follows the RF output. It works from about 0.3 to 1.5 GHz. IOTs drive television transmitters and are also used in accelerators: at 500 MHz in several light sources, and at 1.3 GHz for continuous-wave linac cavities (Brennan and Belomestnykh, in the Handbook, section 7.3.4, p. 782).

Solid-state amplifiers

Transistor amplifiers give hundreds of watts each; combining hundreds of them gives a large source with no high voltage and no single point of failure. SOLEIL, the French light source, built its 352 MHz RF from 330 W MOSFET modules: one amplifier of 35 kW (147 modules) for the booster and four of 190 kW (724 modules each) for the storage ring (Marchand and others 2007). A failed module lowers the output slightly and is replaced without stopping the machine.

Pulsed modulators

A pulsed klystron needs a high-voltage pulse with a flat top. The classic line-type modulator (figure 13.11) charges a pulse-forming network, \(n\) sections of inductance \(L_s\) and capacitance \(C_s\), to a voltage \(V_c\), then discharges it through a switch (a hydrogen thyratron) into a matched load of \(\sqrt{L_s/C_s}\). The network acts as a transmission line: it delivers a pulse of \(V_c/2\) for a time \(2n\sqrt{L_sC_s}\), the time a wave takes to travel down the line and back. A pulse transformer steps the voltage up to what the klystron gun needs. Newer modulators replace the thyratron with stacks of solid-state switches. The flatness matters because the klystron’s phase depends on its voltage: the drift time is \(\omega L_d/v\), and \(\Delta v/v = qV_0(\Delta V/V_0)/(\gamma^3\beta^2mc^2)\). For a 165 kV beam at 1 GHz (the CLIC drive-beam tube) with an assumed 1 m from input to output, the phase delay is 1834°, and a 0.1 per cent voltage error moves the output phase by 0.6°. The CLIC drive-beam specification asks for a flat top within 0.1 per cent and a pulse-to-pulse repeatability of 50 ppm, which is 0.03° on the same assumption.

Figure 13.11. The line-type modulator, schematic. The supply charges the pulse-forming network to \(V_c\) through a choke and a diode, the charging current returning through the pulse transformer’s primary. When the thyratron closes it earths the network’s input end, and the network discharges through the thyratron and the primary in series: a flat pulse of \(V_c/2\) lasting \(2n\sqrt{L_sC_s}\), stepped up by the transformer for the klystron’s gun.

Pre-requisite (continued): Sampling, discrete Fourier transform, FFT, aliasing

Pre-requisite. This continues Sampling, discrete Fourier transform, FFT, aliasing from chapter 8, which sampled a signal once per turn and showed that sampling folds high frequencies onto low ones. An RF signal at a gigahertz is too fast to digitise directly with the precision a cavity field needs, so it is first moved to a low frequency. This section shows how a mixer does that while keeping amplitude and phase, and how the samples give the in-phase and quadrature components \(I\) and \(Q\) of the field. The low-level RF section that follows uses both. Taught from Wangler, section 5.8, and the Handbook, section 7.3.1.2 (low-level RF).

Mixing. A mixer multiplies two signals. Feed it the cavity’s signal \(A\cos(\omega t + \varphi)\) and a local oscillator (LO) \(\cos\omega_{LO}t\) from the master reference. The product of two cosines is half the sum of the cosines of the sum and the difference (\(\cos A\cos B = \tfrac12[\cos(A - B) + \cos(A + B)]\), from the addition formulas of F1, section 3):

\[ A\cos(\omega t + \varphi)\cos\omega_{LO}t = \frac{A}{2}\cos\left[(\omega - \omega_{LO})t + \varphi\right] + \frac{A}{2}\cos\left[(\omega + \omega_{LO})t + \varphi\right]. \]

A low-pass filter removes the sum, leaving a signal at the intermediate frequency (IF) \(\omega_{IF} = \omega - \omega_{LO}\) that carries the original amplitude (halved) and the original phase \(\varphi\) unchanged. If the cavity’s phase moves by 1°, the IF signal’s phase moves by 1°; but 1° of a 250 kHz signal is 11 ns, not the 2.1 ps it is at 1.3 GHz, so it is far easier to measure. Note one trap: a signal at \(\omega_{LO} - \omega_{IF}\), the image frequency, mixes down to the same IF, and must be kept out by filtering before the mixer.

In-phase and quadrature. Any sinusoid at a known frequency is fixed by two numbers. Write

\[ A\cos(\omega t + \varphi) = I\cos\omega t - Q\sin\omega t, \qquad I = A\cos\varphi, \qquad Q = A\sin\varphi, \]

so \(A = \sqrt{I^2 + Q^2}\) and \(\tan\varphi = Q/I\): the pair \((I, Q)\) is the complex amplitude \(Ae^{j\varphi} = I + jQ\) of the phasor, the field vector of section 9. Mixing with both \(\cos\omega t\) and \(-\sin\omega t\) from the reference and filtering gives \(I/2\) and \(Q/2\) directly at zero frequency.

Sampling at four times the IF. A neater way samples the IF signal \(A\cos(\omega_{IF}t + \varphi)\) at exactly four times its frequency. Consecutive samples are a quarter IF period apart, 90° of phase:

\[ x_0 = A\cos\varphi = I, \quad x_1 = A\cos(\varphi + 90°) = -Q, \quad x_2 = -I, \quad x_3 = Q, \quad x_4 = I, \dots \]

Each pair of consecutive samples gives \(I\) and \(Q\) (with signs that repeat every four), so the field vector is updated at every sample. The sampling must be fast enough for the signal’s bandwidth (chapter 8): here the information is the slow change of \(I\) and \(Q\), which the cavity’s own bandwidth limits to kilohertz.

At work. At the TESLA Test Facility the 1.3 GHz probe signals were mixed to 250 kHz, so the LO was at 1299.75 MHz (and the image at 1299.5 MHz was filtered out), and sampled at 1 MHz: four samples per IF period. With the field at \(\varphi = 40°\), the four samples are 0.766, \(-0.643\), \(-0.766\), \(+0.643\) times \(A\): \(I = 0.766A\), \(Q = 0.643A\). The phase tolerance of 1.6° at 1.3 GHz is a timing of 3.4 ps; the CLIC drive beam’s 0.05° at 1 GHz is 0.14 ps.

Exercise P1. A 352.2 MHz cavity signal is to be mixed to an IF of 1 MHz with the LO below the signal. What LO frequency is needed, and which other input frequency would also appear at 1 MHz?

Answer

LO at 351.2 MHz. The image is at \(351.2 - 1 = 350.2\) MHz: a signal there mixes to \(-1\) MHz, indistinguishable from \(+1\) MHz after the mixer, so it is filtered out before.

Exercise P2. Four consecutive samples at four times the IF read 0.50, 0.50, \(-0.50\), \(-0.50\) (volts). What are the amplitude and phase?

Answer

\(I = x_0 = 0.50\), \(Q = -x_1 = -0.50\) (and \(x_2 = -I\), \(x_3 = Q\) check). \(A = \sqrt{0.5^2 + 0.5^2} = 0.707\) V and \(\varphi = \arctan(-1) = -45°\).

Pre-requisite (continued): Laplace transform, feedback and stability

Pre-requisite. This continues Laplace transform, feedback and stability from chapter 6, which closed a loop round a terminal: an error signal drives a correction, the steady error shrinks by \(1 + L_0\) for loop gain \(L_0\), and a delay in the loop limits how high the gain and bandwidth can go before the loop goes unstable. The cavity’s field is held the same way. What is new is feed-forward: correcting a disturbance that repeats, or that is measured before it arrives, by adding the correction to the drive in advance instead of waiting for an error. The low-level RF section uses both. Taught from Wangler, section 5.8, and the Handbook, section 7.3.1.2.

What feedback cannot do. A feedback loop acts on an error that already exists. Its correction is late by the loop’s delay \(\tau_d\) (cables, the amplifier, the klystron, the cavity’s own filling), and chapter 6 showed that the delay caps the loop’s crossover frequency: the delay costs a phase \(\omega\tau_d\), so with a 45° phase margin the crossover can be at most about \(1/(8\tau_d)\). Wangler puts the practical limit for cavity field loops at about 10 kHz. A disturbance faster than that, or one that arrives as a step, gets through before the loop can answer.

Feed-forward. Many disturbances of a pulsed linac are the same on every pulse: the field droops when the beam is switched on, the Lorentz force detunes the cavity in a pattern set by the pulse shape (section 7), the klystron’s phase drifts along the flat top as the modulator voltage sags. A table of corrections, one value of \(I\) and \(Q\) for each time step of the pulse, can be added to the drive so that the correction is already there when the disturbance comes. The table is learnt: after pulse \(k\), the controller records the residual error \(e_k(t)\) and updates

\[ u_{k+1}(t) = u_k(t) + g\,G^{-1}e_k(t), \]

where \(u\) is the feed-forward table, \(G^{-1}\) turns an error in the field into the drive change that would cancel it (a model of the cavity’s response, including its delay), and \(g\) is a gain between 0 and 1. If the model is right, each pulse removes the fraction \(g\) of the repetitive error and leaves \((1 - g)\) of it, so after \(k\) pulses \((1 - g)^k\) remains: with \(g = 0.5\), ten pulses reduce a repetitive error a thousandfold. A gain below one makes the learning robust to errors in the model and to noise that does not repeat. Feedback then deals with what does not repeat. A second kind of feed-forward needs no learning: when a beam current is measured upstream before it reaches the cavity, the drive can be raised in step with it (Wangler notes this works when the signal can outrun the beam, as with slow ions).

At work. Section 7’s TESLA cavity is filled for 531 µs, then the beam takes exactly the power that would have raised the field further. If the beam arrives 10 µs late, the field has risen on with slope \(V/\tau = V/767\) µs for those 10 µs, an error of 1.3 per cent, thirteen times the tolerance of \(10^{-3}\) and too fast for the loop to catch at once. The timing of beam and RF, and a feed-forward table that matches the measured beam pattern, keep it inside.

Exercise Q1. An iterative feed-forward with \(g = 0.3\) starts from a repetitive error of 2 per cent. How many pulses until it is below \(2\times10^{-5}\)?

Answer

A factor of 1000: \((0.7)^k < 10^{-3}\) gives \(k > \ln10^{-3}/\ln0.7 = 19.4\), so 20 pulses. At 10 pulses a second, two seconds.

Exercise Q2. Why does the slope of the field at the end of filling equal \(\hat{V}/\tau\)?

Answer

Without beam the field rises as \(V(t) = 2\hat{V}(1 - e^{-t/\tau})\) towards twice the working value (section 7). Its slope is \((2\hat{V}/\tau)e^{-t/\tau}\), and at the moment it reaches \(\hat{V}\), \(e^{-t/\tau} = \tfrac12\), so the slope is \(\hat{V}/\tau\).

Low-level RF

The low-level RF system measures the cavity field and corrects the drive. A probe samples the field; the signal is mixed down to an intermediate frequency and digitised; the in-phase (\(I\)) and quadrature (\(Q\)) parts give the complex field vector; a controller compares it with the set point and drives an \(I/Q\) vector modulator at the klystron’s input (figure 13.12). Controlling \(I\) and \(Q\) rather than amplitude and phase decouples the two loops and avoids the ill-defined phase at small amplitude. At the TESLA Test Facility one klystron fed up to 32 cavities, so only their vector sum could be controlled; the signals were mixed to 250 kHz and sampled at 1 MHz, and a feedback loop with adaptive feed-forward tables removed the repetitive part of the errors (Aune and others 2000). The goal there was an energy spread of \(2\times10^{-3}\), which needs gradient stability of \(10^{-3}\) and phase stability of 1.6°. The CLIC drive beam asks for ±0.2 per cent and ±0.05°.

Figure 13.12. The low-level RF loop, block diagram. A probe samples the cavity’s field; a mixer and an analog-to-digital converter turn it into the complex field vector \((I, Q)\); the controller compares it with the set point and, with feed-forward tables for the errors that repeat from pulse to pulse, drives the vector modulator at the klystron’s input.

Why phase matters depends on where the beam sits. The energy gain goes as \(\sin\phi\). On crest, \(\phi = 90°\), a phase error \(\Delta\phi\) gives \(\sin(90° + \Delta\phi) = \cos\Delta\phi\), so the energy falls by a fraction \(1 - \cos\Delta\phi \approx \tfrac12(\Delta\phi)^2\) (with \(\Delta\phi\) in radians): \(1.5\times10^{-4}\) for 1°, small because the crest is flat. At \(\phi = 70^\circ\) (20° off crest), the curve has a slope, and the fractional change is \(\Delta(\sin\phi)/\sin\phi = \cos\phi\,\Delta\phi/\sin\phi = \cot\phi\,\Delta\phi\): \(6.4\times10^{-4}\) for 0.1°.

10. Breakdown, pulsed heating and multipacting

Breakdown and the Kilpatrick criterion

At high surface field, a copper surface emits electrons from microscopic tips (Fowler-Nordheim field emission, chapter 4). The current heats the tip, which vaporises; the vapour ionises; an arc forms, shorts the cavity and reflects the incoming power. A breakdown leaves a crater and a burst of gas. W. D. Kilpatrick fitted the 1950s data on the onset of sparking in RF gaps and published a criterion in 1957. The form now used (as given by Gerigk 2011, and by Wangler 2008, section 5.11, p. 163, who credits the formula to a 1982 Los Alamos note by T. J. Boyd, as chapter 4 does) is

\[ f = 1.64\,\mathcal{E}_K^2\,e^{-8.5/\mathcal{E}_K} \qquad (f \text{ in MHz}, \mathcal{E}_K \text{ in MV/m}). \]

It gives \(\mathcal{E}_K = 5.4\) MV/m at 10 MHz, 14.8 MV/m at 201.25 MHz, 18.5 MV/m at 352.2 MHz, 32.1 MV/m at 1.3 GHz, 45.8 MV/m at 2856 MHz and 89.7 MV/m at 12 GHz. At high frequency the exponential tends to one and \(\mathcal{E}_K \approx \sqrt{f/1.64}\): the allowed field grows as the square root of frequency. Designs today quote a “bravery factor” \(b = \mathcal{E}_s/\mathcal{E}_K\) of 1 to 2 for normal-conducting cavities (Gerigk 2011; Wangler 2008, section 5.11, p. 164); CERN’s Linac4 RFQ at 352 MHz was designed for 34 MV/m, 1.84 Kilpatrick. Above about 1 GHz and for very short pulses the criterion has largely lost its importance, because other phenomena govern the maximum field (Gerigk 2011); in practice the limit is a breakdown rate one accepts, at a given pulse length. CLIC’s accelerating structures at 12 GHz are designed for 72 MV/m in the first energy stage and 100 MV/m in the last, and prototypes have exceeded 100 MV/m at very low breakdown rates (CLIC 2018; Degiovanni and others 2016).

Pre-requisite (continued): Diffusion and Fokker-Planck

Pre-requisite. This continues Diffusion and Fokker-Planck from chapter 3, which derived Fick’s law, a flux proportional to minus the gradient, for molecules wandering in a pipe, and showed that a random walk spreads as the square root of time. Heat in a metal spreads the same way. This section derives the heat diffusion equation and the thermal diffusivity \(\kappa = k_{th}/\rho_mc_p\); solves it for heat released at an instant on the surface of a thick wall (a Gaussian); and adds such releases to get the surface temperature under a steady flux switched on for a pulse. The pulsed-heating part of section 10 uses all three. Taught from Tong, Fluid Mechanics, sections 7.1 (the heat equation) and 7.1.4 (diffusion on the line).

The heat equation. Heat flows from hot to cold, at a rate proportional to the temperature gradient (Fourier’s law, the twin of Fick’s):

\[ \mathbf{q} = -k_{th}\nabla T \quad (\text{W/m}^2), \]

with \(k_{th}\) the thermal conductivity (W m⁻¹ K⁻¹). Energy is conserved: the heat stored per unit volume, \(\rho_mc_pT\) (with \(\rho_m\) the density in kg/m³ and \(c_p\) the specific heat in J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹), rises at the rate at which heat flows in, \(-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{q}\). Together,

\[ \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \kappa\nabla^2T, \qquad \kappa = \frac{k_{th}}{\rho_mc_p} \quad (\text{m}^2/\text{s}). \]

For copper, \(\kappa = 401/(8935\times385) = 1.17\times10^{-4}\) m²/s. In one dimension, depth \(z\) into a wall, it reads \(\partial T/\partial t = \kappa\,\partial^2T/\partial z^2\).

An instantaneous release: the Gaussian. Try

\[ T(z, t) = \frac{A}{\sqrt{t}}\,e^{-z^2/4\kappa t} . \]

Differentiating, \(\partial T/\partial t = T\left[-1/(2t) + z^2/(4\kappa t^2)\right]\) and \(\kappa\,\partial^2T/\partial z^2 = \kappa T\left[-1/(2\kappa t) + z^2/(4\kappa^2t^2)\right]\), the same: it is a solution. At small \(t\) it is a narrow spike, at later times a wider, lower bump of width \(\sqrt{2\kappa t}\) (its rms), the random walk’s square-root spread. Now put a pulse of heat \(Q_h\) (J/m²) onto the surface \(z = 0\) of a thick wall at \(t = 0\), with no heat leaving through the surface. The slope \(\partial T/\partial z\) of the Gaussian vanishes at \(z = 0\), so it already obeys “no flux out”; only its height is to be fixed, by requiring all the heat to be in the wall: \(\rho_mc_p\int_0^\infty T\,dz = Q_h\). With \(\int_0^\infty e^{-z^2/4\kappa t}dz = \sqrt{\pi\kappa t}\) (half of chapter 2’s Gaussian integral),

\[ T(z, t) = \frac{Q_h}{\rho_mc_p\sqrt{\pi\kappa t}}\,e^{-z^2/4\kappa t}, \qquad T(0, t) = \frac{Q_h}{\rho_mc_p\sqrt{\pi\kappa t}} . \]

The surface cools as \(1/\sqrt{t}\) while the heat soaks in. Heat has spread a diffusion length \(\sqrt{\kappa t}\) by time \(t\): 13 µm in copper after 1.5 µs, 5.3 µm after 244 ns, 0.34 mm after a millisecond, 11 mm after a second.

A steady flux for a pulse. Let the surface absorb a constant flux \(F_h\) (W/m²) from \(t = 0\) to \(t_p\). The equation is linear, so the temperature is the sum of the responses to the many small releases \(F_h\,dt'\) made at each earlier time \(t'\), each having cooled for \(t_p - t'\):

\[ \Delta T(t_p) = \int_0^{t_p}\frac{F_h\,dt'}{\rho_mc_p\sqrt{\pi\kappa(t_p - t')}} = \frac{F_h}{\rho_mc_p\sqrt{\pi\kappa}}\cdot2\sqrt{t_p} = \frac{2F_h\sqrt{t_p}}{\sqrt{\pi\rho_mc_pk_{th}}} , \]

using \(\rho_mc_p\sqrt{\kappa} = \sqrt{\rho_mc_pk_{th}}\). The rise grows as the square root of the pulse length: the heat has reached a depth of order \(\sqrt{\kappa t_p}\) and is stored in that thin layer. For a given energy per pulse, \(F_ht_p\) fixed, a shorter pulse heats more, as \(1/\sqrt{t_p}\).

At work. The 2856 MHz copper cell at 1 MV has \(F_h = 1.18\times10^7\) W/m² at its cylindrical wall (the Poynting section before section 3). A 3.5 µs pulse raises the surface by \(2\times1.18\times10^7\times\sqrt{3.5\times10^{-6}}/\sqrt{\pi\times8935\times385\times401} = 0.67\) K, section 10’s figure. Run continuously, the same flux would have to cross the wall in steady state: through 10 mm of copper that needs \(F_hd/k_{th} = 295\) K of temperature difference. Pulsing at a low duty factor (the fraction of the time the RF is on: microsecond pulses a few hundred times a second are on for about a thousandth of the time) is what makes such fields possible in copper.

Exercise R1. What surface flux heats copper by 82 K in a 1.5 µs pulse, and what surface magnetic field gives that flux at 11.424 GHz (\(R_s = 27.9\) mΩ)?

Answer

\(F_h = \Delta T\sqrt{\pi\rho_mc_pk_{th}}/(2\sqrt{t_p}) = 82\times\sqrt{\pi\times8935\times385\times401}/(2\sqrt{1.5\times10^{-6}}) = 2.2\times10^9\) W/m². With \(F_h = \tfrac12R_sH^2\), \(H = \sqrt{2F_h/R_s} = 3.97\times10^5\) A/m, or 499 mT: section 10’s Pritzkau and Siemann threshold.

Exercise R2. A structure’s pulse is shortened from 1.5 µs to 244 ns at the same surface field. By what factor does the pulsed temperature rise change, and how deep does the heat reach?

Answer

\(\Delta T \propto \sqrt{t_p}\) at fixed flux: \(\sqrt{0.244/1.5} = 0.40\), so 60 per cent less. The diffusion length falls from 13 µm to 5.3 µm.

Pulsed heating

In a short pulse the heat has no time to spread. The surface of a copper wall heated by a flux \(F_h = \tfrac12R_s|H_t|^2\) (W/m²) behaves as a half-space with a constant flux. An instantaneous release \(Q_h\) (J/m²) at the surface spreads as a Gaussian, leaving the surface at a temperature rise \(Q_h/(\rho_m c_p\sqrt{\pi\kappa t})\) after a time \(t\), with \(\rho_m\) the density, \(c_p\) the specific heat and \(\kappa = k_{th}/\rho_mc_p\) the diffusivity (\(k_{th}\) the thermal conductivity). Summing releases over a pulse of length \(t_p\) with a constant flux,

\[ \Delta T = \int_0^{t_p}\frac{F_h\,dt'}{\rho_mc_p\sqrt{\pi\kappa(t_p - t')}} = \frac{2F_h\sqrt{t_p}}{\sqrt{\pi\rho_mc_pk_{th}}} = \frac{R_s|H_t|^2\sqrt{t_p}}{\sqrt{\pi\rho_mc_pk_{th}}} . \]

The surface expands, is held by the cold metal underneath, and is stressed every pulse. Pritzkau and Siemann (2002) cycled oxygen-free copper in an 11.424 GHz cavity with 1.5 µs pulses: 56 million pulses at a rise of 120 K and 86 million at 82 K both cracked the grain boundaries, and a rise of 250 K for several million pulses destroyed the surface. With copper’s constants (\(\rho_m = 8935\) kg/m³, \(c_p = 385\) J/(kg K), \(k_{th} = 401\) W/(m K)), 82 K in 1.5 µs at that frequency needs a surface field of 397 kA/m (499 mT). Heat diffuses only 13 µm in 1.5 µs. Designers of high-gradient copper structures keep the pulsed temperature rise well below the damage seen there. For the 2856 MHz cell at 1 MV, a 3.5 µs pulse heats the cylindrical wall by 0.67 K.

Multipacting

Multipacting is a resonant electron avalanche. An electron leaves a surface, is driven by the RF field, and strikes a surface again at a moment when the field is ready to launch the secondaries it knocks out. If the secondary emission yield exceeds one at the impact energy, the number grows each cycle until space charge or the RF power it absorbs limits it. The cavity cannot be pushed through that field level until the surface is cleaned. Camille Gutton saw the effect at Nancy in 1924; Philo Farnsworth studied it in 1934 and tried to use it as an amplifier.

Two-surface condition. Take parallel plates a distance \(d\) apart with voltage \(V\sin(\omega t + \phi_e)\) between them, an electron leaving one plate at rest at \(t = 0\), and the field pushing it across. Integrating \(m\ddot{x} = (eV/d)\sin(\omega t + \phi_e)\) twice,

\[ x(t) = \frac{eV}{md\,\omega^2}\left[\omega t\cos\phi_e - \sin(\omega t + \phi_e) + \sin\phi_e\right]. \]

For the secondaries to start in step, the electron must arrive after an odd number \(N\) of half periods, \(\omega t = N\pi\), so \(\sin(\omega t + \phi_e) = -\sin\phi_e\) and

\[ V = \frac{m\omega^2d^2}{e\,(N\pi\cos\phi_e + 2\sin\phi_e)}, \qquad W_{imp} = \frac{2e^2V^2\cos^2\phi_e}{md^2\omega^2} . \]

For \(\phi_e = 0\), \(V = m\omega^2d^2/(N\pi e)\) and \(W_{imp} = 2m(\omega d)^2/(N\pi)^2\). A 5 mm gap at 1.3 GHz resonates at 3.0 kV with 1.9 keV impacts for \(N = 1\), and at 1.0 kV with 214 eV impacts for \(N = 3\). Niobium as prepared for cavities has a secondary yield of 1.4 to 1.6, peaking near 200 eV (Padamsee and Joshi 1978, in Padamsee 2020), so the higher orders are the dangerous ones. Narrow gaps, such as those between the conductors of a coaxial coupler, can meet this condition. In elliptical cavities a weak two-point barrier remains near the equator, with impact energies of 50 to 100 eV; XFEL and LCLS-II cavities show it at about 17 to 21 MV/m, and it processes away easily (Padamsee 2020).

One-surface multipacting. In a cavity, an electron leaving the wall where the magnetic field is strong curls back to the wall in about one cyclotron period. If that is an integer number \(n\) of RF periods, it resonates: \(eB/m = \omega/n\), or \(B = m\omega/(en)\), which is 46.4 mT divided by \(n\) at 1.3 GHz. The flat outer wall of a pillbox-like cell is the worst place. This limited the early superconducting cavities and was cured by shape (How it was researched and built).

Pre-requisite (continued): Superconductivity basics

Pre-requisite. This continues Superconductivity basics from chapter 12, which gave zero resistance and the persistent current, the critical temperature, field and current density, and the type II superconductors whose field enters as fluxoids, for magnets carrying large direct currents with no loss. A cavity asks more of superconductivity, because its currents alternate. This section gives the critical temperature, the Cooper pairs and the energy gap \(\Delta = 1.76k_BT_c\); why the number of unpaired electrons falls as \(e^{-\Delta/k_BT}\); the London penetration depth and the two-fluid picture that explains a small but non-zero RF loss rising as \(\omega^2\); the expulsion of magnetic field and the critical and superheating fields that limit it; and how magnetic flux trapped during cool-down adds a loss. Section 11 uses all of them. Taught from Padamsee, Knobloch and Hays, sections 3.3, 3.4, 4.5, 4.6, 5.2 to 5.5 and 9.3, and Tong, Electromagnetism, section 6.5.2 (an invitation to superconductivity).

Zero resistance and the Meissner effect. Below a critical temperature \(T_c\) some metals lose all DC resistance: a current set up in a ring persists with no measurable decay (the resistivity is below \(10^{-12}\) of the normal value, Padamsee, Knobloch and Hays report). Niobium has the highest \(T_c\) of the elements, 9.2 K, and is pure and workable enough to form into cavities; lead (7.2 K) was used before it. A superconductor does more than conduct perfectly. Cooled in a magnetic field, it expels the field from its interior (the Meissner effect), which a merely perfect conductor would not do: Faraday’s law would only freeze in whatever field was there. The field is excluded except from a thin surface layer, as the next paragraphs show.

Cooper pairs and the gap. In the theory of Bardeen, Cooper and Schrieffer (BCS, 1957), an electron passing through the lattice draws the positive ions slightly towards it; the ions are slow to relax, and the slight excess of positive charge left behind attracts a second electron. Through this lattice-mediated attraction, electrons near the Fermi level bind in pairs of opposite momentum and spin, Cooper pairs, all in one collective quantum state. Breaking a pair costs at least \(2\Delta\), where \(\Delta\) is the energy gap; at temperatures well below \(T_c\) BCS theory gives

\[ \Delta = 1.76\,k_BT_c , \]

1.40 meV for niobium (\(\Delta/k_B = 16.2\) K; measurements give a slightly larger ratio, about 1.9, for niobium). An RF photon at 1.3 GHz has \(h\nu = 5.4\) µeV (F7, section 4), five hundred times too little to break a pair (that needs 675 GHz): RF does not destroy superconductivity directly. Pairs carry a direct current without loss because scattering one would need either an impurity to supply energy, which it cannot, or a lattice vibration of energy above \(2\Delta\), rare when \(k_BT \ll \Delta\).

Unpaired electrons and the Boltzmann factor. At a temperature \(T > 0\) some pairs are broken by thermal agitation, leaving unpaired electrons (quasiparticles) that behave like normal electrons. Their number goes as the Boltzmann factor (F6, section 13) for an excitation of energy \(\Delta\) above the ground state:

\[ n_n \propto e^{-\Delta/k_BT} . \]

For niobium, \(e^{-\Delta/k_BT}\) is 0.021 at 4.2 K, \(3.0\times10^{-4}\) at 2 K and \(1.2\times10^{-4}\) at 1.8 K: cooling from 4.2 to 2 K removes 98.6 per cent of the unpaired electrons. These carry the RF loss.

The London penetration depth. The pairs are not scattered, so in a field they simply accelerate: \(m_e\,d\mathbf{v}_s/dt = -e\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\), and with the supercurrent density \(\mathbf{J}_s = -n_se\mathbf{v}_s\) (\(n_s\) the density of paired electrons, counted singly),

\[ \frac{\partial\mathbf{J}_s}{\partial t} = \frac{n_se^2}{m_e}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} \qquad \text{(the first London equation).} \]

Take the curl, use Faraday’s law, and add London’s second assumption, which encodes the Meissner effect: not just \(\partial/\partial t\) of the result but the result itself vanishes, \(\nabla\times\mathbf{J}_s = -(n_se^2/m_e)\mathbf{B}\). With Ampère’s law, \(\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{J}_s\), and the vector identity of the Maxwell section, this gives \(\nabla^2\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{B}/\lambda_L^2\), with

\[ \lambda_L = \sqrt{\frac{m_e}{\mu_0n_se^2}} . \]

In a superconductor filling \(z > 0\), a field parallel to the surface falls as \(e^{-z/\lambda_L}\), carried by supercurrents in a layer of thickness \(\lambda_L\). Padamsee, Knobloch and Hays list 36 nm for niobium, which corresponds to \(n_s = 2.2\times10^{28}\) m⁻³. Unlike the skin depth of section 3, \(\lambda_L\) does not depend on frequency.

The two-fluid picture of RF loss. Pairs have inertia. To swing a supercurrent back and forth, an electric field is needed inside the layer, and the first London equation gives it: in phasor form \(j\omega\mathbf{J}_s = (n_se^2/m_e)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\). Equivalently, by Faraday’s law, the magnetic flux \(\mu_0H\lambda_L\) per unit length inside the layer changes at \(\omega\) and induces \(|\mathcal{E}| = \omega\mu_0\lambda_L|H|\). The superconductor’s surface impedance is therefore mainly a reactance, \(X_s = \omega\mu_0\lambda_L\) (0.37 mΩ at 1.3 GHz), which stores energy but dissipates none. The same field, however, also drives the unpaired electrons, a second “fluid” with an ordinary conductivity \(\sigma_n\) proportional to their number. They dissipate \(\tfrac12\sigma_n|\mathcal{E}|^2\) per unit volume. Integrating over the layer, where \(|\mathcal{E}|^2\) falls as \(e^{-2z/\lambda_L}\) (integral \(\lambda_L/2\)),

\[ \frac{dP}{dA} = \frac12\sigma_n(\omega\mu_0\lambda_L|H|)^2\frac{\lambda_L}{2} \equiv \frac12R_s|H|^2, \qquad R_s = \frac12\sigma_n\omega^2\mu_0^2\lambda_L^3 . \]

Two features of section 11 follow: \(R_s \propto \omega^2\), because both the inducing field and the induced current grow with \(\omega\); and \(R_s \propto \sigma_n \propto e^{-\Delta/k_BT}\). The full BCS calculation adds a factor \(1/T\) and other slowly varying terms. A curious consequence (Padamsee, Knobloch and Hays, section 4.6): a larger normal-state conductivity means more loss, so very pure niobium is not best for \(R_s\), though it is best for heat conduction.

At work. With \(\Delta/k_B = 16.2\) K, the ratio \(R_{BCS}(4.2\ \text{K})/R_{BCS}(2\ \text{K}) = (2/4.2)\,e^{16.2(1/2 - 1/4.2)} = 33\); with section 11’s fitted 17.67 K it is 49, the factor of about 50 quoted there. Going from 1.3 to 2.6 GHz at fixed temperature would quadruple \(R_{BCS}\), which is why superconducting cavities above about 3 GHz lose their advantage at 2 K.

Critical fields. Expelling a field costs energy: the field’s energy density \(\mu_0H^2/2\) is pushed out of the volume, and that is paid from the energy the electrons gained by pairing. When \(\mu_0H^2/2\) reaches the condensation energy per unit volume, the normal state becomes cheaper: that field is the thermodynamic critical field \(H_c\), about 200 mT (\(\mu_0H_c\)) for niobium at zero temperature, falling with temperature roughly as \(1 - (T/T_c)^2\), so only 5 per cent lower at 2 K. Niobium is a type II superconductor: above a lower critical field \(H_{c1}\) (about 170 mT) it becomes cheaper to let the field in as a lattice of thin tubes, vortices, each carrying one flux quantum \(\Phi_0 = h/2e = 2.07\times10^{-15}\) Wb around a normal core, until at the upper critical field \(H_{c2}\) (about 240 mT for cavity niobium) the whole metal goes normal. In an RF field, vortices need time and a starting point to enter. A clean surface can stay in the Meissner state, metastably, above \(H_{c1}\) up to the superheating field \(H_{sh}\), about 200 to 240 mT for niobium (section 11). Above it, field enters, vortices moving in an RF field dissipate heavily, and the cavity quenches. That is the hard limit of section 11.

Trapped flux. If a cavity is cooled through \(T_c\) in a small static field \(B_{ext}\), such as the Earth’s, impurities and defects pin some of the vortices before the Meissner effect can push them out. Each pinned vortex has a normal core of radius about \(\xi\) (the coherence length), and in the RF field that core behaves as normal metal with surface resistance \(R_n\). The number of vortices per unit area is \(B_{ext}/\Phi_0\), so the normal fraction of the surface is \(\pi\xi^2B_{ext}/\Phi_0\). Since \(\mu_0H_{c2} = \Phi_0/2\pi\xi^2\) (the field at which the cores would touch), this is

\[ R_{fl} = \frac{B_{ext}}{2\mu_0H_{c2}}R_n . \]

For niobium at 1.3 GHz, \(R_n \approx 1.7\) mΩ (Padamsee, Knobloch and Hays give 1.5 mΩ at 1 GHz for RRR 300, and it grows as \(\sqrt{f}\)) and \(\mu_0H_{c2} = 0.24\) T, so each microtesla trapped adds 3.6 nΩ. That matches the 3.5 nΩ per µT that section 11 quotes from Aune and others. The 40 µT of an unshielded test pit gives 140 nΩ, thirteen times the BCS loss at 2 K.

11. Superconducting cavities

Why superconductors have losses at all. In a superconductor at temperature \(T\) below the critical temperature \(T_c\), most conduction electrons are bound in Cooper pairs, which carry a direct current without loss. But an RF field must accelerate the pairs back and forth, and that takes an electric field inside the thin layer the magnetic field penetrates (of the order of the London penetration depth). That field also drives the few electrons thermally excited across the energy gap \(\Delta\), whose number goes as \(e^{-\Delta/k_BT}\). They dissipate. In the two-fluid picture the result is (Aune and others 2000, from BCS theory)

\[ R_{BCS} \propto \frac{\omega^2}{T}\exp\left(-\frac{1.76\,T_c}{T}\right) \qquad (T < T_c/2), \]

where \(1.76k_BT_c\) is the BCS gap. A widely used fit for niobium (quoted in the Wikipedia article on superconducting RF; here \(T\) in kelvin, \(f\) in Hz) is \(R_{BCS} \approx 2\times10^{-4}\,(1/T)\,(f/1.5\times10^9)^2\exp(-17.67/T)\) Ω. At 1.3 GHz it gives 533 nΩ at 4.2 K, 10.9 nΩ at 2 K and 4.6 nΩ at 1.8 K. Aune and others read about 800 nΩ at 4.2 K and 15 nΩ at 2 K off their measurements; the fit is a guide, not a law. Cooling from 4.2 K to 2 K lowers \(R_{BCS}\) by a factor of about 50 in the fit. That is why 1.3 GHz cavities run at 1.8 to 2 K in superfluid helium, whose high heat conductivity also carries the heat away.

Residual resistance. Added to \(R_{BCS}\) is a temperature-independent \(R_{res}\) from impurities, surface oxides, hydrides and trapped magnetic flux. It is a few nΩ for good niobium. Trapped flux costs 3.5 nΩ per µT at 1.3 GHz (Aune and others 2000): the 40 µT in the DESY test pit would give 140 nΩ and limit \(Q_0\) to \(1.9\times10^9\), so cavities are shielded to 1 to 2 µT (Reschke and others 2017).

\(Q_0\) and the geometry factor. For a given shape, \(Q_0 = G/R_s\), where \(G = \omega\mu_0\int|H|^2dV/\int|H|^2dA\) (Ω) depends only on the shape; \(G = 270\ \Omega\) for the TESLA cell. With \(R_s = 10.9 + 3\) nΩ at 2 K, \(Q_0 = 1.9\times10^{10}\). The same shape in copper (\(R_s = 9.4\) mΩ) has \(Q_0 = 2.9\times10^4\): six orders of magnitude.

Worked number: the European XFEL. Its 1.3 GHz TESLA cavities are designed to operate at an average of 23.6 MV/m with \(Q_0 \geq 10^{10}\) (Reschke and others 2017). With \(\hat{V} = 23.6\ \text{MV/m} \times 1.038\ \text{m} = 24.5\) MV and \(R/Q = 1036\ \Omega\), the wall loss is

\[ P = \frac{\hat{V}^2}{(R/Q)\,Q_0} = \frac{(24.5\times10^6)^2}{1036\times10^{10}} = 58\ \text{W} \]

if the RF were continuous; the XFEL is pulsed, so the average is lower. But that heat is removed at 2 K. A perfect refrigerator lifting heat from 2 K to 300 K needs \((300 - 2)/2 = 149\) W of work per watt (Carnot’s limit, \(W/Q = (T_{hot} - T_{cold})/T_{cold}\), from the second law: F6, section 16); real plants manage a fraction of that: CEBAF’s 2 K plant takes about 1000 W per watt (Rode and Ganni, in the Handbook, section 5.9.1). Even so, the same voltage from a copper cavity of that shape would dissipate 20 MW continuously. This is why continuous-wave and long-pulse linacs are superconducting.

The field limit. A superconductor expels magnetic field only up to a critical field. In RF the relevant one is the superheating field, up to which a metastable field-free surface can survive: about 200 to 240 mT for niobium (Aune and others 2000; Padamsee 2020 uses 220 mT; Padamsee 2009, section 3.6.1, p. 80, gives about 240 mT at zero temperature and 220 mT at 2 K). The RF magnetic field at the wall must stay below it. The TESLA shape has \(B_{pk}/\mathcal{E}_{acc} = 4.26\) mT per MV/m and \(\mathcal{E}_{pk}/\mathcal{E}_{acc} = 2.0\), where \(\mathcal{E}_{acc} = \hat{V}/L\). So 200 mT corresponds to 46.9 MV/m and 240 mT to 56.3 MV/m. Shapes with a larger equator lower \(B_{pk}/\mathcal{E}_{acc}\) by 10 to 15 per cent at the cost of a higher \(\mathcal{E}_{pk}\); a single-cell re-entrant cavity at Cornell reached 59 MV/m (Padamsee 2020). That is near the end for niobium.

What stops cavities before the critical field. Each limit has its signature in the curve of \(Q_0\) against \(\mathcal{E}_{acc}\) (figure 13.13; Padamsee 2020):

  • Multipacting (section 10): \(Q_0\) drops at a fixed field level and recovers after “processing” as the surface is cleaned by the electrons themselves.
  • Quench: a sub-millimetre defect with high loss heats until the niobium around it goes normal; the normal region spreads, the stored energy goes into the helium, and the field collapses. Purer niobium conducts heat better and lets a defect survive higher fields. A rule of thumb for niobium’s conductivity at 4.2 K is \(0.25 \times \text{RRR}\) W/(m K), with RRR the residual resistivity ratio, the resistance at room temperature over that just above \(T_c\) (Aune and others 2000).
  • Field emission: dust particles and tips on the surface emit electrons, which are accelerated, strike the wall, heat it and make X-rays. \(Q_0\) falls steeply and X-ray detectors respond. The cures are cleanliness: assembly in class 10 to 100 clean rooms (no more than 10 to 100 dust particles larger than 0.5 µm per cubic foot of air) and high-pressure rinsing with ultrapure water at about 100 bar.
  • High-field Q-slope: without any X-rays, \(Q_0\) falls sharply above 20 to 25 MV/m. The cure, found empirically, is electropolishing (removing the surface layer electrochemically, in an acid bath with the cavity as the anode, which leaves it smoother than chemical etching) followed by baking at 120 °C for about two days. Hydrogen in the surface, forming niobium hydrides that go normal in the field, is the leading explanation.

Bottom: the curve of \(Q_0\) against gradient, drawn schematically, not measured, with the signature of each limit: a multipacting dip that processes away, the high-field Q-slope, the steep fall of field emission with its X-rays, and the quench where the curve ends.

Nitrogen doping. In 2013 A. Grassellino and colleagues at Fermilab reported that annealing a cavity at 800 °C in a partial pressure of nitrogen, then electropolishing a few micrometres, lowers \(R_s\) below what was thought to be niobium’s limit and reverses the field dependence: \(Q_0\) rises with field at medium gradients. One cavity held about \(3\times10^{10}\) at 17 MV/m and 2 K, and the efficiency, set by the cryogenic load, improved by up to a factor of three. The method was developed for continuous-wave linacs at 15 to 20 MV/m, where the wall loss, not the gradient, sets the cost (Padamsee 2020; the Handbook, section 5.8, p. 532, describes the 800 °C recipe and its cryogenic benefit for continuous-wave operation).

How it was researched and built

The klystron at Stanford, 1937 to 1940

The Varians’ first tube, built by Sigurd to Russell’s design, worked in August 1937; the name klystron was suggested by the Stanford classicist Hermann Fränkel, from a Greek verb for waves washing over a shore. In 1938 Sperry Gyroscope took the work on contract. The 1939 paper in the Journal of Applied Physics described a two-cavity oscillator and amplifier, citing Hansen’s analysis of the resonators; Webster’s theory of the bunching appeared in the same volume. Klystron equipment was used in successful blind-landing tests of aircraft in Boston in 1939, and the Varians moved east to Sperry in 1940. A Westinghouse klystron of 1940 is usually described as the first commercial one.

Hansen returned to Stanford in 1945 and turned to electron linacs fed by microwaves. His group first accelerated electrons in a travelling-wave linac, the Mark I, in 1947; lengthened to 12 feet, it reached 6 MeV with 0.9 MW (Neal 1968). The Mark I was powered by a magnetron, which left much to be desired as a source, and the group then needed far more power than wartime tubes gave (Livingston and Blewett 1962, section 10-9, p. 320); the Stanford group developed pulsed klystrons of high power for its next machines (chapter 14). Hansen died in 1949, aged 39. The two-mile SLAC linac, built from 1962 and running from 1966, was a row of klystrons at 2856 MHz in a gallery above the beamline, feeding copper structures below.

The magnetron at Birmingham, 1940

Randall and Boot’s magnetron was a block of copper with resonant holes drilled around a central bore, each joined to the bore by a slot: resonators like Hansen’s, arranged in a ring around the electron cloud. It worked by late February 1940 at about 400 W, and within a week at about 1 kW. The General Electric Company (GEC) engineered it into sealed tubes; the version taken to the United States on the Tizard Mission in September 1940, GEC’s eight-hole No. 12, gave 10 kW. Boot and Randall told the story in 1976.

From Stanford to CEBAF and TESLA: superconducting cavities

At Stanford’s High Energy Physics Laboratory (HEPL), Fairbank, Wilson and Schwettman began work on superconducting cavities in the early 1960s, aiming at a continuous-wave electron linac of 2 GeV at 14 MV/m with \(Q_0\) of several \(10^9\), to keep the refrigeration below 10 kW at 2 K. HEPL and other laboratories also pursued lead-plated resonators. Around 1970 Turneaure and Viet made 8.6 GHz TM\(_{010}\) cavities from solid niobium, machined in halves and joined by an electron-beam weld, fired in an ultra-high-vacuum furnace at 1750 to 2100 °C. They reached a peak magnetic field of 108 mT and a peak electric field of 70 MV/m at \(Q_0 = 8\times10^9\), and niobium became the material.

Moving to 1.3 GHz for the linac went badly. By the end of 1973 HEPL had built and installed several 55-cell structures, but they gave only 2.0 to 3.8 MV/m: the limit was one-surface multipacting, whose field levels scale with frequency. Parts of the Superconducting Accelerator (SCA) were installed by 1977: four 5.65 m sections and one recirculation, for a beam energy of 84 MeV, with the structures at 1.5 to 2.5 MV/m. At Cornell, an 11-cell niobium muffin-tin cavity at 2.86 GHz accelerated a beam in the Cornell Electron Synchrotron in 1974 at up to 4 MV/m with \(Q_0 = 1.1\times10^9\), the first use of a superconducting cavity in a high-energy physics machine (Padamsee, Knobloch and Hays 1998, section 1.5.1, date the run 1975, with a beam of 4 GeV and \(Q_0 = 10^9\)). Their account has HEPL’s and Illinois’s 1.3 GHz structures held to about 2 MV/m by multipacting, and the SCA completed in 1977 at 50 MV in 27 m.

The cure for multipacting was geometry. Proch and Klein found that in a spherical cell, electrons drift to the equator, where the field is too weak to make them multiply. CERN built a 500 MHz spherical cell that reached 3 MV/m at 4.2 K by the end of 1981 with no multipacting; the same year Kneisel and Halbritter showed a 3 GHz elliptical cell, mechanically stiffer and easier to rinse, free of it. KEK installed sixteen five-cell 508 MHz cavities in TRISTAN by the summer of 1988 and sixteen more in August 1989, the first large-scale superconducting RF system, providing more than 240 MV (Padamsee 2009, table 11.1, lists the 32 cavities as providing 200 MV); HEPL’s SCA had held the previous record of 50 MV. Cornell’s five-cell 1.5 GHz elliptical cavity, tested in the CESR storage ring in 1984 at 6.5 MV/m, was chosen in 1985 for CEBAF at Newport News, Virginia. CEBAF’s construction began in February 1987. There were 338 such cavities, in 42.25 cryomodules of eight (a cryomodule is the vacuum-insulated vessel that holds a string of cavities in their helium bath) (Padamsee 2009, section 11.2.1). Specified for 5 MV/m and cooled to 2 K by a 4.8 kW helium refrigerator (Handbook, section 5.9.1, table 1), they sent the first beam to an experimental hall on 1 July 1994. The design energy of 4 GeV was reached in 1995 and 6 GeV in 2000; the cavities were eventually run at about 7.5 MV/m.

The first TESLA workshop met in 1990. Superconducting cavities then ran at about 5 MV/m, too low and too costly for a linear collider. The TESLA collaboration, formally founded in 1994 and centred on DESY, set out to raise the gradient by a factor of five, to 25 MV/m, and to cut the cost per metre. Its nine-cell 1.3 GHz cavity copied CEBAF’s fabrication and added thicker chemical removal, annealing at 1400 °C with a titanium getter, high-pressure rinsing and high-power processing. The first 27 industrial cavities averaged 20.1 MV/m at \(Q_0 = 5\times10^9\), the second 24 averaged 25.0 MV/m, in each case leaving out the few with serious fabrication or material defects (Aune and others 2000). The high-field Q-slope then blocked the way until Saito’s electropolished cavities at KEK (1997) and Lilje’s demonstration at DESY (1999) that a bake at 120 °C was the essential step. For the European XFEL at Hamburg, 816 cavities were built into 102 cryomodules, 97 of which were installed; 832 cavities were vertically tested at DESY between 2013 and March 2016, at up to 15 a week, and reached an average maximum of about 33 MV/m and an average usable gradient of about 30 MV/m. Only 16 per cent needed a second surface treatment (Reschke and others 2017). Electrons were first accelerated in April 2017, in a 2.1 km linac built for energies up to 17.5 GeV; the facility’s construction cost was estimated at EUR 1.22 billion at 2005 prices.

How cavities and RF systems are designed today

The frequency is chosen first, from the beam (bunch spacing, aperture and wakefields, chapter 22), from what power sources exist, and, for superconductors, from the balance between \(R_{BCS} \propto f^2\) and size: the TESLA collaboration chose 1.3 GHz (Aune and others 2000) because low frequencies make bulky cavities, \(R_{BCS}\) dominates above about 3 GHz at 2 K, and high-power klystrons existed at 1.3 GHz. Copper cavities are shaped for shunt impedance under a limit on peak surface field; superconducting cavities for low \(\mathcal{E}_{pk}/\mathcal{E}_{acc}\) and \(B_{pk}/\mathcal{E}_{acc}\). Fields are computed with two-dimensional codes such as Superfish for round cavities and three-dimensional codes such as HFSS, CST Microwave Studio and GdfidL for couplers and ports (Jensen 2014); trajectory codes check for multipacting in cells and couplers. Thermal and mechanical models follow, then the tuner and the coupler. A copper prototype is measured cold on a network analyser and with a bead before high-power tests.

At the bench

Measuring a cavity with a network analyser

A vector network analyser sends a swept sine wave into a port and measures what comes back (\(S_{11}\), reflection) and what comes out of a second port (\(S_{21}\), transmission), in amplitude and phase. On a cavity with an input coupler and a weak pick-up probe:

  • \(S_{21}\) shows a resonance peak. Its centre is \(f_0\); its full width at half power (3 dB down) gives \(Q_L = f_0/\Delta f_{3\,\mathrm{dB}}\). At resonance \(|S_{21}| = 2\sqrt{\beta_1\beta_2}/(1 + \beta_1 + \beta_2)\) for couplings \(\beta_1\), \(\beta_2\) at the two ports.
  • \(S_{11}\) dips at resonance. Its magnitude \(|\Gamma|\) gives the coupling: \(\beta_1 = (1 - |\Gamma|)/(1 + |\Gamma|)\) if undercoupled, \((1 + |\Gamma|)/(1 - |\Gamma|)\) if overcoupled. On a Smith chart the reflection traces a circle; whether the circle encloses the chart’s centre tells which case it is. Then \(Q_0 = Q_L(1 + \beta_1 + \beta_2)\).

For the 2856 MHz copper cell, critically coupled, \(Q_L = 9200\) and the 3 dB width is 310 kHz, easily seen in a sweep. A superconducting cavity is different: in the XFEL vertical tests the fixed input antenna had a design \(Q_{ext}\) of \(8\times10^9\) (5 to \(9\times10^9\) measured) against an expected \(Q_0 \approx 2\times10^{10}\) at 2 K (Reschke and others 2017). That is \(\beta_c = 2.5\), \(Q_L = 5.7\times10^9\) and a bandwidth of 0.23 Hz, too narrow to sweep. Instead a phase-locked loop (a feedback circuit that steers the source’s frequency to hold a fixed phase against the cavity’s signal) keeps the source on the cavity’s frequency, the forward, reflected and transmitted powers are measured, and the power is switched off: the stored energy decays with time constant \(Q_L/\omega\), here 0.70 s, and \(Q_0\) follows from \(Q_L\) and \(\beta_c\).

Pre-requisite (continued): Adiabatic invariants

Pre-requisite. This continues Adiabatic invariants from chapter 7, which showed that when an oscillator’s frequency is changed slowly, its energy changes but \(E/\omega\) does not, and its continuations, which wrote the invariant as a phase-space area. A cavity mode is an oscillator too, so its stored energy over its frequency, \(U/\omega\), is invariant when the cavity is deformed slowly. This section combines that with the pressure the fields exert on the walls to derive Slater’s perturbation theorem, then applies the same argument to a small bead, using the dipoles of section 1’s pre-requisite. The bead pull that follows uses it, and so does the Lorentz-force detuning of section 7. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 1, §49; Wangler, sections 5.12 and 5.13; and Padamsee, Knobloch and Hays, sections 7.4 and 7.6.

A cavity mode as an oscillator. Each mode of a cavity is a single oscillator: its energy swings between electric and magnetic at \(\omega\) (the Maxwell section before section 2). Push a wall in slowly, slowly meaning that the shape changes very little during one RF period, and chapter 7’s result applies:

\[ \frac{U}{\omega} = \text{constant}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{\Delta\omega}{\omega} = \frac{\Delta U}{U} \]

for a small change. The frequency shift is the fractional change of stored energy, which equals the work done on the field by whatever moved the wall.

The pressure of the fields. The fields push and pull on a wall. Chapter 4 showed that an electric field at a conductor pulls it with a force per unit area \(\tfrac12\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}^2\), towards the field region. A magnetic field along a conductor pushes it away with \(\tfrac12\mu_0H^2\): the same energy density, the opposite sign (field lines pull along their length and push sideways on one another: electric lines ending on the wall pull it, magnetic lines running along it press on it). Averaged over an RF cycle, with peak amplitudes, the net outward pressure on the wall is

\[ p = \tfrac14\mu_0|H|^2 - \tfrac14\varepsilon_0|\mathcal{E}|^2 \quad (\text{Pa}). \]

Slater’s theorem. Push a small piece of wall into the cavity, slowly, sweeping out a volume \(\Delta V\). Against an outward pressure \(p\), the work done on the field is \(p\,\Delta V\), and it adds to the stored energy: \(\Delta U = (\tfrac14\mu_0|H|^2 - \tfrac14\varepsilon_0|\mathcal{E}|^2)\Delta V\). The time-averaged stored energy is \(U = \tfrac14\int(\mu_0|H|^2 + \varepsilon_0|\mathcal{E}|^2)\,dV\) (the Poynting section). With \(\Delta\omega/\omega = \Delta U/U\),

\[ \frac{\Delta\omega}{\omega_0} = \frac{\int_{\Delta V}(\mu_0|H|^2 - \varepsilon_0|\mathcal{E}|^2)\,dV}{\int_V(\mu_0|H|^2 + \varepsilon_0|\mathcal{E}|^2)\,dV}, \]

the bench section’s formula. Push in where the magnetic field dominates and the frequency rises; where the electric field dominates, it falls. In the LC picture, pushing in near the capacitor’s plates narrows the gap and raises \(C\); pushing in near the coil shrinks the area the flux uses and lowers \(L\).

A check. Push a whole end plate of the TM\(_{010}\) pillbox inwards by \(\Delta L\). On the plate, \(\int\varepsilon_0|\mathcal{E}_z|^2dA = \varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}_0^2\cdot2\pi\int_0^aJ_0^2r\,dr\) and \(\int\mu_0|H_\varphi|^2dA = \mu_0(\mathcal{E}_0/\eta_0)^2\cdot2\pi\int_0^aJ_1^2r\,dr\). By Lommel’s integrals (the Bessel section before section 2) both radial integrals equal \(\tfrac12a^2J_1^2(2.405)\), and \(\mu_0/\eta_0^2 = \varepsilon_0\), so the two terms cancel exactly: \(\Delta\omega = 0\). That agrees with section 2, where the TM\(_{010}\) frequency does not depend on \(L\).

Lorentz-force detuning. The fields’ own pressure deforms a thin-walled cavity, slowly compared with a period (the RF pulse lasts a millisecond), so Slater’s theorem gives the frequency shift. In the TESLA cell at 25 MV/m, the electric field of 50 MV/m near the irises pulls inward with \(\tfrac14\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}^2 = 5.5\) kPa (time-averaged), and the magnetic field of 107 mT at the equator pushes outward with \(\tfrac14\mu_0H^2 = 2.3\) kPa. The irises move into the electric region and the equator moves out of the magnetic region; both lower the frequency. The pressure grows as the square of the gradient, so the shift does too: section 7’s 600 Hz at 25 MV/m. The tuner, at 315 kHz per mm, must undo 1.9 µm.

A bead. A small object is not a wall displacement with the unperturbed field around it, because the object distorts the field nearby. Use the dipoles instead (section 1’s pre-requisite). A conducting sphere of radius \(r\) in a field \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) becomes a dipole \(\mathbf{p} = \alpha\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) with \(\alpha = 4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3\). An induced dipole is drawn towards strong field: the force on it is \(\tfrac12\alpha\nabla(\mathcal{E}^2)\), or \(\tfrac14\alpha\nabla|\mathcal{E}|^2\) averaged over a cycle. Moving it slowly from where the field is zero to where it is \(\mathcal{E}\), the field does work \(\tfrac14\alpha|\mathcal{E}|^2\) on it, and so loses that energy: \(\Delta U = -\tfrac14\mathbf{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^*\). In a magnetic field the sphere becomes \(\mathbf{m} = -2\pi r^3\mathbf{H}\), which is pushed out of strong field, so the work goes the other way and \(\Delta U = -\tfrac14\mu_0\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{H}^* = +\tfrac12\pi\mu_0r^3|H|^2\). Adding, and dividing by \(U\),

\[ \frac{\Delta\omega}{\omega_0} = -\frac{\pi r^3\left(\varepsilon_0|\mathcal{E}|^2 - \tfrac12\mu_0|H|^2\right)}{U}, \]

the bead formula of the bench section. On the axis of a TM\(_{010}\) cell \(H = 0\) and the shift measures \(|\mathcal{E}|^2\) alone, falling in frequency.

At work. A 0.5 mm bead at the centre of the 2856 MHz pillbox shifts it by \(\Delta f/f = -2r^3/(a^2LJ_1^2) = -1.09\times10^{-5}\), that is 31.3 kHz, which a network analyser sees easily against the cell’s loaded bandwidth.

Exercise T1. The bead pull moves the bead in 1 mm steps, one step a second. Is that slow in the sense of the adiabatic invariant?

Answer

Yes, overwhelmingly: a period at 2856 MHz is 0.35 ns, so the field changes by a tiny fraction per period, and even the motor’s fastest motion takes billions of periods. In practice the analyser measures the steady state at each position, so the condition is met trivially; it matters more for Lorentz-force detuning, where the wall moves during a millisecond pulse, still a million periods.

Exercise T2. A tuner plunger enters the cylindrical wall of the pillbox, at mid-length, where the field is purely magnetic. Does the frequency rise or fall? And a plunger entering an end plate on the axis?

Answer

At the cylindrical wall \(\mathcal{E}_z = 0\) and \(H_\varphi\) is \(0.519\mathcal{E}_0/\eta_0\): only the magnetic term counts, so the frequency rises. On the axis of an end plate \(H_\varphi = 0\) and \(\mathcal{E}_z = \mathcal{E}_0\), the strongest electric field: the frequency falls. Tuners use either, depending on which way and how far they must move the frequency.

Exercise T3. At what gradient would the TESLA cavity’s Lorentz-force detuning equal its loaded bandwidth of 430 Hz, if the shift is 600 Hz at 25 MV/m?

Answer

The shift goes as \(\mathcal{E}^2\): \(\mathcal{E} = 25\sqrt{430/600} = 21.2\) MV/m. Above that, during the pulse the cavity drifts off resonance by more than its bandwidth unless the tuner or the drive compensates, which is why section 7 counts 15 per cent extra power for it.

The bead pull

Slater’s perturbation theorem (Slater, Microwave Electronics, 1950, pp. 80–81, as cited by Wangler 2008, section 5.13, who gives the theorem and the bead formulas below; Maier and Slater 1952 used it to measure fields) says that pushing the wall of a cavity inward by a small volume \(\Delta V\) shifts the frequency by

\[ \frac{\Delta\omega}{\omega_0} = \frac{\int_{\Delta V}(\mu_0|H|^2 - \varepsilon_0|\mathcal{E}|^2)\,dV}{\int_V(\mu_0|H|^2 + \varepsilon_0|\mathcal{E}|^2)\,dV} . \]

Removing volume where the electric field is strong lowers the frequency; where the magnetic field is strong, raises it. (It follows from the work done against the field’s radiation pressure, the adiabatic invariance of \(U/\omega\) for a slowly deformed oscillator; Landau and Lifshitz vol. 1, §49.) A small metal sphere of radius \(r\) in fields of amplitude \(\mathcal{E}\), \(H\) shifts the frequency by \(\Delta\omega/\omega_0 = -\pi r^3(\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}^2 - \mu_0H^2/2)/U\) (Maier and Slater; Wangler 2008, Eq. 5.92, p. 166). On the axis of a TM\(_{010}\) cell \(H = 0\), so the shift measures \(\mathcal{E}^2\) directly. For the pillbox it is \(\Delta f/f = -2r^3/(a^2LJ_1^2)\): a bead of 0.5 mm radius shifts the 2856 MHz cell by 31 kHz.

In practice a bead on a nylon thread is pulled along the axis in steps by a motor (figure 13.14). The analyser sits at the unperturbed resonance and records the phase of \(S_{21}\); for small shifts, \(\tan\Delta\varphi = 2Q_L\Delta f/f\) (11° for the bead above with \(Q_L = 9200\)). The profile of \(\sqrt{\Delta f}\) along the axis is the field profile. Multi-cell cavities are tuned cell by cell until the field is flat. The frequencies of the whole passband (the nine modes of a nine-cell cavity) are a quicker check of the same thing: the XFEL cavities’ passband was measured at the vendor and again on arrival at DESY, to catch deformation in transport (Reschke and others 2017).

A small metal bead on a nylon thread is pulled along the axis by a motor while a network analyser, set at the unperturbed resonance, records the phase of \(S_{21}\). On the axis the magnetic field is zero, so the shift measures \(\mathcal{E}^2\); a well-tuned cavity in the \(\pi\) mode shows one equal hump per cell (drawn, not measured).

Conditioning a copper cavity

A new copper cavity will not take full power at once. The power is raised slowly, often with short pulses first, then lengthened. The vacuum gauges show bursts of gas as surfaces outgas under electron bombardment; the reflected-power monitor shows sudden reflections as breakdowns short the cavity; X-ray monitors count field emission. The control system backs off after each event and climbs again. Conditioning a high-gradient structure takes weeks to months of running; the CLIC prototypes each needed three to four months. Comparisons of CLIC prototypes tested at KEK and CERN showed that conditioning advances with the number of RF pulses, not the number of breakdowns, so the best strategy may be to raise the field slowly enough never to exceed the target breakdown rate (Degiovanni and others 2016). Afterwards, breakdown spots show as craters under a microscope.

The vertical test of a superconducting cavity

In the AMTF test hall at DESY, each XFEL cavity, sealed under vacuum and fitted with its antennas, was mounted in an insert holding up to four cavities, and lowered into one of two bath cryostats in a concrete pit (Reschke and others 2017). The procedure, which was largely automated:

  1. Pump and leak-check; residual gas analysis.
  2. Cool from 300 K to 100 K in about 12 hours (5 mK/s), hold at 100 K for 4 to 6 hours to test for hydride (“Q disease”), cool through \(T_c\) at about 200 mK/s, then pump the helium bath down to about 30 mbar to reach 2 K.
  3. Measure \(Q_0\) against \(\mathcal{E}_{acc}\) at 2 K in long pulses (5 to 20 s on, about 50 s off) for the \(\pi\) mode, with input power limited to about 200 W, and the frequencies of the passband; X-ray detectors above and below the cryostat at each point. At least two power rises, until two curves agree.
  4. Record the limit for each rise: breakdown (quench), breakdown with field emission, forward power (with or without field emission), or a quench caused by heating of a higher-order-mode coupler.

The usable gradient was the lowest of the quench field, the field where \(Q_0\) falls below \(10^{10}\), and the field where X-rays exceed 0.01 mGy/min at the top detector (0.12 at the bottom). The facility ran about 40 tests a month.

A klystron’s saturation curve

At fixed beam voltage, the output power rises with drive power, then levels off and falls: past the optimum bunching parameter, the bunches overshoot. The top of that curve is saturation, where the efficiency is highest. But at saturation the output no longer responds to the drive, so the low-level RF cannot correct anything. Operators therefore run below saturation, giving up some efficiency for control headroom. Raising the beam voltage moves the whole curve up: the Toshiba multi-beam klystron’s measured efficiency stayed high over a wide range of output power as the voltage was varied (CLIC 2018).

Interlocks and how failures look

RF systems protect themselves faster than an operator can:

  • Reflected power: a directional coupler measures the reflected wave; above a threshold, the drive is cut, within the pulse in fast systems. Signature: a step in reflected power and a collapse of the cavity probe signal, usually a breakdown in the cavity or waveguide.
  • Arc detectors: photodiodes or optical fibres viewing waveguide windows and couplers see the light of an arc and trip the RF. A cracked ceramic window lets air into the vacuum.
  • Vacuum: a pressure rise near a coupler or cavity trips the RF.
  • Quench detection in a superconducting cavity: the probe signal collapses and the helium pressure jumps.
  • Klystron: body current, cathode current and collector temperature; a gun arc trips the modulator. Each event is logged with the waveforms of forward, reflected and probe power at the moment of the trip, which is how the fault is located.

Where it stops

For copper, the limits are breakdown and pulsed heating. The field a surface holds rises with frequency, and short pulses limit both the heating and the breakdown rate, so the high-gradient copper path leads to short pulses at high frequency: 72 to 100 MV/m at 12 GHz in CLIC’s design, in RF pulses of 244 ns. The peak power then dominates the cost.

For superconducting niobium, the hard limit is the critical magnetic field at the surface, about 200 to 240 mT, which in the TESLA shape is 47 to 56 MV/m; the best single cells have reached 59 MV/m. Real production cavities stop earlier, at quench, field emission and Q-slope, around 30 MV/m on average in the XFEL’s vertical tests. Other superconductors with higher critical fields, such as Nb\(_3\)Sn, are being studied.

Any metal cavity holds a field at its surface, and the surface sets the limit: tens of megavolts per metre. Fields of gigavolts per metre need a medium that is already broken down, a plasma, carrying a wave driven by a laser or a beam (chapter 27). The structures that string cavities into linacs, and their beam dynamics, are chapter 14; what a strong beam does back to the cavities is chapter 22; and why linear colliders chose between copper and niobium is chapter 25.

Exercises

1. Find the skin depth and surface resistance of copper (\(\sigma = 5.8\times10^7\) S/m) at 352.2 MHz and 1.3 GHz. By what factor does \(R_s\) change?

Answer

\(\delta = \sqrt{2/\omega\mu_0\sigma}\), \(R_s = 1/\sigma\delta\). At 352.2 MHz, \(\delta = 3.52\) µm and \(R_s = 4.90\) mΩ. At 1.3 GHz, \(\delta = 1.83\) µm and \(R_s = 9.41\) mΩ. The ratio is \(\sqrt{1300/352.2} = 1.92\).

2. What radius must a pillbox have for its TM\(_{010}\) mode at 200 MHz, 352.2 MHz and 1.3 GHz? Why are low-frequency cavities given nose cones or made re-entrant?

Answer

\(a = 2.405c/2\pi f\): 574 mm, 326 mm and 88.3 mm. A plain pillbox at 200 MHz would be over a metre across. Nose cones add capacitance near the axis, which lowers the frequency for a given outer size, and shorten the gap to raise \(T_t\).

3. A Kilpatrick factor of 1.84 at 352 MHz: what surface field is that? What is \(\mathcal{E}_K\) at 201.25 MHz and at 2856 MHz?

Answer

Solve \(f = 1.64\mathcal{E}_K^2e^{-8.5/\mathcal{E}_K}\) numerically: \(\mathcal{E}_K = 18.45\) MV/m at 352.2 MHz, so \(1.84\mathcal{E}_K = 34\) MV/m (Linac4’s RFQ). \(\mathcal{E}_K = 14.8\) MV/m at 201.25 MHz and 45.8 MV/m at 2856 MHz.

4. For WR-284 at 2856 MHz, find the cutoff frequency, the guide wavelength and the group velocity. How long does the front of an RF pulse take to travel 30 m of guide?

Answer

\(a_w = 72.14\) mm, \(f_c = c/2a_w = 2.078\) GHz. \(\lambda_g = \lambda/\sqrt{1 - (f_c/f)^2} = 153.0\) mm. \(v_g = c\sqrt{1 - (f_c/f)^2} = 0.686c\). 30 m takes 146 ns.

5. Show that for the TM\(_{010}\) pillbox \(Q_0 = (a/\delta)\,L/(L + a)\), and evaluate it for a copper cell at 2856 MHz with \(L = \lambda/2\). What power holds 1 MV, and why is such a cavity pulsed?

Answer

From section 4, \(Q_0 = 2.405\eta_0/[2R_s(1 + a/L)]\). With \(R_s = 1/\sigma\delta\) and \(\sigma\delta = 2/(\omega\mu_0\delta)\), \(2.405\eta_0\sigma\delta/2 = 2.405\eta_0/(\omega\mu_0\delta) = 2.405/(k\delta) = a/\delta\). Numbers: \(a = 40.2\) mm, \(\delta = 1.24\) µm, \(a/\delta = 32\,480\), \(L/(L + a) = 0.566\), \(Q_0 = 18\,400\). With \(R/Q = 196\ \Omega\), \(R_{sh} = 3.61\) MΩ and 1 MV needs 277 kW, with 1.2 kW/cm² at the cylindrical wall. Only pulsed operation keeps the average heat removable.

6. For a TESLA cavity (\(R/Q = 1036\ \Omega\), \(G = 270\ \Omega\), \(L = 1.038\) m) at 23.6 MV/m: (a) the wall loss at \(Q_0 = 10^{10}\); (b) the ideal refrigerator work to remove it at 2 K; (c) the loss if the cavity were copper.

Answer
  1. \(\hat{V} = 24.5\) MV, \(P = \hat{V}^2/(R/Q)Q_0 = 57.9\) W. (b) The Carnot factor \((300 - 2)/2 = 149\) gives 8.6 kW of work; real plants need several times more. (c) \(Q_0 = G/R_s = 270/0.0094 = 2.9\times10^4\), and \(P = 20\) MW, continuously.

7. An 8 mA beam is to be accelerated on crest by a TESLA cavity at 25 MV/m. Find the matched \(Q_{ext}\), the bandwidth and the time to fill the cavity with the beam’s forward power. What fraction of the forward power is reflected when the cavity is full and there is no beam?

Answer

\(Q_{ext} = \hat{V}/[(R/Q)I_b] = 25.95\times10^6/(1036 \times 0.008) = 3.13\times10^6\); with \(Q_0 \gg Q_{ext}\), \(Q_L \approx Q_{ext}\) and the bandwidth is 1.3 GHz / \(3.13\times10^6\) = 415 Hz (430 Hz for the rounded \(3\times10^6\) of Aune and others). \(\tau = 2Q_L/\omega = 767\) µs; the asymptotic voltage without beam is twice the target, so filling takes \(\tau\ln2 = 531\) µs. With no beam at steady state, \(\beta_c = Q_0/Q_{ext}\) is in the thousands and \(\Gamma = (\beta_c - 1)/(\beta_c + 1) \approx 1\): almost all the power is reflected (for \(Q_0 = 10^{10}\), \(\beta_c = 3200\) and 99.9 per cent) into the circulator’s load.

8. A bead of radius 0.5 mm is pulled along the axis of the 2856 MHz pillbox (\(Q_L = 9200\)). Find the frequency shift at the centre and the phase shift of \(S_{21}\) at the unperturbed frequency.

Answer

\(U = (\varepsilon_0/2)\mathcal{E}_0^2\pi a^2LJ_1^2\) and \(\Delta\omega/\omega = -\pi r^3\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}_0^2/U\), so \(\Delta f/f = -2r^3/(a^2LJ_1^2) = -1.10\times10^{-5}\): 31.3 kHz. \(\tan\Delta\varphi = 2Q_L\Delta f/f\) gives 11.4°.

9. Derive the two-cavity klystron’s ideal efficiency of 58 per cent. Then find the drift length that gives \(X = 1.841\) for a 420 kV beam at 2856 MHz with \(V_1 = 0.1V_0\), using the relativistic velocity modulation.

Answer

The fundamental current is \(2I_0J_1(X)\), maximal at \(X = 1.841\) with \(J_1 = 0.5819\). With the output gap voltage at most \(V_0\), \(P_{out} = \tfrac12 \cdot 2I_0J_1 \cdot V_0\) and \(\eta = J_1 = 58.2\) per cent. Relativistically \(\Delta v/v = qV_1/(\gamma^3\beta^2mc^2)\); at 420 kV, \(\gamma = 1.822\), \(\beta = 0.836\), so \(\Delta v/v = 0.0195\). \(X = (\Delta v/v)\,\omega L_d/v_0\) gives \(L_d = 1.32\) m. Space charge, ignored here, shortens the useful drift; real tubes add intermediate cavities.

10. Derive the two-surface multipacting condition for parallel plates with emission at \(\phi_e = 0\). For a 5 mm gap at 1.3 GHz, find the voltage and impact energy for \(N = 1\) and \(N = 3\). Which is more dangerous on niobium, and why? Then compare the BCS fit’s \(Q_0\) for a TESLA cavity at 4.2 K and 2 K with \(R_{res} = 3\) nΩ.

Answer

From section 10, \(V = m\omega^2d^2/(N\pi e)\) and \(W_{imp} = 2m(\omega d)^2/(N\pi)^2\). For \(d = 5\) mm: \(N = 1\), 3.02 kV and 1.92 keV; \(N = 3\), 1.01 kV and 214 eV. Niobium’s secondary yield peaks near 200 eV at 1.4 to 1.6, so \(N = 3\) multiplies; at 1.9 keV the yield is lower. With the fit, \(R_{BCS} = 533\) nΩ at 4.2 K and 10.9 nΩ at 2 K; \(Q_0 = 270/R_s = 5.0\times10^8\) at 4.2 K and \(1.9\times10^{10}\) at 2 K.
