F5 Oscillations and waves

1545 to 1893: Cardano’s impossible roots, Euler’s formula, d’Alembert’s vibrating string, Fourier’s sums of sines, Young’s fringes, Doppler’s moving stars and Steinmetz’s complex currents. Complex numbers as the natural language of anything that repeats; the oscillator, its resonance and its quality factor; and waves, from a string to a waveguide. Every RF cavity, every betatron oscillation and every photon of synchrotron light in the course is one of these.

What this chapter assumes. High-school mathematics and physics, F1 Calculus (derivatives, the exponential, Taylor series, partial derivatives, and the oscillation equation \(\ddot x = -\omega^2x\) with its solution \(A\cos\omega t + B\sin\omega t\)), F2 Vectors and vector calculus for section 13, and from F3 Newtonian mechanics and F4 Electricity and magnetism only Newton’s second law, Hooke’s law, the energy of a spring, and the facts that a capacitor stores charge \(Q = CV\), an inductor resists a change of current with a voltage \(L\,dI/dt\), and Maxwell’s equations give waves travelling at \(c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}\).

What comes next. The pre-requisite sections inside the chapters take this further: Complex numbers and complex functions, Simple and damped harmonic oscillator and Driven oscillator, resonance and quality factor in chapter 7, RLC circuits and phasors in chapter 4, Fourier series and transforms and Transmission lines in chapter 8, and Coupled oscillators, Maxwell’s equations and the wave equation and Waveguides in chapter 13. F7 Atoms, nuclei and quanta turns waves into photons and matter waves.

In one page

The problem. An accelerator is full of things that repeat. The voltage across an RF cavity swings hundreds of millions of times a second; a particle in a ring wobbles about its ideal path a few times a turn; the light from a bending magnet or an undulator is a wave; a signal travelling down a cable to a cavity is a wave, and part of it bounces back if the cavity is not matched to the cable. Writing every one of these with sines and cosines works, but the algebra grows fast: every derivative turns a sine into a cosine, every phase shift needs an addition formula, and two oscillations added together need a page of trigonometry.

The idea. Represent an oscillation as an arrow turning steadily in a plane, and the plane as the plane of complex numbers. Euler’s formula, \(e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta\), says that the turning arrow is an exponential, \(e^{i\omega t}\). Differentiating it multiplies it by \(i\omega\); shifting its phase multiplies it by a fixed complex number; adding two oscillations is adding two arrows. A linear differential equation becomes algebra. With that tool, the damped and driven oscillator, its resonance curve and its quality factor \(Q\), and the circuits that make up a cavity (impedances \(R\), \(i\omega L\) and \(1/(i\omega C)\)) each take a few lines. A wave is an oscillation that also moves: the wave equation, derived for a string, has solutions that travel at a fixed speed, and its sinusoidal solutions are \(e^{i(\omega t - kx)}\). Two waves add (interference); two waves running opposite ways make a standing wave, whose allowed frequencies are the modes of a string, a pipe or a cavity. Where the medium changes, part of a wave is reflected, unless the impedances match. Where the speed depends on frequency (dispersion), a pulse travels at the group velocity. Light is the electromagnetic case. And by Fourier’s idea, any signal is a sum of such sinusoids.

Equations to remember.

  • Complex numbers: \(z = x + iy = re^{i\theta}\), with \(i^2 = -1\), modulus \(r = |z| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\), argument \(\theta\); Euler’s formula \(e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta\).
  • An oscillation: \(x(t) = \mathrm{Re}(Xe^{i\omega t})\), with complex amplitude \(X = |X|e^{i\phi}\); \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\) becomes multiplication by \(i\omega\). \(\omega = 2\pi f\) (rad/s), \(f\) in Hz.
  • Damped oscillator: \(\ddot x + 2\Gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2x = 0\) gives \(x = Ae^{-\Gamma t}\cos(\omega_d t + \phi)\), \(\omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \Gamma^2}\); \(\Gamma\) is the damping rate (s\(^{-1}\)).
  • Quality factor: \(Q = \dfrac{\omega_0 U}{P} = \dfrac{\omega_0}{2\Gamma}\), with \(U\) the stored energy (J) and \(P\) the power lost (W); the amplitude rings down with time constant \(2Q/\omega_0\).
  • Driven oscillator: \(X = \dfrac{F_0/m}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 + 2i\Gamma\omega}\); near resonance the response falls to \(1/\sqrt{1 + y^2}\) of its peak and lags by \(\arctan y\) more, with \(y = 2Q\,\Delta f/f_0\); full width at half power \(\Delta f = f_0/Q\).
  • Impedances: \(Z_R = R\), \(Z_L = i\omega L\), \(Z_C = 1/(i\omega C)\); \(V = ZI\) for complex amplitudes. An \(LC\) circuit resonates at \(\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}\); a series \(RLC\) has \(Q = \omega_0L/R\).
  • Beats: \(\cos\omega_1 t + \cos\omega_2 t = 2\cos\tfrac{(\omega_1 - \omega_2)t}{2}\cos\tfrac{(\omega_1 + \omega_2)t}{2}\); the loudness repeats at \(f_1 - f_2\).
  • Wave equation: \(\dfrac{\partial^2y}{\partial t^2} = v^2\dfrac{\partial^2y}{\partial x^2}\), solved by any \(f(x - vt) + g(x + vt)\); on a string \(v = \sqrt{T/\mu}\), with tension \(T\) (N) and mass per length \(\mu\) (kg/m).
  • A sinusoidal wave: \(y = A\cos(\omega t - kx)\), wavenumber \(k = 2\pi/\lambda\) (rad/m), phase velocity \(v_{ph} = \omega/k = f\lambda\).
  • Standing waves between fixed ends a distance \(L\) apart: \(f_n = nv/(2L)\), \(n = 1, 2, 3, \dots\)
  • Reflection at a change of impedance (voltage on a line): \(\Gamma = \dfrac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0}\); the fraction \(\Gamma^2\) of the power is reflected.
  • Dispersion: group velocity \(v_g = d\omega/dk\); for \(\omega^2 = \omega_c^2 + c^2k^2\) (a waveguide), \(v_g = c\sqrt{1 - (f_c/f)^2}\) and \(v_{ph}v_g = c^2\).
  • Electromagnetic waves: \(c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0} = 299\,792\,458\) m/s, \(B_0 = \mathcal{E}_0/c\), intensity \(I = \mathcal{E}_0^2/(2\eta_0)\) with \(\eta_0 = \sqrt{\mu_0/\varepsilon_0} = 376.7\ \Omega\).
  • Doppler, slow source or observer approaching at speed \(u\): \(f' \approx f(1 + u/v)\).
  • Interference and diffraction: bright fringes at \(d\sin\theta = m\lambda\); nothing much smaller than \(\lambda\) can be resolved.
  • Fourier: any periodic signal is a sum of sinusoids at multiples of its frequency; a pulse of length \(\Delta t\) contains a band of frequencies \(\Delta f \sim 1/\Delta t\).

Numbers worth knowing.

  • Wavelengths in free space: 1.499 m at 200 MHz, 0.749 m at 400 MHz, 0.2306 m at 1.3 GHz, 0.1050 m at 2.856 GHz. A photon of 1 eV has a wavelength of 1.240 µm; of 12.4 keV, 0.1 nm.
  • A copper cell at 2856 MHz with \(Q_0 = 18\,400\) has a bandwidth of 155 kHz and rings down with an amplitude time constant of 2.05 µs, about 4060 cycles to half amplitude. A superconducting 1.3 GHz cavity with \(Q_0 = 10^{10}\) would ring for seconds.
  • A resonator with \(Q = 200\) at 10 MHz detuned by 19 kHz gives only 0.80 of its peak voltage, 37° late: a resonator must be held on tune to a small part of its bandwidth.
  • A 50 Ω cable into a 75 Ω load reflects 20 per cent of the voltage and 4 per cent of the power.
  • In the WR-284 waveguide at 2856 MHz, the cutoff is 2.078 GHz, the group velocity \(0.686c\), the phase velocity \(1.458c\) and the guide wavelength 153.0 mm.
  • Sunlight at the top of the atmosphere, 1361 W/m\(^2\), has an electric field of 1013 V/m (peak).
  • To see a structure of 1 fm, a probe needs a wavelength of about 1 fm, which for an electron means a momentum of about 1.24 GeV/\(c\).

The problem it solved

Square roots of negative numbers

Girolamo Cardano’s Ars Magna (1545), the book that published the solution of the cubic equation, asks for two numbers that add to 10 and multiply to 40. The answer comes out as \(5 + \sqrt{-15}\) and \(5 - \sqrt{-15}\); Cardano noted that the product works and called the exercise as subtle as it was useless. Rafael Bombelli, in his L’Algebra (1572), went further: the cubic formula sometimes passes through square roots of negative numbers on the way to a perfectly real answer, and he wrote down the rules for computing with them. For two centuries they stayed “imaginary”, tolerated as a device. Leonhard Euler, in his Introductio in analysin infinitorum (1748), gave the formula \(e^{iv} = \cos v + i\sin v\) that joins them to the exponential and to trigonometry. Caspar Wessel (a paper read to the Royal Danish Academy in 1797 and printed in 1799) and Jean-Robert Argand (1806) independently drew complex numbers as points in a plane, with multiplication as turning and stretching, and Carl Friedrich Gauss gave them their name, complex numbers, in 1831.

The vibrating string

Jean le Rond d’Alembert, in a memoir dated 1747 for the Berlin Academy, wrote down the partial differential equation of a vibrating string and showed that its solutions are two shapes travelling in opposite directions without change: the wave equation and its general solution, both in section 8. Daniel Bernoulli argued in 1753 that every motion of the string is a sum of its simple modes, each a sine. Euler and d’Alembert doubted that a sum of smooth sines could make an arbitrary shape, such as a string plucked into a corner. The question was settled by Joseph Fourier, who in a memoir on the flow of heat presented to the Paris Academy in 1807, and in his book Théorie analytique de la chaleur (1822), claimed and used that any reasonable function on an interval is such a sum (section 16).

Light, sound and the moving source

Thomas Young, in lectures to the Royal Society between 1801 and 1803, explained the coloured bands seen where light crosses itself as interference of waves, and measured the wavelengths of the colours, about half a micrometre. Augustin Fresnel’s memoir on diffraction won the French Academy’s prize in 1819. Christian Doppler argued in 1842 that the colour of a star should change with its speed towards or away from us. Christophorus Buys Ballot tested the idea for sound in 1845, with musicians playing a steady note on a train near Utrecht and listeners by the track. James Clerk Maxwell showed in 1865 that his equations of electricity and magnetism carry waves at the speed of light, and Heinrich Hertz made and detected such waves in 1887 and 1888, measuring their wavelength from the standing waves in front of a metal sheet.

Alternating currents

When electric power and telephones arrived, engineers had to calculate circuits driven by alternating voltages. Oliver Heaviside coined the word impedance in 1886. Charles Proteus Steinmetz, at the International Electrical Congress in Chicago in 1893, showed that if every sinusoidal voltage and current is written as a complex number, a circuit of resistors, coils and capacitors obeys Ohm’s law with complex resistances, and the calculus disappears. Every RF engineer since has worked that way, and so does section 6. Lord Rayleigh, in 1897, solved for waves inside a hollow metal tube and found that they travel only above a cutoff frequency (section 12), the theory that made the waveguides of chapter 13 possible.

The mathematics

0. What this chapter assumes, and a quick check

  • Trigonometry. Radians; \(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1\); \(\cos(a \pm b) = \cos a\cos b \mp \sin a\sin b\) and \(\sin(a \pm b) = \sin a\cos b \pm \cos a\sin b\).
  • Calculus (F1). \((\sin\omega t)' = \omega\cos\omega t\), \((e^{st})' = se^{st}\), the chain rule, partial derivatives, and the Taylor series of \(e^x\), \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\).
  • The oscillator (F1, section 10). A mass \(m\) (kg) on a spring of stiffness \(k\) (N/m) obeys \(m\ddot x = -kx\), which is solved by \(x = A\cos\omega_0 t + B\sin\omega_0 t\) with \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}\). Its energy \(\tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + \tfrac12 kx^2\) stays constant.
  • Circuits (F4). Ohm’s law \(V = IR\); a capacitor holds charge \(Q = CV\) (farads, F); an inductor has a voltage \(V = L\,dI/dt\) across it (henries, H); current is the rate of flow of charge, \(I = dQ/dt\).

Check yourself. (a) Write \(3\cos\omega t + 4\sin\omega t\) as \(R\cos(\omega t - \phi)\). (b) What is \(\dfrac{d^2}{dt^2}e^{-2t}\)? (c) A 50 pF capacitor at 10 kV holds how much charge? (d) Expand \(\cos x\) to the \(x^4\) term. Answers: (a) \(R = 5\), \(\phi = 53.13°\) (from \(R\cos\phi = 3\), \(R\sin\phi = 4\)); (b) \(4e^{-2t}\); (c) 0.5 µC; (d) \(1 - x^2/2 + x^4/24\).

1. Complex numbers

Why invent them. The equation \(x^2 = -1\) has no real solution, since every real number squared is positive or zero. Rather than give up, admit a new number \(i\) with

\[ i^2 = -1, \]

and keep every other rule of algebra (adding, multiplying, brackets) as it was. Nothing contradictory follows, and the reward, as this chapter shows, is that oscillations become as easy as exponentials. Electrical engineers write \(j\) instead of \(i\), since \(i\) is their symbol for current; chapter 13 follows them. The two mean the same thing.

Complex numbers and their arithmetic. A complex number is \(z = x + iy\) with \(x\) and \(y\) real. \(x\) is its real part, \(\mathrm{Re}\,z\), and \(y\) its imaginary part, \(\mathrm{Im}\,z\) (a real number, despite the name). Add part by part. Multiply by expanding the brackets and replacing \(i^2\) by \(-1\). With \(z_1 = 3 + 4i\) and \(z_2 = 1 - 2i\):

\[ z_1 + z_2 = 4 + 2i, \qquad z_1z_2 = 3 - 6i + 4i - 8i^2 = 11 - 2i . \]

The conjugate and division. The complex conjugate of \(z = x + iy\) is \(z^* = x - iy\) (also written \(\bar z\)). Its use: \(zz^* = x^2 + y^2\) is real and never negative. To divide, multiply top and bottom by the conjugate of the bottom, which makes the bottom real:

\[ \frac{z_1}{z_2} = \frac{(3 + 4i)(1 + 2i)}{(1 - 2i)(1 + 2i)} = \frac{-5 + 10i}{5} = -1 + 2i . \]

Check: \((-1 + 2i)(1 - 2i) = -1 + 2i + 2i + 4 = 3 + 4i\).

The complex plane. Draw \(z = x + iy\) as the point \((x, y)\), or as the arrow from the origin to it: real parts along the horizontal axis, imaginary parts up the vertical one. Adding complex numbers is adding arrows nose to tail, exactly like adding vectors in F2. The length of the arrow is the modulus,

\[ |z| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = \sqrt{zz^*}, \]

and its angle from the positive real axis is the argument \(\theta = \arg z\), with \(x = |z|\cos\theta\) and \(y = |z|\sin\theta\). For \(z_1 = 3 + 4i\): \(|z_1| = 5\) and \(\theta = \arctan(4/3) = 53.13°\). (Take care with the quadrant: \(-3 - 4i\) also has \(y/x = 4/3\), but its angle is \(233.13°\), or \(-126.87°\). Draw the point.) The conjugate is the mirror image in the real axis: same modulus, opposite angle.

Polar form. So every complex number can be written

\[ z = |z|(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) . \]

Multiply two numbers in this form and use the addition formulas:

\[ \begin{aligned} z_1z_2 &= r_1r_2\big[(\cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2 - \sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2) \\ &\qquad + i(\sin\theta_1\cos\theta_2 + \cos\theta_1\sin\theta_2)\big] \\ &= r_1r_2\big[\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) + i\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)\big] . \end{aligned} \]

Moduli multiply and angles add. This is the geometric meaning of complex multiplication: multiplying by a complex number of modulus \(r\) and angle \(\theta\) stretches every arrow by \(r\) and turns it by \(\theta\). In particular, multiplying by \(i\) (modulus 1, angle \(90°\)) turns an arrow a quarter turn anticlockwise without changing its length, and doing it twice turns it half a turn, which is multiplying by \(-1\). That is what \(i^2 = -1\) means geometrically.

2. Euler’s formula

The claim. For any real \(\theta\),

\[ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta . \]

The proof, by Taylor series. F1 section 8 gave \(e^x = 1 + x + x^2/2! + x^3/3! + \dots\) for every real \(x\). Use the same series to define \(e^z\) for complex \(z\); it converges for every \(z\) for the same reason. Put \(z = i\theta\), and use \(i^2 = -1\), \(i^3 = -i\), \(i^4 = 1\), after which the powers repeat:

\[ \begin{aligned} e^{i\theta} &= 1 + i\theta - \frac{\theta^2}{2!} - i\frac{\theta^3}{3!} + \frac{\theta^4}{4!} + i\frac{\theta^5}{5!} - \dots \\ &= \Big(1 - \frac{\theta^2}{2!} + \frac{\theta^4}{4!} - \dots\Big) + i\Big(\theta - \frac{\theta^3}{3!} + \frac{\theta^5}{5!} - \dots\Big) . \end{aligned} \]

The two brackets are the Taylor series of \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\). That is the proof. Numerically, at \(\theta = 1\) rad the real bracket to \(\theta^6\) gives 0.540278 against \(\cos 1 = 0.540302\), and the imaginary bracket to \(\theta^5\) gives 0.841667 against \(\sin 1 = 0.841471\).

A second view. The function \(g(\theta) = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta\) has the derivative \(-\sin\theta + i\cos\theta = i(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) = ig(\theta)\), and \(g(0) = 1\). The exponential \(e^{i\theta}\) has the same two properties, and a first-order equation with a starting value has only one solution (F1 section 10). So they are the same function.

What it means. \(e^{i\theta}\) is the point at angle \(\theta\) on the circle of radius 1. Every complex number is

\[ z = re^{i\theta}, \]

and the rule “moduli multiply, angles add” is just the law of exponents: \(r_1e^{i\theta_1}\cdot r_2e^{i\theta_2} = r_1r_2e^{i(\theta_1 + \theta_2)}\). Some values worth knowing: \(e^{i\pi/2} = i\), \(e^{i\pi} = -1\), \(e^{2\pi i} = 1\), \(e^{i\pi/3} = 0.5 + 0.866i\). Powers become easy: \((re^{i\theta})^n = r^ne^{in\theta}\), which is de Moivre’s formula.

Cosine and sine from exponentials. Adding \(e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta\) and \(e^{-i\theta} = \cos\theta - i\sin\theta\), and subtracting them,

\[ \cos\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta}}{2}, \qquad \sin\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta}}{2i} . \]

A cosine is two arrows turning in opposite directions, whose imaginary parts cancel. Every trigonometric identity can be derived from these two lines and the law of exponents, without memorising it. For example \(\cos^2\theta = \tfrac14(e^{2i\theta} + 2 + e^{-2i\theta}) = \tfrac12(1 + \cos 2\theta)\).

3. Oscillations as turning arrows

The picture. Let an arrow of length \(A\) turn anticlockwise in the complex plane at a steady \(\omega\) radians per second, starting at angle \(\phi\). Its tip is at \(z(t) = Ae^{i(\omega t + \phi)}\), and the shadow of the tip on the real axis is

\[ x(t) = \mathrm{Re}\,z(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi) . \]

That shadow is a simple harmonic oscillation of amplitude \(A\), angular frequency \(\omega\) and phase \(\phi\). Every sinusoidal quantity in the course, a cavity voltage, a betatron oscillation, the field of a light wave, can be pictured as the shadow of a turning arrow.

The complex amplitude. Collect the length and starting angle into one complex number, the complex amplitude \(X = Ae^{i\phi}\). Then

\[ x(t) = \mathrm{Re}\big(Xe^{i\omega t}\big) . \]

\(X\) holds everything about the oscillation except the frequency, which is shared by everything in a circuit driven at one frequency. In circuits \(X\) is called a phasor. (Some books use \(\mathrm{Re}(Xe^{-i\omega t})\); quantum mechanics does. The physics is the same, but signs of phases flip. This chapter, like most of the course, uses \(e^{+i\omega t}\).)

Why this is so useful. Three operations become simple:

  • Differentiating multiplies by \(i\omega\): \(\dfrac{d}{dt}Xe^{i\omega t} = i\omega Xe^{i\omega t}\). Since \(i = e^{i\pi/2}\), the velocity of an oscillation is \(\omega\) times its displacement, turned a quarter cycle ahead. Twice: the acceleration is \((i\omega)^2 = -\omega^2\) times the displacement, opposite to it.
  • Shifting the phase by \(\delta\) multiplies by \(e^{i\delta}\).
  • Adding oscillations of the same frequency adds their complex amplitudes, arrow to arrow.

And taking the real part can wait until the end. If the coefficients of a linear equation are real, the real part of any complex solution is itself a solution, because the real part of a sum is the sum of the real parts, and the real part of (real number) \(\times z\) is that real number times \(\mathrm{Re}\,z\). So: solve with complex exponentials, take the real part last.

Example: the free oscillator again. For \(\ddot x = -\omega_0^2x\) try \(x = e^{st}\), with \(s\) a number to be found. Then \(\ddot x = s^2e^{st}\), and the equation needs \(s^2 = -\omega_0^2\), so \(s = \pm i\omega_0\). The general solution is \(x = c_1e^{i\omega_0 t} + c_2e^{-i\omega_0 t}\), and for \(x\) to be real \(c_2\) must be \(c_1^*\), which gives back \(A\cos(\omega_0 t + \phi)\), as in F1. The trial \(e^{st}\) will solve every equation in sections 4 to 7.

Example: two cavities adding. A particle crosses two RF gaps, each with a peak voltage of 100 kV at the same frequency, but the second reaches its peak \(60°\) later as seen by the particle. The voltage it gains in total is the real part of the sum of the two phasors:

\[ 100 + 100e^{i\pi/3} = 150 + 86.6i = 173.2\,e^{i\,30°}\ \text{kV} . \]

At best it gains 173.2 kV, not 200 kV, and the best moment is \(30°\) after the first gap’s peak. In a real linac every cell is phased so that the arrows line up; chapter 12 shows why they are deliberately set a little off the crest.

Example: the rate of change. \(V(t) = \mathrm{Re}(V_0e^{i\omega t})\) with \(V_0 = 100\) kV at 200 MHz has \(\dot V = \mathrm{Re}(i\omega V_0e^{i\omega t})\), of amplitude \(\omega V_0 = 1.26 \times 10^{14}\) V/s, as F1 found the long way.

Example: what one detector sees. A particle in a ring makes \(Q\) betatron oscillations per turn: \(Q\) is the tune (chapter 11). A pickup at one place sees its oscillation once a turn, so between readings the arrow turns by \(2\pi Q\). For \(Q = 6.23\) that is 6 full turns and \(0.23\) of a turn more; since \(e^{2\pi i\times 6} = 1\), the pickup sees exactly what a tune of 0.23 would give. Only the fractional part of the tune can be read from one pickup, which is why ring physicists speak of it so often.

4. The damped oscillator and its Q

The picture. A real oscillator loses energy: a pendulum to air, a circuit to resistance, a cavity to the walls in which its currents flow. The simplest loss is a force against the velocity, \(-b\dot x\), like the drag on a slowly moving body in a fluid. Newton’s law becomes \(m\ddot x = -kx - b\dot x\), or

\[ \ddot x + 2\Gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2x = 0, \qquad \omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m},\quad \Gamma = \frac{b}{2m} . \]

\(\Gamma\) (s\(^{-1}\)) is the damping rate; the factor 2 makes the answer tidy.

Solving it. Try \(x = e^{st}\): \(s^2 + 2\Gamma s + \omega_0^2 = 0\), a quadratic with roots

\[ s = -\Gamma \pm \sqrt{\Gamma^2 - \omega_0^2} = -\Gamma \pm i\omega_d, \qquad \omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \Gamma^2} . \]

For light damping (\(\Gamma < \omega_0\), every resonator in the course) the square root is imaginary and \(e^{st} = e^{-\Gamma t}e^{\pm i\omega_d t}\). Taking the real part,

\[ x(t) = Ae^{-\Gamma t}\cos(\omega_d t + \phi) . \]

The oscillation goes on at nearly the old frequency, inside an envelope that decays as \(e^{-\Gamma t}\). (For \(\Gamma > \omega_0\) both roots are real and negative: the body creeps back without swinging, which is how a car’s shock absorbers are meant to work. At \(\Gamma = \omega_0\), critical damping, it returns fastest without overshoot. Chapter 7’s pre-requisite treats all three cases.)

The energy and the quality factor. The energy goes as the square of the amplitude, so \(U(t) = U_0e^{-2\Gamma t}\), and the power lost is \(P = -dU/dt = 2\Gamma U\). The quality factor is defined by energy:

\[ Q = \frac{\omega_0 U}{P} = 2\pi\,\frac{\text{energy stored}}{\text{energy lost per cycle}} = \frac{\omega_0}{2\Gamma} . \]

\(Q\) is a pure number: roughly the number of radians of oscillation (or \(Q/2\pi\) cycles) over which the energy falls by a factor \(e\). Three consequences:

  • The amplitude falls with time constant \(1/\Gamma = 2Q/\omega_0\), the energy with \(Q/\omega_0\).
  • Per cycle the amplitude falls by the factor \(e^{-\Gamma/f_0} = e^{-\pi/Q}\). Half amplitude takes \(Q\ln 2/\pi\) cycles.
  • The frequency is lowered by damping, \(\omega_d/\omega_0 = \sqrt{1 - 1/(4Q^2)}\), by a second-order amount: for \(Q = 18\,400\), by 4 parts in \(10^{10}\). For a good resonator, \(\omega_d = \omega_0\).

Worked number: a copper cell. Chapter 13’s idealised copper pillbox cell at 2856 MHz has \(Q_0 = 18\,400\) (the subscript 0 marks the unloaded \(Q\), with only the walls taking energy). Its field rings down with time constant

\[ \frac{2Q_0}{\omega_0} = \frac{2 \times 18\,400}{2\pi \times 2.856 \times 10^9} = 2.05\ \mu\text{s}, \]

about \(Q_0\ln2/\pi = 4060\) cycles to half amplitude. A niobium cavity at 1.3 GHz and 2 K with \(Q_0 = 10^{10}\) would, with nothing coupled to it, ring with a time constant of 2.45 s, three billion cycles: superconductivity makes the walls almost lossless. (In practice the coupler that feeds power in also lets it out, and sets a loaded \(Q\) near \(3 \times 10^6\); chapter 13 explains.)

5. The driven oscillator: resonance, phase and bandwidth

The picture. Push a swing at the right moment of every swing and the amplitude grows until the friction takes out per cycle what you put in. Push at the wrong rhythm and your pushes fight the swing half the time. An RF cavity is driven by a klystron or a tetrode in the same way, and must be pushed at its own frequency.

The equation and its steady answer. Add a driving force \(F_0\cos\omega t = \mathrm{Re}(F_0e^{i\omega t})\):

\[ \ddot x + 2\Gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2x = \frac{F_0}{m}\cos\omega t . \]

After the start-up has died away (as \(e^{-\Gamma t}\), section 4), the body oscillates at the drive’s frequency \(\omega\), not its own. Try \(x = \mathrm{Re}(Xe^{i\omega t})\): every \(d/dt\) becomes \(i\omega\), so

\[ (-\omega^2 + 2i\Gamma\omega + \omega_0^2)X = \frac{F_0}{m}, \qquad X = \frac{F_0/m}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 + 2i\Gamma\omega} . \]

A differential equation has become a division. The modulus of \(X\) is the amplitude and its argument the phase:

\[ |X| = \frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + 4\Gamma^2\omega^2}}, \qquad \tan\delta = \frac{2\Gamma\omega}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2}, \]

where \(\delta\) is how far the displacement lags behind the force.

Reading the answer. Three regions:

  • Driven slowly (\(\omega \ll \omega_0\)): \(X \approx F_0/(m\omega_0^2) = F_0/k\), the static stretch of the spring. The body follows the force, in step.
  • At resonance (\(\omega = \omega_0\)): \(X = F_0/(2im\Gamma\omega_0)\). The amplitude is \(Q\) times the static stretch, and the factor \(1/i = e^{-i\pi/2}\) means the displacement lags the force by \(90°\). The velocity, a quarter cycle ahead of the displacement, is then exactly in step with the force, and the force does positive work all the time. That is why resonance gives the largest response.
  • Driven fast (\(\omega \gg \omega_0\)): \(X \approx -F_0/(m\omega^2)\). The body barely moves, half a cycle out of step: its inertia dominates.

The resonance curve near the peak. Close to resonance write \(\omega = \omega_0 + \Delta\omega\). Then \(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 \approx -2\omega_0\Delta\omega\) and \(2\Gamma\omega \approx 2\Gamma\omega_0\), so

\[ X \approx \frac{F_0/m}{2\omega_0(-\Delta\omega + i\Gamma)}, \qquad |X|^2 \propto \frac{1}{\Delta\omega^2 + \Gamma^2} . \]

The power absorbed goes as \(|X|^2\) and is a peaked curve called a Lorentzian. It falls to half its peak where \(\Delta\omega = \pm\Gamma\), so the full width at half power is \(2\Gamma = \omega_0/Q\), or in hertz

\[ \Delta f = \frac{f_0}{Q} . \]

A high \(Q\) means a narrow resonance: the oscillator responds strongly, but only to a drive very close to its own frequency. With the detuning parameter \(y = 2Q\,\Delta f/f_0 = \Delta\omega/\Gamma\), the amplitude relative to the peak is \(1/\sqrt{1 + y^2}\), and the extra phase lag is \(\arctan y\).

Worked numbers.

  • Chapter 9’s resonator at 10 MHz with \(Q_0 = 200\) has a bandwidth of 50 kHz. Detuned by 19 kHz, \(y = 2 \times 200 \times 0.0019 = 0.76\): the voltage falls to \(1/\sqrt{1 + 0.76^2} = 0.80\) of its peak, \(37°\) late. (The full formula, not the near-resonance form, gives 0.795 and \(37.2°\).) Chapter 9 shows that 5 per cent too little voltage already loses the beam.
  • The 2856 MHz copper cell of section 4 has a bandwidth of \(2.856 \times 10^9/18\,400 = 155\) kHz; a copper cavity at 200 MHz with \(Q_0 = 50\,000\), only 4 kHz. A temperature change that makes copper expand by a few parts in \(10^5\) moves such a cavity by a large part of its bandwidth, which is why cavities have tuners and cooling water held to a fraction of a degree.

Bandwidth and ring-down are the same fact. The field time constant was \(\tau = 2Q/\omega_0\), and the bandwidth \(\Delta f = f_0/Q\). Multiply: \(\tau\,\Delta f = 1/\pi\). A resonator that rings for a long time can only be driven in a narrow band, and the 155 kHz cell above responds to a change of drive in about 2 µs. Section 16 shows this is a case of a general rule. The driven oscillator is taken further, with the energy absorbed per cycle and the cavity’s response, in chapter 7’s Driven oscillator, resonance and quality factor and its continuation in chapter 9.

6. LC and RLC circuits with phasors

The LC circuit. A charged capacitor \(C\) connected across a coil \(L\) starts to discharge through it. The coil resists the growth of the current, and once the current flows it resists its decay, so the current overshoots and charges the capacitor the other way. Energy passes back and forth between the capacitor’s electric field, \(\tfrac12 CV^2\), and the coil’s magnetic field, \(\tfrac12 LI^2\), as between a spring’s stretch and a mass’s motion. Around the loop the two voltages must cancel: \(L\dot I + Q/C = 0\), and with \(I = \dot Q\),

\[ L\ddot Q + \frac{Q}{C} = 0, \qquad \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} . \]

This is the oscillator of section 3, with charge for displacement, \(L\) for mass and \(1/C\) for stiffness. Worked number: chapter 9’s Wideroe drift tubes present 50 pF, and a coil of 5.07 µH makes them resonate at \(1/(2\pi\sqrt{5.07 \times 10^{-6} \times 50 \times 10^{-12}}) = 10.0\) MHz. For 200 MHz with 10 pF the coil would be only 63 nH, a single loop of wire: at high frequency, the “circuit” becomes a shaped metal box, the cavity, but the mathematics is the same.

Impedance. Drive a circuit at one frequency \(\omega\) and write every voltage and current as a phasor: \(V(t) = \mathrm{Re}(Ve^{i\omega t})\), \(I(t) = \mathrm{Re}(Ie^{i\omega t})\), with \(V\) and \(I\) complex amplitudes. Each element then obeys a complex Ohm’s law, \(V = ZI\), with its impedance \(Z\) (ohms):

  • Resistor: \(V = RI\), so \(Z_R = R\). Voltage and current in step.
  • Inductor: \(V = L\,dI/dt = i\omega L\,I\), so \(Z_L = i\omega L\). The voltage leads the current by \(90°\), and the impedance grows with frequency: a coil blocks fast changes.
  • Capacitor: \(I = C\,dV/dt = i\omega C\,V\), so \(Z_C = 1/(i\omega C) = -i/(\omega C)\). The voltage lags the current by \(90°\), and the impedance falls with frequency: a capacitor passes fast changes and blocks steady current.

Impedances in series add, and in parallel their reciprocals add, exactly like resistances. The imaginary part of \(Z\) is called the reactance: \(\omega L\) for a coil, \(-1/(\omega C)\) for a capacitor.

The series RLC circuit. A source of voltage amplitude \(V\) drives \(R\), \(L\) and \(C\) in series. The total impedance is

\[ Z = R + i\Big(\omega L - \frac{1}{\omega C}\Big) . \]

At \(\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}\) the two reactances cancel, \(Z = R\), and the current \(V/R\) is largest: resonance. The circuit’s quality factor is

\[ Q = \frac{\omega_0L}{R} = \frac{1}{\omega_0CR}, \]

the reactance of either element over the resistance (exercise 5 checks this is the \(Q\) of section 4). At resonance the voltage across the capacitor is \(|Z_C|\,V/R = QV\): the circuit multiplies the source voltage by \(Q\), while the coil’s equal and opposite voltage cancels it around the loop.

Worked numbers. \(L = 1\) µH, \(R = 1\ \Omega\), and \(C = 253.3\) pF for \(f_0 = 10\) MHz. Then \(\omega_0L = 62.8\ \Omega\), \(Q = 62.8\) and the bandwidth is 159 kHz. At 10.5 MHz, \(Z = 1 + 6.13i\) Ω, of modulus 6.21 Ω and angle \(80.7°\): the current is down to \(1/6.21 = 0.16\) of its peak and lags the voltage by nearly a quarter cycle. At 9.5 MHz, \(Z = 1 - 6.45i\) Ω, and the current leads.

Power. The average power into an impedance is \(\tfrac12\mathrm{Re}(VI^*)\): only the part of the current in step with the voltage does work, and the \(\tfrac12\) is the average of \(\cos^2\) (F1 exercise 5). Into a pure coil or capacitor it is zero; they store energy and give it back each cycle.

A cavity as a circuit. Near one of its resonances, an RF cavity behaves like a parallel \(L\), \(C\) and \(R\), where \(R\) (the shunt impedance) stands for the wall losses. Chapter 9 derives \(Q_0 = \omega_0R_pC = R_p/(\omega_0L)\) for it, and chapter 13 measures every cavity by \(R\) and \(Q\). The circuit model is in chapter 4’s RLC circuits and phasors, and the idea of an impedance as the response of a system at each frequency in chapter 8’s Impulse response, transfer functions, impedance.

7. Coupled oscillators, normal modes and beats

The picture. Hang two identical pendulums from a slack string and set one swinging. Its swing slowly dies while the other’s grows, until the first is still and the second swings fully; then the energy flows back. Christiaan Huygens noticed in 1665 that two pendulum clocks on one beam fell into step, through the small motions of the beam. Coupled oscillators are everywhere in an accelerator: the cells of a multi-cell cavity, the horizontal and vertical betatron motions in a ring with a tilted quadrupole, and the bunches of a beam talking to each other through the fields they leave behind.

Two masses and three springs. Two equal masses \(m\), each held by a spring \(k\) to a wall, are joined to each other by a weaker spring \(\kappa\) (N/m). With \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) their displacements, the middle spring is stretched by \(x_2 - x_1\), so

\[ m\ddot x_1 = -kx_1 + \kappa(x_2 - x_1), \qquad m\ddot x_2 = -kx_2 - \kappa(x_2 - x_1) . \]

Each equation contains both coordinates. The trick is to find combinations that move on their own. Add the equations and subtract them:

\[ \begin{aligned} m(\ddot x_1 + \ddot x_2) &= -k(x_1 + x_2), \\ m(\ddot x_1 - \ddot x_2) &= -(k + 2\kappa)(x_1 - x_2) . \end{aligned} \]

The sum \(x_1 + x_2\) oscillates at \(\omega_1 = \sqrt{k/m}\): the masses move together, the middle spring is never stretched, and it does not matter. The difference \(x_1 - x_2\) oscillates at \(\omega_2 = \sqrt{(k + 2\kappa)/m}\): the masses move oppositely, the middle spring is stretched twice over, and the frequency is higher. These two patterns are the normal modes. In each, every part moves at one frequency with a fixed pattern of amplitudes; any motion of the system is a sum of the normal modes. With \(N\) coupled oscillators there are \(N\) normal modes.

Energy passing back and forth. Start with \(x_1 = A\), \(x_2 = 0\), both at rest. That is half of each mode: \(x_1 + x_2 = A\cos\omega_1 t\) and \(x_1 - x_2 = A\cos\omega_2 t\), so

\[ x_1 = \tfrac{A}{2}(\cos\omega_1 t + \cos\omega_2 t), \qquad x_2 = \tfrac{A}{2}(\cos\omega_1 t - \cos\omega_2 t) . \]

By the sum formulas of trigonometry (or by section 2’s exponentials),

\[ x_1 = A\cos\Big(\frac{\omega_2 - \omega_1}{2}t\Big)\cos\Big(\frac{\omega_1 + \omega_2}{2}t\Big), \]

and \(x_2\) is the same with sines. Each mass oscillates at the average frequency inside a slow envelope. The energy is all in mass 2 when \(\tfrac12(\omega_2 - \omega_1)t = \pi/2\), at \(t = \pi/(\omega_2 - \omega_1)\). Worked number: \(m = 0.1\) kg, \(k = 10\) N/m, \(\kappa = 0.5\) N/m: \(\omega_1 = 10.000\) rad/s, \(\omega_2 = 10.488\) rad/s, and the energy moves fully across in 6.4 s, about ten swings. The weaker the coupling, the closer the two frequencies and the slower the exchange.

Beats. The same identity explains beats. Two oscillations of nearly equal frequency added together,

\[ \cos\omega_1 t + \cos\omega_2 t = 2\cos\frac{(\omega_1 - \omega_2)t}{2}\,\cos\frac{(\omega_1 + \omega_2)t}{2}, \]

make a tone at the average frequency whose loudness swells and fades. The envelope’s size \(|\cos|\) repeats twice in each of its own cycles, so the loudness repeats at \(f_1 - f_2\), the beat frequency. Two tuning forks at 440 and 442 Hz beat twice a second; a musician tunes until the beats stop. In RF work the same thing is done on purpose: mixing a 400 MHz signal with a reference at 400.004 MHz (multiplying them, which by the identity read backwards gives sum and difference frequencies) brings the information down to a 4 kHz signal that slow electronics can measure. This is called heterodyning.

Coupled cells. A cavity made of \(N\) coupled cells, such as the nine-cell 1.3 GHz TESLA cavity, has \(N\) modes of its accelerating family at slightly different frequencies, a passband. The one used has neighbouring cells in opposite phase, the \(\pi\) mode. Chapter 13’s Coupled oscillators and normal modes does the \(N\)-cell chain with matrices, and chapter 18 the coupling of horizontal and vertical motion.

8. The wave equation on a string

The picture. Pluck a stretched string near one end. The kink does not stay where it was made; it runs along the string, reflects at the far end and comes back. Each bit of string only moves up and down, but the shape travels. That is a wave: a disturbance that moves through a medium while the medium stays where it is.

Deriving the equation. A string of mass per length \(\mu\) (kg/m) is stretched with tension \(T\) (N) along the \(x\) axis. Its sideways displacement is \(y(x, t)\), and we assume it small: the slope \(\partial y/\partial x\) is much less than 1, so the tension is the same everywhere and the string’s length hardly changes. Look at a short piece between \(x\) and \(x + \Delta x\). The tension pulls along the string at each end. At the right end its upward part is \(T\) times the sine of the string’s angle, which for a small angle is the slope: \(T\,\partial y/\partial x\) at \(x + \Delta x\). At the left end the pull is the other way, \(-T\,\partial y/\partial x\) at \(x\). The net upward force is

\[ T\Big[\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\Big|_{x + \Delta x} - \frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\Big|_x\Big] \approx T\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial x^2}\Delta x . \]

A straight string feels no net force; a curved one is pulled towards its centre of curvature, more where it is more curved. Newton’s law for the piece, mass \(\mu\Delta x\), then gives

\[ \mu\,\Delta x\,\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial t^2} = T\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial x^2}\Delta x, \]

which is the wave equation:

\[ \frac{\partial^2y}{\partial t^2} = v^2\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial x^2}, \qquad v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} . \]

\(v\) has units of \(\sqrt{\text{N}/(\text{kg/m})}\), which is m/s: a speed. A tighter string is faster, a heavier one slower. The approximation fails when the slope is not small: a string plucked hard does not obey the equation exactly, and its waves change shape.

The travelling solutions. Take any function \(f\) of one variable and set \(y = f(x - vt)\). Write \(u = x - vt\). By the chain rule, \(\partial y/\partial x = f'(u)\) and \(\partial y/\partial t = -vf'(u)\), so \(\partial^2y/\partial x^2 = f''(u)\) and \(\partial^2y/\partial t^2 = v^2f''(u)\). The equation holds, whatever the shape \(f\). And \(f(x - vt)\) is the shape \(f(x)\) moved a distance \(vt\) to the right: at time \(t\) the value that was at \(x = 0\) is at \(x = vt\). Likewise \(g(x + vt)\) moves to the left. d’Alembert’s result is that every solution is a sum of the two:

\[ y(x, t) = f(x - vt) + g(x + vt) . \]

Worked number. A steel wire 0.25 mm in diameter (density 7850 kg/m\(^3\)) has \(\mu = 7850 \times \pi \times (0.125 \times 10^{-3})^2 = 3.85 \times 10^{-4}\) kg/m. Under 70 N of tension, its waves travel at \(\sqrt{70/3.85 \times 10^{-4}} = 426\) m/s.

The same equation elsewhere. The wave equation appears wherever a quantity’s acceleration is set by its curvature. Sound in air is one case (the pressure); a transmission line is another, with \(v = 1/\sqrt{L'C'}\) for \(L'\) and \(C'\) the inductance and capacitance per metre; and F4 shows that each component of the electric and magnetic field in empty space obeys it with \(v = c\) (section 13).

9. Sinusoidal waves

The picture. Shake the end of a string up and down steadily. Every point of the string then oscillates at the shaking frequency, each a little later than the one before: a sinusoidal wave,

\[ y(x, t) = A\cos(\omega t - kx + \phi) = \mathrm{Re}\big(Ae^{i\phi}e^{i(\omega t - kx)}\big) . \]

It is \(f(x - vt)\) for a cosine shape, since \(\omega t - kx = -k(x - (\omega/k)t)\).

The names.

  • Amplitude \(A\): the largest displacement.
  • Angular frequency \(\omega\) (rad/s) and frequency \(f = \omega/2\pi\) (Hz): how fast each point oscillates. The period is \(T = 1/f\).
  • Wavenumber \(k\) (rad/m) and wavelength \(\lambda = 2\pi/k\) (m): a snapshot of the string repeats every \(\lambda\). \(k\) is to space what \(\omega\) is to time.
  • Phase \(\omega t - kx + \phi\): where in its cycle each point is. A crest is a place of constant phase.
  • Phase velocity: a crest moves so that \(\omega t - kx\) stays fixed, so \(x\) grows at \(dx/dt = \omega/k\):

\[ v_{ph} = \frac{\omega}{k} = f\lambda . \]

In one period a crest moves one wavelength. For the string, \(v_{ph}\) is \(v = \sqrt{T/\mu}\) at every frequency; section 12 meets waves where it is not.

Worked numbers. Electromagnetic waves in free space travel at \(c = 299\,792\,458\) m/s, so \(\lambda = c/f\): 1.499 m at 200 MHz, 0.749 m at 400 MHz, 0.2306 m at 1.3 GHz and 0.1050 m at 2.856 GHz. These wavelengths set the size of everything RF in an accelerator: a cavity is about half a wavelength across, and a cell of a linac for electrons at \(\beta = 1\) is \(\lambda/2\) or \(\lambda/3\) long.

Riding a wave. An electron linac (chapter 14) sends a wave \(\mathcal{E}_z = \hat{\mathcal{E}}\cos(\omega t - kz)\) along the beam’s direction \(z\). An electron at \(z = vt\) sees the phase \(\omega t - kvt = (\omega - kv)t\). If its speed equals the phase velocity, \(v = \omega/k\), the phase it sees is constant: it sits on one point of the wave and feels a steady force, like a surfer on a breaker. The engineering of chapter 14 is about making \(v_{ph}\) equal \(c\).

The energy a wave carries. Each piece of string has kinetic energy \(\tfrac12\mu\,\Delta x\,(\partial y/\partial t)^2\), and stretched energy \(\tfrac12 T\,\Delta x\,(\partial y/\partial x)^2\) (the work done lengthening it, to the same small-slope approximation). For the wave \(A\cos(\omega t - kx)\) the two are equal, since \(T k^2 = T\omega^2/v^2 = \mu\omega^2\), and each averages to \(\tfrac14\mu\omega^2A^2\) per metre (\(\sin^2\) averages to \(\tfrac12\)). The energy per metre is \(\tfrac12\mu\omega^2A^2\), and it moves at \(v\), so the power passing any point is

\[ P = \tfrac12\mu v\,\omega^2A^2 = \tfrac12 Z\omega^2A^2, \qquad Z = \mu v = \sqrt{T\mu} . \]

\(Z\) (N s/m) is the string’s impedance: the force needed to move its end at unit speed. Section 11 shows it decides reflections. Worked number: the steel wire above, 0.648 m long and vibrating at its fundamental 329 Hz (section 10), carries 0.35 W in each direction for a 1 mm amplitude, since \(Z = 0.164\) N s/m. Note the scaling: power goes as amplitude squared and frequency squared, as for every wave in the course.

10. Superposition, interference and standing waves

Superposition. The wave equation is linear: if \(y_1\) and \(y_2\) are solutions, so is \(y_1 + y_2\). Two waves pass through each other and each carries on as if the other were not there; where they overlap the displacement is the sum. (This is true as long as the small-slope approximation holds. Large waves in water, and the most intense laser light in matter, break it.)

Interference. Two waves of the same frequency arriving at one place add as phasors. Equal amplitudes \(A\) with a phase difference \(\varphi\) give \(A + Ae^{i\varphi}\), of modulus \(2A|\cos(\varphi/2)|\). In step (\(\varphi = 0\)) the amplitude doubles and the intensity, which goes as amplitude squared, quadruples: constructive interference. Half a cycle apart (\(\varphi = \pi\)) they cancel: destructive interference. The energy is not lost; it is redistributed, more in some places and less in others, and the total averages out (section 15).

Standing waves. A wave running right and an equal one running left add to

\[ A\cos(\omega t - kx) + A\cos(\omega t + kx) = 2A\cos kx\,\cos\omega t . \]

The pattern \(\cos kx\) no longer moves; it just swells and shrinks in time. Points where \(\cos kx = 0\), every half wavelength, never move: nodes. Halfway between them are the antinodes, which swing hardest. Since the two travelling waves carry equal power in opposite directions, a standing wave carries no net power: the energy sloshes between kinetic and stretched energy, a quarter cycle apart, as in a single oscillator.

Modes of a string. A string fixed at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\) must have nodes at both ends. The standing wave \(\sin kx\,\cos\omega t\) has a node at 0; it has one at \(L\) only if \(kL = n\pi\) for a whole number \(n\). So only certain wavelengths fit, \(\lambda_n = 2L/n\), and only certain frequencies:

\[ f_n = \frac{v}{\lambda_n} = \frac{nv}{2L}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots \]

These are the string’s modes (its normal modes, as in section 7, now infinitely many because the string has infinitely many parts). \(n = 1\) is the fundamental and the rest are its harmonics. The steel wire at 70 N, 0.648 m long, has \(f_1 = 426/(2 \times 0.648) = 329\) Hz, close to the top string of a guitar, and harmonics at 658, 987 Hz and so on.

Modes of a pipe. Sound in a pipe open at both ends has pressure nodes at the ends, and the same \(f_n = nv/(2L)\). Closed at one end (a pressure antinode there), only odd quarter-wavelengths fit: \(f_n = (2n - 1)v/(4L)\).

The seed of cavity modes. Light between two parallel metal plates behaves like the string: the electric field along a good conductor’s surface must be zero (charges in the metal move to cancel it), so the field parallel to the plates has nodes at both. Modes exist at \(f_n = nc/(2L)\). Plates 52.5 mm apart resonate first at \(c/(2 \times 0.0525) = 2.855\) GHz. A real cavity is closed on all sides, and its lowest accelerating mode, with the field along the axis, is set mainly by its radius rather than its length: for a pillbox of radius \(a\) it is \(f = 2.405c/(2\pi a)\) (the 2.405 comes from a Bessel function, a cousin of the sine for round shapes), which gives 2.854 GHz for \(a = 40.2\) mm, chapter 13’s cell. But the idea is the string’s: walls force nodes, so only waves that fit between them survive, at a discrete set of frequencies. See chapter 7’s Resonant cavity modes.

Phasing a standing-wave linac. A particle at nearly \(c\) crosses a cell \(\lambda/2\) long in half an RF period. If neighbouring cells are in opposite phase (the \(\pi\) mode), the field in the next cell has just reversed as it arrives, and points the right way again. That is why the cells of chapter 13’s 2856 MHz structure are 52.5 mm long.

11. Reflection and impedance matching

The picture. Tie a light string to a heavy rope and send a pulse along the string. At the knot, part of the pulse goes on into the rope, slower and smaller, and part comes back along the string, upside down. Send it the other way, from rope to string, and the reflection comes back the right way up. Whenever a wave meets a change in the medium, some of it reflects. The same happens to an RF signal reaching the end of a cable, or a cavity whose input does not match its feed line.

Working it out. Strings 1 and 2 (same tension \(T\), different \(\mu\)) are joined at \(x = 0\). At frequency \(\omega\) they have wavenumbers \(k_1 = \omega/v_1\) and \(k_2 = \omega/v_2\). Let an incident wave \(Ae^{i(\omega t - k_1x)}\) arrive from the left; there will be a reflected wave \(Be^{i(\omega t + k_1x)}\) on the left and a transmitted one \(Ce^{i(\omega t - k_2x)}\) on the right. Two conditions hold at the knot:

  • The strings are joined, so the displacement is the same on both sides: \(A + B = C\).
  • The knot has no mass, so the sideways forces on it must balance (any net force would give it infinite acceleration). The force is \(T\,\partial y/\partial x\), so the slopes match: \(-ik_1(A - B) = -ik_2C\).

Solve: \(B/A = (k_1 - k_2)/(k_1 + k_2)\) and \(C/A = 2k_1/(k_1 + k_2)\). Since \(Z = \sqrt{T\mu} = Tk/\omega\) is proportional to \(k\) at fixed \(T\) and \(\omega\),

\[ r = \frac{B}{A} = \frac{Z_1 - Z_2}{Z_1 + Z_2}, \qquad t = \frac{C}{A} = \frac{2Z_1}{Z_1 + Z_2} . \]

From light string into heavy rope, \(Z_2 > Z_1\) and \(r\) is negative: the reflection is inverted. From heavy to light, \(r > 0\). Into a wall (\(Z_2 \to \infty\)), \(r = -1\): total reflection, inverted, which is why a fixed end is a node. Into a free end (\(Z_2 = 0\)), \(r = +1\).

Energy. With \(P = \tfrac12 Z\omega^2|A|^2\) from section 9, the reflected fraction of the power is \(r^2\) and the transmitted fraction \((Z_2/Z_1)t^2\). For \(Z_2 = 2Z_1\): \(r = -1/3\), \(t = 2/3\), and the power splits \(1/9\) reflected and \(2 \times 4/9 = 8/9\) transmitted, adding to 1 as it must.

Matching. If \(Z_2 = Z_1\), \(r = 0\): the wave passes the join without noticing it. Nothing is reflected when the impedances match, whatever else differs. That is the general rule for every wave in the course, and engineers spend much effort on it.

Transmission lines. An RF cable or waveguide has a characteristic impedance \(Z_0\), the ratio of voltage to current in a wave travelling along it: 50 Ω for most laboratory cable. A load \(Z_L\) at its end (an antenna, a cavity, a resistor) reflects the fraction

\[ \Gamma = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0} \]

of the incident voltage. It is the string’s formula with the sign reversed, because on the line the voltage plays the part of the string’s force and the current the part of its velocity; \(r\) above was for the displacement, like the current. A 75 Ω load on a 50 Ω cable gives \(\Gamma = 0.2\), and reflects \(\Gamma^2 = 4\) per cent of the power. A short circuit (\(Z_L = 0\)) gives \(\Gamma = -1\) and an open end \(+1\): everything comes back. The reflected and incident waves together make a partial standing wave on the line, whose largest and smallest voltages are in the ratio \((1 + |\Gamma|)/(1 - |\Gamma|)\), the voltage standing-wave ratio, 1.5 here: it is what an engineer measures to judge a match.

The match of a klystron’s waveguide to a cavity, and why the power reflected from an unmatched cavity can destroy the klystron unless a circulator diverts it, are in chapter 13 (via chapter 8’s Transmission lines, matching, S-parameters). F1 section 5 met the same idea from the other side: a source delivers most power to a load equal to its own internal resistance.

12. Dispersion: phase and group velocity

The picture. On a string, waves of every frequency travel at the same speed, so a pulse, which is a sum of many frequencies (section 16), keeps its shape. In many media this is not so: waves of different frequencies travel at different speeds, and the medium is called dispersive (a prism disperses white light into colours because red and blue travel through glass at different speeds). Then the relation between \(\omega\) and \(k\), the dispersion relation, is no longer \(\omega = vk\), and two speeds must be told apart.

Group velocity from beats. Add two waves of equal amplitude and slightly different frequency and wavenumber, \((\omega_1, k_1)\) and \((\omega_2, k_2)\). By the beat identity of section 7,

\[ \cos(\omega_1 t - k_1x) + \cos(\omega_2 t - k_2x) = 2\cos\Big(\frac{\Delta\omega}{2}t - \frac{\Delta k}{2}x\Big)\cos(\bar\omega t - \bar kx), \]

with \(\Delta\omega = \omega_1 - \omega_2\), \(\Delta k = k_1 - k_2\), and bars for averages. The fast wave inside moves at \(\bar\omega/\bar k\), the phase velocity. The envelope, the groups of waves, moves at \(\Delta\omega/\Delta k\). In the limit of close frequencies this is the group velocity:

\[ v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk} . \]

Energy and information travel with the envelope, at \(v_g\); the crests slide through it at \(v_{ph}\). When \(\omega = vk\), both are \(v\) and there is no difference.

The waveguide example. Section 10’s waves between two plates bounce back and forth across a hollow metal pipe as they travel along it. Chapter 13 shows that in a rectangular guide of broad width \(a_w\), the lowest mode has the dispersion relation

\[ \omega^2 = \omega_c^2 + c^2k^2, \qquad \omega_c = \frac{\pi c}{a_w}, \]

where \(k\) is the wavenumber along the guide and \(\omega_c = 2\pi f_c\) the cutoff frequency. (The picture: the wave’s total wavenumber \(\omega/c\) is split between the across-guide part, fixed at \(\pi/a_w\) by the walls, and the along-guide part \(k\), by Pythagoras.) Then

\[ v_{ph} = \frac{\omega}{k} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{1 - (f_c/f)^2}}, \qquad v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk} = \frac{c^2k}{\omega} = c\sqrt{1 - (f_c/f)^2} . \]

(For \(v_g\), differentiate \(\omega^2 = \omega_c^2 + c^2k^2\): \(2\omega\,d\omega = 2c^2k\,dk\).) The phase velocity is above \(c\), and the group velocity below; their product is exactly \(c^2\). The phase velocity breaking the speed of light breaks no law: no energy or signal moves at it. The guide wavelength \(\lambda_g = 2\pi/k = \lambda/\sqrt{1 - (f_c/f)^2}\) is longer than in free space.

Worked numbers: WR-284 at 2856 MHz. The broad width is 72.14 mm, so \(f_c = c/(2a_w) = 2.078\) GHz. At 2856 MHz, \(\sqrt{1 - (2.078/2.856)^2} = 0.686\), so \(v_g = 0.686c\), \(v_{ph} = 1.458c\), and \(\lambda_g = 105.0/0.686 = 153.0\) mm. A numerical check, \((\omega_2 - \omega_1)/(k_2 - k_1)\) between 2855 and 2857 MHz, gives \(0.68607c\), the same to five figures.

Below cutoff. For \(\omega < \omega_c\), \(k^2 = (\omega^2 - \omega_c^2)/c^2\) is negative, so \(k\) is imaginary, \(k = -i\kappa\) with \(\kappa = \sqrt{\omega_c^2 - \omega^2}/c\). The wave \(e^{i(\omega t - kx)}\) becomes \(e^{-\kappa x}e^{i\omega t}\): it does not travel at all, but dies away exponentially into the guide. Such a wave is evanescent. A 1.5 GHz signal in WR-284 falls as \(e^{-x/(33.2\ \text{mm})}\), by a factor 1000 in 23 cm. This is how a beam pipe, too narrow to carry an accelerating cavity’s frequency, keeps the cavity’s field from leaking along it.

Other dispersive waves. Electromagnetic waves in an ionised gas obey the same relation with \(\omega_c\) replaced by the plasma frequency \(\omega_p\) (chapter 7’s Waves in plasmas, and chapter 27’s plasma accelerators); a chain of coupled cavity cells has a passband with a curved \(\omega(k)\) (chapter 14). Chapter 13’s Waveguides derives the guide’s modes from Maxwell’s equations.

13. Electromagnetic waves

From F4. In empty space, with no charges or currents, Maxwell’s equations combine (in F4, using the vector calculus of F2) into the wave equation for each component of the electric field \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) and the magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\), with speed

\[ c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}} = 299\,792\,458\ \text{m/s} . \]

A changing magnetic field makes an electric field, and a changing electric field makes a magnetic field; each keeps the other going, and the pair moves off at \(c\). In 1865 Maxwell noticed that this speed, computed from laboratory measurements of electricity and magnetism, matched the measured speed of light, and concluded that light is such a wave.

A plane wave. The simplest solution travelling along \(z\):

\[ \mathcal{E}_x = \mathcal{E}_0\cos(\omega t - kz), \qquad B_y = \frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{c}\cos(\omega t - kz), \]

with \(\omega = ck\). Three facts, all from Maxwell’s equations: the fields are transverse (perpendicular to the direction of travel); \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are perpendicular to each other and in step, with the direction of travel along \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} \times \mathbf{B}\); and \(B = \mathcal{E}/c\).

Intensity. The power per unit area carried by the wave, averaged over a cycle, is

\[ I = \frac{\mathcal{E}_0^2}{2\eta_0} = \tfrac12 c\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}_0^2, \qquad \eta_0 = \sqrt{\frac{\mu_0}{\varepsilon_0}} = 376.7\ \Omega . \]

(The derivation, from the energy densities \(\tfrac12\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}^2\) and \(B^2/2\mu_0\) moving at \(c\), is chapter 13’s Poynting’s theorem.) \(\eta_0\), the impedance of free space, is the ratio \(\mathcal{E}/H\) in the wave, with \(H = B/\mu_0\): the wave in space has an impedance like a cable’s, and an antenna is a device for matching a line to it. Worked number: sunlight above the atmosphere brings 1361 W/m\(^2\). Then \(\mathcal{E}_0 = \sqrt{2 \times 376.7 \times 1361} = 1013\) V/m and \(B_0 = 3.4\) µT. Compare the 30 MV/m on the axis of a copper accelerating cell: an accelerator’s fields are confined and resonant, and vastly stronger than light’s.

Polarisation. The direction of \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) is the wave’s polarisation. Above it is fixed along \(x\): linear polarisation. Add a second wave polarised along \(y\), a quarter cycle behind:

\[ \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = \mathcal{E}_0\big(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\cos(\omega t - kz) + \hat{\mathbf{y}}\sin(\omega t - kz)\big) . \]

At any fixed \(z\) the field arrow keeps its length and turns round once per period: circular polarisation. A planar undulator, whose electrons wiggle in one plane, gives linearly polarised light; a helical one, whose electrons spiral, gives circular. Any polarisation is a sum of two linear ones with some phase between them, which complex amplitudes \(\mathcal{E}_x\) and \(\mathcal{E}_y\) describe at once.

The spectrum. Electromagnetic waves are named by frequency; physics is the same throughout. F7 explains that light also comes in photons of energy \(E = hf\), with Planck’s constant \(h = 4.136 \times 10^{-15}\) eV s, or \(E\,[\text{eV}] = 1239.84/\lambda\,[\text{nm}]\).

14. The Doppler effect

The picture. An ambulance’s siren sounds higher as it comes towards you and lower as it goes away. A source that moves towards you emits each crest from a little closer than the last, so the crests arrive bunched: shorter wavelength, higher frequency.

Moving source. A source emits sound of frequency \(f\) (period \(1/f\)) while moving towards a listener at speed \(u\); sound travels at \(v\) through still air. Between one crest and the next, the first crest travels \(v/f\) but the source follows it by \(u/f\), so the crests are spaced \(\lambda' = (v - u)/f\) apart. They arrive at speed \(v\), so

\[ f' = \frac{v}{\lambda'} = \frac{f}{1 - u/v} . \]

Moving listener. A listener running at \(u\) towards a still source meets crests at the relative speed \(v + u\), so \(f' = f(1 + u/v)\).

For \(u \ll v\) the two formulas agree to first order (since \(1/(1 - x) \approx 1 + x\), F1): \(f' \approx f(1 + u/v)\), a fractional shift \(u/v\), upwards for approach and downwards for recession. For a source at 30 m/s and \(v = 343\) m/s, a 1000 Hz tone becomes 1096 Hz from a moving source and 1087 Hz for a moving listener: for sound, who is moving matters, because the air is a reference.

Light. For light there is no air; only the relative speed can matter, and the exact formula needs relativity (chapter 1): for approach at \(\beta = u/c\), \(f' = f\sqrt{(1 + \beta)/(1 - \beta)}\). To first order in \(\beta\) it is again \(f(1 + \beta)\). If the motion is at an angle \(\theta\) to the line of sight, only the part along it counts: the shift is \(\beta\cos\theta\) to first order.

Worked number. A beam of hydrogen atoms at 100 keV (\(\beta = \sqrt{2K/mc^2} = \sqrt{0.2/938.8} = 0.0146\)) emits the red Balmer line, 656.28 nm at rest. Seen head on as it approaches, the first-order formula gives \(656.28 \times (1 - 0.0146) = 646.70\) nm, and the exact one 646.77 nm; seen at \(45°\), 649.51 nm. Measuring this shift is a standard way to find the speed of a neutral beam. The first-order formula is off by \(\beta^2/2\), 0.07 nm here.

Where first order fails. For an electron of 3 GeV (\(\gamma = 5871\)) in an undulator, the first-order picture is useless. Chapter 20 shows that the undulator’s period of 18 mm comes out as light of 0.26 nm, shortened by \(2\gamma^2\): a factor of about \(2\gamma\) from the Doppler effect and \(\gamma\) from relativity’s contraction of the undulator. See chapter 20’s Lorentz transformations, continued.

15. Interference and diffraction

Two slits. Light of wavelength \(\lambda\) falls on two narrow slits a distance \(d\) apart. Each slit sends out waves in every direction. At an angle \(\theta\), the wave from one slit has travelled \(d\sin\theta\) further than the other’s. They arrive in step, and make a bright band, when the extra path is a whole number of wavelengths:

\[ d\sin\theta = m\lambda, \qquad m = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \dots \]

Halfway between, they cancel. On a screen a distance \(L\) away, for small angles, the bright fringes are spaced \(\lambda L/d\). Worked number: green laser light (532 nm) through slits 0.1 mm apart gives fringes 5.32 mm apart on a screen 1 m away. Measuring fringes this size is how Young found that light’s wavelength is about half a micrometre.

Diffraction. A wave passing a single opening of width \(a\) spreads. Each part of the opening acts as a source, and at the angle where \(a\sin\theta = \lambda\) the parts pair off and cancel, so the beam spreads over an angle of about \(\lambda/a\). For a round hole of diameter \(D\) the first dark ring is at \(\sin\theta = 1.22\lambda/D\). A wave squeezed through a narrow opening spreads widely; through a wide one, hardly at all.

Resolution. The same fact limits every instrument that sees with waves. A lens of diameter \(D\) blurs a point into a spot of angular size about \(\lambda/D\), and a microscope cannot separate details much smaller than the wavelength it uses, about 0.2 µm for visible light. To see smaller things, use shorter waves. X-rays of 12.4 keV have \(\lambda = 0.1\) nm, the spacing of atoms in a crystal, which is why synchrotron light sources are used to find the structure of proteins (chapter 20). To see inside a nucleus, a few femtometres across, the probe needs a wavelength of femtometres: a particle with de Broglie wavelength \(\lambda = h/p\), so that \(pc = hc/\lambda = 1240\) MeV fm\(/\lambda\). A 1 fm wavelength needs \(pc \approx 1.24\) GeV. This is the argument of chapter 1: to see small things, accelerate.

16. Fourier’s idea

The claim. Any periodic signal of period \(T_0\), however jagged, can be written as a sum of sines and cosines at the frequencies \(f_0 = 1/T_0\), \(2f_0\), \(3f_0\), …: its harmonics. A smooth signal needs only a few; a signal with sharp corners needs many.

A first example: a square wave. A signal that is \(+1\) for half of each period and \(-1\) for the other half is

\[ s(t) = \frac{4}{\pi}\Big(\sin\omega_0t + \frac{\sin 3\omega_0t}{3} + \frac{\sin 5\omega_0t}{5} + \dots\Big) . \]

Only odd harmonics appear (the shape is the same upside down half a period later), with amplitudes \(4/(\pi n)\): 1.273, 0.424, 0.255, 0.182 for \(n = 1, 3, 5, 7\). At the middle of the positive half (\(\omega_0t = \pi/2\)), the sums of one, two and three terms are 1.273, 0.849 and 1.103, and of 100 terms 0.997: closing in on 1. Near the jumps the partial sums always overshoot by about 9 per cent however many terms are taken (the Gibbs phenomenon), but the overshoot gets narrower. How the coefficients are found, by multiplying by each sine and averaging, is in chapter 8’s Fourier series and transforms.

Why it matters. For a linear system (a circuit, a cavity, a string), the response to a sum is the sum of the responses. So find the response to each sinusoid separately (a complex division, as in section 5) and add. Fourier analysis plus phasors is how every RF system, every beam pickup and every impedance calculation of the course is done.

Pulses contain a band of frequencies. A signal that is not periodic, such as a single pulse, needs a continuous band of frequencies instead of a ladder of harmonics. The rule of thumb: a pulse lasting \(\Delta t\) contains frequencies spread over

\[ \Delta f \sim \frac{1}{\Delta t} . \]

The exact number depends on how the widths are defined; for a Gaussian pulse with rms length \(\sigma_t\) the rms spread is \(\sigma_f = 1/(2\pi\sigma_t)\). Three consequences in the course:

  • A 1 µs pulse of 2856 MHz RF is not one frequency but a band about 1 MHz wide around it.
  • A resonator of bandwidth \(\Delta f\) cannot follow changes faster than about \(1/\Delta f\): section 5 found \(\tau\,\Delta f = 1/\pi\) exactly. The 155 kHz copper cell responds in 2 µs; the TESLA cavity, with a loaded bandwidth of about 400 Hz, in about a millisecond.
  • A bunch of particles 1 ps long (0.3 mm at \(c\)) has a current containing frequencies up to about \(1/(2\pi \times 1\ \text{ps}) = 159\) GHz. Short bunches excite fields at high frequencies in every pipe and cavity they pass (chapter 22) and radiate coherently at long wavelengths (chapter 26).

And the converse: a beam going round a ring repeats every turn, so its signal at a pickup contains only harmonics of the revolution frequency, 11.245 kHz in the LHC’s 26.7 km. A spectrum analyser on a ring pickup shows lines at these harmonics, with betatron sidebands beside them at the fractional tune times the revolution frequency, which is how tunes are measured.

  1. For \(z = 1 + i\sqrt3\), find \(|z|\), \(\arg z\), \(z^3\) and \(1/z\). Do \(z^3\) twice: by expanding the brackets and by polar form.
Answer

\(|z| = \sqrt{1 + 3} = 2\) and \(\arg z = \arctan\sqrt3 = 60°\), so \(z = 2e^{i\pi/3}\). Polar form: \(z^3 = 8e^{i\pi} = -8\). Expanding: \(z^2 = 1 + 2i\sqrt3 - 3 = -2 + 2i\sqrt3\), and \(z^3 = (-2 + 2i\sqrt3)(1 + i\sqrt3) = -2 - 2i\sqrt3 + 2i\sqrt3 - 6 = -8\). Finally \(1/z = z^*/|z|^2 = (1 - i\sqrt3)/4 = 0.25 - 0.433i\), which is \(\tfrac12e^{-i\pi/3}\): modulus inverted, angle reversed.

  1. Two RF cells each give a particle a peak of 1 MV, but the particle reaches the second \(40°\) later in its RF cycle than the first. What is the most it can gain from the pair? At what phase difference would the pair give no more than one cell alone?
Answer

The phasor sum is \(1 + e^{i\,40°}\), of modulus \(2\cos 20° = 1.879\) MV (section 10’s \(2A|\cos(\varphi/2)|\)). The pair gives only 1 MV when \(2\cos(\varphi/2) = 1\), at \(\varphi = 120°\). At \(180°\) the two cancel.

  1. A TESLA cavity at 1.3 GHz is run with a loaded \(Q_L = 3.13 \times 10^6\). Find its bandwidth, the time constant with which its field rings down when the RF stops, and how many RF cycles that is. How long until the field is 1 per cent of its starting value?
Answer

\(\Delta f = f_0/Q_L = 1.3 \times 10^9/3.13 \times 10^6 = 415\) Hz. \(\tau = 2Q_L/\omega_0 = 2 \times 3.13 \times 10^6/(2\pi \times 1.3 \times 10^9) = 766\) µs, about \(10^6\) cycles. The field falls to 1 per cent after \(\tau\ln 100 = 3.53\) ms. Check: \(\tau\,\Delta f = 766 \times 10^{-6} \times 415 = 0.318 = 1/\pi\).

  1. A copper cavity at 200 MHz has \(Q_0 = 50\,000\). What is its bandwidth? If it drifts 1 kHz off the drive frequency, by what factor does its voltage fall for the same drive, and how far does its phase move? How much more power is then needed to restore the voltage?
Answer

\(\Delta f = 200 \times 10^6/50\,000 = 4\) kHz. \(y = 2Q\,\Delta f/f_0 = 2 \times 50\,000 \times 1000/(2 \times 10^8) = 0.5\), so the voltage falls to \(1/\sqrt{1.25} = 0.894\) and the phase moves \(\arctan 0.5 = 26.6°\). The power goes as voltage squared, so restoring the voltage needs \(1 + y^2 = 1.25\) times the power: 25 per cent more, from a 1 kHz drift, a quarter of the bandwidth.

    1. For a series \(RLC\) circuit, show that \(\omega_0L/R\) equals \(\omega_0 U/P\), with \(U\) the stored energy and \(P\) the average power lost at resonance. (b) With \(L = 1\) µH, \(C = 253.3\) pF and \(R = 1\ \Omega\) driven by 1 V amplitude, find the current at 10 MHz and at 10.5 MHz, and the voltage across the capacitor at 10 MHz.
Answer
  1. At resonance the current amplitude is \(I_0\). The energy sloshes between coil and capacitor, and when the current peaks it is all in the coil: \(U = \tfrac12 LI_0^2\). The average power in \(R\) is \(\tfrac12 I_0^2R\). So \(\omega_0U/P = \omega_0L/R\). (b) At 10 MHz, \(Z = R = 1\ \Omega\) and \(I = 1\) A. At 10.5 MHz, \(Z = 1 + 6.13i\) Ω, \(|Z| = 6.21\ \Omega\) and \(I = 0.161\) A, lagging by \(80.7°\). At 10 MHz the capacitor’s voltage is \(I/(\omega_0C) = 1 \times 62.8 = 62.8\) V, \(Q\) times the source.

Answer

\(\omega_1 = \sqrt{10/0.1} = 10.000\) rad/s (masses together) and \(\omega_2 = \sqrt{11/0.1} = 10.488\) rad/s (masses opposed): 1.592 and 1.669 Hz.   1. A steel wire with \(\mu = 3.85 \times 10^{-4}\) kg/m and length 0.648 m is under 70 N of tension. Find the wave speed, the fundamental frequency and the third harmonic. What tension would tune the fundamental to 329.63 Hz?

  1. A neutral hydrogen beam is viewed at \(45°\) to its direction, approaching the spectrometer. The Balmer line, 656.28 nm at rest, is seen at 650.00 nm. To first order, what is the beam’s speed and its kinetic energy per atom (\(mc^2 = 938.8\) MeV)? About how big an error does first order make?
Answer

First order: \(\lambda' = \lambda(1 - \beta\cos 45°)\), so \(\beta = (1 - 650.00/656.28)/0.7071 = 0.01353\), \(v = 4.06 \times 10^6\) m/s, and \(K = \tfrac12 mc^2\beta^2 = 0.5 \times 938.8 \times 0.01353^2\) MeV \(= 86\) keV.
