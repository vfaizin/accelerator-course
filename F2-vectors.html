F2 Vectors and vector calculus

1843 to 1893: Hamilton’s quaternions, Grassmann’s extension, and the vector analysis of Gibbs and Heaviside. Quantities with a direction, fields that fill space, and the calculus of fields: gradient, divergence and curl, and the two theorems that join them to flux and circulation. The language in which Maxwell’s equations, and every accelerator magnet, are written.

What this chapter assumes. High-school mathematics and physics (Pythagoras’s theorem, coordinates, trigonometry), and F1 Calculus: derivatives and their rules, integrals and the fundamental theorem, partial derivatives, and Taylor series to first order.

What comes next. F4 Electricity and magnetism turns the theorems of sections 9 to 11 into Maxwell’s equations. Chapter 1’s pre-requisite on Gauss’s law and chapter 2’s on Laplace and Poisson equations use them at once.

In one page

The problem. A temperature or a voltage is one number at each point. A velocity, a force or an electric field is not: it has a size and a direction, and in three dimensions it needs three numbers. Writing every law three times, once for each direction, is slow and hides what the law says. Worse, the laws of electricity and magnetism are about how fields spread out from charges and circulate round currents, and ordinary calculus has no words for that.

The idea. Treat a quantity with a direction as one object, a vector, with its own algebra: adding, scaling, and two kinds of product, the dot product (how much two vectors point the same way) and the cross product (a vector at right angles to both). Then do calculus with vectors and with fields, quantities that have a value at every point of space. Three derivatives of a field matter. The gradient of a scalar field points uphill. The divergence of a vector field measures how much it spreads out from a point. The curl measures how much it swirls round a point. Two theorems, Gauss’s and Stokes’s, say that adding up divergence inside a volume gives the flux out through its surface, and adding up curl over a surface gives the circulation round its edge. Each is F1’s fundamental theorem of calculus, in three dimensions.

Equations to remember.

  • Components: \(\mathbf{A} = A_x\hat{\mathbf{x}} + A_y\hat{\mathbf{y}} + A_z\hat{\mathbf{z}}\), with size \(|\mathbf{A}| = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2 + A_z^2}\).
  • Dot product: \(\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B} = |\mathbf{A}||\mathbf{B}|\cos\theta = A_xB_x + A_yB_y + A_zB_z\). Work: \(W = \int\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{l}\).
  • Cross product: \(|\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}| = |\mathbf{A}||\mathbf{B}|\sin\theta\), at right angles to both by the right-hand rule. Magnetic force: \(\mathbf{F} = q\,\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\).
  • Circular motion: acceleration \(v^2/R\) towards the centre.
  • Gradient: \(\nabla f = \left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}, \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right)\); field from potential: \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\nabla V\).
  • Divergence: \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = \dfrac{\partial F_x}{\partial x} + \dfrac{\partial F_y}{\partial y} + \dfrac{\partial F_z}{\partial z}\); Gauss’s theorem: \(\displaystyle\oint_S\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{A} = \int_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\,dV\).
  • Curl: \(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}\), components in section 11; Stokes’s theorem: \(\displaystyle\oint_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \int_S(\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot d\mathbf{A}\).
  • Two identities: \(\nabla\times(\nabla f) = 0\) and \(\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}) = 0\).
  • Laplacian: \(\nabla^2 f = \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} + \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2} + \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial z^2}\).

Numbers worth knowing.

  • An electron from 2 kV (\(v = 2.65 \times 10^7\) m/s) in the Earth’s field of 50 μT feels a force of \(2.1 \times 10^{-16}\) N at right angles to its motion and curves on a circle of radius 3.0 m.
  • A proton in a field of 1 T goes round at 15.2 MHz, whatever its speed (while it is slow compared with light): the cyclotron frequency of chapter 10.
  • A sphere of radius 1 m at 1 MV holds a charge of \(1.11 \times 10^{-4}\) C and has a field of 1 MV/m at its surface.
  • A current of 1 A in a long straight wire makes a magnetic field of 20 μT at 1 cm, less than the Earth’s.
  • An electron beam of 10 mA at 2 kV, 1 mm in radius, has a field of 6.8 kV/m at its edge from its own charge: the space charge of chapter 2 and chapter 22.

The problem it solved

Numbers that point

Forces had been added by the parallelogram rule since Simon Stevin around 1586 and Newton’s Principia of 1687: put two arrows head to tail and the sum is the arrow from the first tail to the last head. But algebra with directed quantities had no general method. In 1843 William Rowan Hamilton, walking along the Royal Canal in Dublin, found the rule for multiplying quaternions, numbers with one ordinary part and three directed parts, and cut it into the stone of Brougham Bridge. The year after, Hermann Grassmann published a broader algebra of directed quantities in any number of dimensions, which almost nobody read for decades.

Maxwell, and a quarrel

James Clerk Maxwell’s Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism of 1873 wrote its equations mostly component by component, about twenty of them, with some quaternion notation alongside. In the 1880s Josiah Willard Gibbs at Yale and Oliver Heaviside in England, independently, cut quaternions down to what physics needed: the vector, the dot product and the cross product, and the operator \(\nabla\) for derivatives. Gibbs printed his Elements of Vector Analysis privately for his students between 1881 and 1884; Heaviside set out his in his articles and in Electromagnetic Theory (volume 1, 1893), and wrote Maxwell’s equations as the four used today. The defenders of quaternions, led by Peter Guthrie Tait, fought the new notation through the 1890s, and lost.

The two theorems

The theorem relating divergence to flux was found by Lagrange in 1762, by Gauss in 1813 and by Mikhail Ostrogradsky, who gave a general proof in 1826. The theorem relating curl to circulation appeared in a letter from William Thomson (later Lord Kelvin) to George Gabriel Stokes in 1850; Stokes set it as a question in the Cambridge Smith’s Prize examination of 1854, and it has carried his name since.

The mathematics

0. What this chapter assumes, and a quick check

  • Pythagoras. A right-angled triangle with sides \(a\) and \(b\) has hypotenuse \(\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\).
  • Coordinates. A point in space is \((x, y, z)\): distances along three perpendicular axes from an origin. The axes are right-handed: point the fingers of the right hand along \(x\), curl them towards \(y\), and the thumb points along \(z\).
  • From F1. Derivatives, the chain rule, integrals, the fundamental theorem, and partial derivatives such as \(\partial f/\partial x\) (the slope in \(x\) with \(y\) and \(z\) held fixed).

Check yourself. (a) How far is the point \((1, 2, 2)\) from the origin? (b) For \(f(x, y) = x^2y\), find \(\partial f/\partial x\) and \(\partial f/\partial y\). (c) \(\int_0^R 2\pi r\,dr\). Answers: (a) 3; (b) \(2xy\) and \(x^2\); (c) \(\pi R^2\), the area of a circle (section 8 shows why).

1. Vectors and their components

The picture. A vector is an arrow: a size (its magnitude) and a direction, with no fixed starting point. A displacement of 3 m east is the same vector wherever it starts. Velocity, acceleration, force, momentum and the electric and magnetic fields are vectors. Quantities with only a size, like mass, energy, temperature and voltage, are scalars. Vectors are printed in bold, \(\mathbf{v}\); by hand, with an arrow on top, \(\vec v\). The magnitude is \(|\mathbf{v}|\), or just \(v\).

Adding and scaling. To add two vectors, put them head to tail; the sum runs from the first tail to the second head. Order does not matter: \(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{B} + \mathbf{A}\). Multiplying by a number \(c\) stretches the arrow by \(c\), and reverses it if \(c < 0\). So \(\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{A} + (-1)\mathbf{B}\).

Components. Choose three unit vectors, of length 1, along the axes: \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\), \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\), \(\hat{\mathbf{z}}\) (also written \(\mathbf{i}\), \(\mathbf{j}\), \(\mathbf{k}\)). Any vector is a sum of steps along them:

\[ \mathbf{A} = A_x\hat{\mathbf{x}} + A_y\hat{\mathbf{y}} + A_z\hat{\mathbf{z}} = (A_x, A_y, A_z) . \]

The numbers \(A_x\), \(A_y\), \(A_z\) are the components. Adding vectors adds components, and scaling scales them: \(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B} = (A_x + B_x, A_y + B_y, A_z + B_z)\). By Pythagoras, used twice,

\[ |\mathbf{A}| = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2 + A_z^2} . \]

A unit vector in the direction of \(\mathbf{A}\) is \(\hat{\mathbf{A}} = \mathbf{A}/|\mathbf{A}|\). The position vector of a point is the arrow from the origin to it, \(\mathbf{r} = (x, y, z)\), with length \(r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}\).

Example: leaving the deflecting plates. In chapter 2, a 2 kV electron moves along \(z\) at \(v_z = 2.65 \times 10^7\) m/s between plates 2 cm long and 5 mm apart, with 100 V across them. It leaves with a sideways velocity \(v_x\) that is 0.1 of \(v_z\) (chapter 2 derives the factor \(lV_d/(2dV_a) = 0.02 \times 100/(2 \times 0.005 \times 2000) = 0.1\)). Its velocity is \(\mathbf{v} = (2.65 \times 10^6, 0, 2.65 \times 10^7)\) m/s, and it is turned through the angle whose tangent is \(v_x/v_z = 0.1\), \(5.71°\).

2. The dot product

The definition. The dot product of two vectors is a number:

\[ \mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B} = |\mathbf{A}|\,|\mathbf{B}|\cos\theta, \]

where \(\theta\) is the angle between them. It is the length of \(\mathbf{A}\) times the part of \(\mathbf{B}\) that lies along \(\mathbf{A}\). Two vectors at right angles have \(\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0\); parallel vectors have the product of their lengths; and \(\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{A} = |\mathbf{A}|^2\).

In components. Since the unit vectors are perpendicular and of length 1, \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{x}} = 1\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{y}} = 0\), and so on. Multiplying out the components term by term leaves only the matching pairs:

\[ \mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B} = A_xB_x + A_yB_y + A_zB_z . \]

That the two forms agree is the cosine rule of trigonometry: \(|\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{B}|^2 = |\mathbf{A}|^2 + |\mathbf{B}|^2 - 2|\mathbf{A}||\mathbf{B}|\cos\theta\), and expanding the left side in components gives \(|\mathbf{A}|^2 + |\mathbf{B}|^2 - 2(A_xB_x + A_yB_y + A_zB_z)\).

Work. A force \(\mathbf{F}\) acting on a body that moves a displacement \(\mathbf{d}\) does work \(W = \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{d}\): only the part of the force along the motion counts. A force of \((3, 0, 4)\) N moving its point of action by \((2, 1, 0)\) m does \(3 \times 2 + 0 \times 1 + 4 \times 0 = 6\) J of work, although the force is 5 N and the displacement \(\sqrt5 = 2.24\) m; the angle between them is \(57.5°\).

A force that does no work. Section 3 shows that the magnetic force on a moving charge is always at right angles to its velocity. Then \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0\): the force does no work, and a magnetic field can bend a particle but never change its energy. That single fact is why accelerators use electric fields to accelerate and magnetic fields to steer (chapter 1).

3. The cross product

The definition. The cross product of two vectors is a vector, \(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}\), with

  • magnitude \(|\mathbf{A}||\mathbf{B}|\sin\theta\), the area of the parallelogram the two vectors span;
  • direction at right angles to both, given by the right-hand rule: fingers along \(\mathbf{A}\), curled towards \(\mathbf{B}\), thumb along \(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}\).

Parallel vectors have zero cross product. Reversing the order reverses the result: \(\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{A} = -\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}\).

In components. From \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\times\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \hat{\mathbf{z}}\), \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\times\hat{\mathbf{z}} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}\), \(\hat{\mathbf{z}}\times\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \hat{\mathbf{y}}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\times\hat{\mathbf{x}} = 0\), multiplying out gives

\[ \mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B} = (A_yB_z - A_zB_y,\ A_zB_x - A_xB_z,\ A_xB_y - A_yB_x) . \]

The pattern: the \(x\) component uses only \(y\) and \(z\), in the cyclic order \(x \to y \to z \to x\). For example \((1, 2, 3)\times(4, 5, 6) = (2 \cdot 6 - 3 \cdot 5,\ 3 \cdot 4 - 1 \cdot 6,\ 1 \cdot 5 - 2 \cdot 4) = (-3, 6, -3)\). Check: its dot product with \((1, 2, 3)\) is \(-3 + 12 - 9 = 0\), as it must be.

The magnetic force. A charge \(q\) moving with velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) through a magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\) feels the force

\[ \mathbf{F} = q\,\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B} . \]

The field \(B\) is measured in tesla (T): 1 T is the field in which a charge of 1 C moving at 1 m/s at right angles to it feels 1 N. The force is zero for motion along the field and largest, \(qvB\), for motion across it. Together with the electric force, \(\mathbf{F} = q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})\), this is the Lorentz force, which drives everything in the course.

Example. A 2 kV electron moving horizontally at \(2.65 \times 10^7\) m/s across the Earth’s field of 50 μT feels a force \(evB = 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.65 \times 10^7 \times 5 \times 10^{-5} = 2.1 \times 10^{-16}\) N. Small, but on an electron it is an acceleration of \(2.3 \times 10^{14}\) m/s\(^2\), and chapter 2 shows it moves the spot of a 30 cm tube by 15 mm.

Other cross products. The torque of a force \(\mathbf{F}\) applied at position \(\mathbf{r}\) is \(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}\), and the angular momentum of a body of momentum \(\mathbf{p}\) at \(\mathbf{r}\) is \(\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p}\). F3 uses both.

4. Vectors that change: velocity, acceleration and circles

Differentiating a vector. A moving point has a position vector \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) that changes with time. Its velocity is the rate of change of position, done component by component, since the unit vectors themselves do not change:

\[ \mathbf{v} = \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} = \left(\frac{dx}{dt}, \frac{dy}{dt}, \frac{dz}{dt}\right), \qquad \mathbf{a} = \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} . \]

The product rules of F1 carry over: \(\dfrac{d}{dt}(\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}) = \dot{\mathbf{A}}\cdot\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{A}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{B}}\), and the same for the cross product, keeping the order.

Speed stays fixed when the push is sideways. Apply this to \(v^2 = \mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}\): \(\dfrac{d}{dt}(v^2) = 2\,\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a}\). If the acceleration is always at right angles to the velocity, the speed does not change. That is the magnetic force of section 3, now proved in one line.

Circular motion. A point going round a circle of radius \(R\) at a steady angular rate \(\omega\) (radians per second) has position

\[ \mathbf{r} = (R\cos\omega t,\ R\sin\omega t,\ 0) . \]

Differentiating with F1’s rules,

\[ \mathbf{v} = (-R\omega\sin\omega t,\ R\omega\cos\omega t,\ 0), \qquad \mathbf{a} = (-R\omega^2\cos\omega t,\ -R\omega^2\sin\omega t,\ 0) = -\omega^2\mathbf{r} . \]

The speed is \(v = R\omega\), and the acceleration points to the centre with size \(R\omega^2 = v^2/R\). Moving in a circle at steady speed is accelerating: the direction changes. Something must supply the force \(mv^2/R\) towards the centre.

The cyclotron frequency. In a uniform magnetic field \(B\) at right angles to the motion, the magnetic force \(qvB\) is always sideways, so the speed is fixed and the path is a circle. Setting the force equal to \(mv^2/R\):

\[ qvB = \frac{mv^2}{R} \quad\Rightarrow\quad R = \frac{mv}{qB} = \frac{p}{qB}, \qquad \omega = \frac{v}{R} = \frac{qB}{m} . \]

The radius grows with the momentum, but the time to go round does not depend on the speed at all. For a proton in 1 T, \(f = \omega/2\pi = qB/(2\pi m_p) = 15.2\) MHz; a 10 MeV proton circles with radius 0.457 m. Lawrence built the cyclotron on that independence of speed (chapter 10). The 2 kV electron in the Earth’s field circles with \(R = v/(\omega) = m_ev/(eB) = 3.0\) m. (Near the speed of light \(m\) becomes \(\gamma m\); chapter 1 shows why, and chapter 12 what it does to the cyclotron.)

5. Fields

Scalar and vector fields. A field is a quantity with a value at every point of space, and possibly changing in time. The temperature in a room, \(T(x, y, z)\), is a scalar field: one number at each point. The velocity of water in a river, \(\mathbf{v}(x, y, z)\), is a vector field: an arrow at each point. The electric potential \(V\) is a scalar field; the electric field \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) and the magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\) are vector fields.

Pictures of fields. A scalar field is drawn with contours, surfaces (or, on paper, lines) where it is constant, like the height contours of a map. A vector field is drawn with field lines, curves that run along the arrow at every point, drawn closer together where the field is stronger. The electric field of a point charge \(Q\) at the origin,

\[ \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\,\hat{\mathbf{r}}, \]

has straight field lines pointing out from the charge, spreading apart as \(1/r^2\); its potential, \(V = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)\), has spherical contours. Here \(\varepsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12}\) F/m and \(\hat{\mathbf{r}} = \mathbf{r}/r\) points away from the charge.

6. The gradient

The picture. Stand on a hillside of height \(h(x, y)\). Which way is uphill, and how steep? In the \(x\) direction the slope is \(\partial h/\partial x\), in the \(y\) direction \(\partial h/\partial y\). In a direction given by a unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}\), a small step \(ds\,\hat{\mathbf{u}}\) changes the height by (F1, section 9)

\[ dh = \frac{\partial h}{\partial x}u_x\,ds + \frac{\partial h}{\partial y}u_y\,ds = (\nabla h\cdot\hat{\mathbf{u}})\,ds, \]

where the gradient is the vector of the partial derivatives:

\[ \nabla h = \left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial h}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial h}{\partial z}\right) . \]

The slope in direction \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}\) is \(\nabla h\cdot\hat{\mathbf{u}} = |\nabla h|\cos\theta\). It is largest when \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}\) points along \(\nabla h\). So the gradient points straight uphill, its size is the steepest slope, and it is at right angles to the contours (along a contour, the height does not change, so \(\nabla h\cdot\hat{\mathbf{u}} = 0\)). The symbol \(\nabla\), “del” or “nabla”, is the vector of derivatives \((\partial/\partial x, \partial/\partial y, \partial/\partial z)\), waiting for a function to act on.

Field from potential. The electric field points downhill in potential, and its size is the potential’s steepest slope:

\[ \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\nabla V . \]

Between parallel plates, \(V = -\mathcal{E}x\) and \(\nabla V = (-\mathcal{E}, 0, 0)\), a uniform field. For a point charge, \(V = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)\) with \(r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}\). By the chain rule, \(\partial r/\partial x = x/r\), so \(\partial(1/r)/\partial x = -x/r^3\), and similarly for \(y\) and \(z\): \(\nabla(1/r) = -\mathbf{r}/r^3 = -\hat{\mathbf{r}}/r^2\). Then \(-\nabla V = Q\hat{\mathbf{r}}/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)\), Coulomb’s field of section 5. One function, \(V\), carries all three components of the field.

Example: a field that focuses. Four electrodes arranged round a beam pipe, alternately positive and negative, make near the axis the potential \(V = V_0(x^2 - y^2)/a^2\), with \(a\) the distance to the electrodes. Its gradient gives

\[ \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\nabla V = \left(-\frac{2V_0x}{a^2},\ +\frac{2V_0y}{a^2},\ 0\right) . \]

A positive particle displaced in \(x\) is pushed back towards the axis, with a force proportional to the displacement: the oscillation of F1 section 10. Displaced in \(y\), it is pushed away. For \(V_0 = 10\) kV and \(a = 2\) cm, the field 1 mm off axis is \(5 \times 10^4\) V/m. This quadrupole focuses in one plane and defocuses in the other, and chapter 16 shows how alternating them focuses in both.

7. Line integrals: work along a path

The definition. Move a charge along a curved path \(C\) through an electric field. On each short step \(d\mathbf{l}\) the field does work \(q\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot d\mathbf{l}\). Adding the steps gives a line integral:

\[ W = q\int_C\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot d\mathbf{l} . \]

To compute one, describe the path by a parameter, \(\mathbf{r}(s)\) for \(s\) from \(s_1\) to \(s_2\); then \(d\mathbf{l} = (d\mathbf{r}/ds)\,ds\) and the line integral becomes an ordinary F1 integral over \(s\). When the path is a closed loop the integral is written \(\oint\) and called the circulation of the field round the loop.

The fundamental theorem for gradients. If the field is a gradient, \(\mathbf{F} = \nabla f\), then along any path \(\nabla f\cdot d\mathbf{l}\) is just the change \(df\) of \(f\) over that step (section 6), and the steps add up to the total change:

\[ \int_A^B\nabla f\cdot d\mathbf{l} = f(B) - f(A) . \]

The answer depends only on the end points, not on the path between them; and round any closed loop it is zero. A field with this property is called conservative. Since a static electric field is \(-\nabla V\), the energy a charge gains from it is \(q[V(A) - V(B)]\), whatever route it takes. That is why an accelerator’s energy gain can be quoted in volts, and why no arrangement of static charges can make a ring that gives energy on every turn (chapter 5 to chapter 9 are about getting round that).

A field that is not a gradient. Some fields have a non-zero circulation: the magnetic field round a wire, or the electric field induced by a changing magnetic field, which drives the betatron (chapter 11). For such fields no potential \(V\) exists, and the work depends on the path. Section 11 gives the test.

8. Integrals over areas and volumes

Sums in two and three dimensions. F1’s integral adds up thin strips. To add up a quantity over an area, cut the area into small patches \(dA\), and over a volume into small cells \(dV\). In \(x, y, z\) coordinates, \(dA = dx\,dy\) and \(dV = dx\,dy\,dz\), and the sum is done one variable at a time: integrate over \(x\) holding \(y\) fixed, then over \(y\).

Choosing the cuts to fit. Shapes in physics are often round, and then it pays to cut them to match. A disc of radius \(R\) cuts into thin rings of radius \(r\) and width \(dr\), each of area \(2\pi r\,dr\) (its circumference times its width):

\[ \text{area} = \int_0^R 2\pi r\,dr = \pi R^2 . \]

A ball of radius \(R\) cuts into thin shells of area \(4\pi r^2\) and thickness \(dr\):

\[ \text{volume} = \int_0^R 4\pi r^2\,dr = \tfrac43\pi R^3 . \]

Example: the charge in a beam. A beam’s density usually falls off from the centre. If the charge per unit volume is \(\rho(r)\), depending only on the distance \(r\) from the axis, the charge per metre of beam is \(\int_0^\infty\rho(r)\,2\pi r\,dr\). Chapter 7 and chapter 22 use exactly this sum, with a Gaussian \(\rho\).

9. Flux

The picture. Water flows with velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) through a small flat hoop of area \(dA\). If the hoop faces the flow, the volume passing each second is \(v\,dA\); if it is tilted by an angle \(\theta\), only the part of the velocity along the hoop’s normal (the direction it faces) gets through, \(v\cos\theta\,dA\). Give the patch a vector \(d\mathbf{A}\), of size \(dA\) and pointing along its normal. Then the flow through it is \(\mathbf{v}\cdot d\mathbf{A}\), and through a whole surface \(S\), the flux:

\[ \Phi = \int_S\mathbf{v}\cdot d\mathbf{A} . \]

For a closed surface, written \(\oint\), the normal is taken to point outwards, and the flux is the net amount flowing out. The same definition applies to any vector field, whether or not anything really flows: the electric flux \(\int\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot d\mathbf{A}\), the magnetic flux \(\int\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{A}\).

The flux of a point charge. Take a sphere of radius \(r\) centred on a charge \(Q\). On it, the field points straight out, along \(d\mathbf{A}\), with the same size everywhere. So the flux is the field times the area:

\[ \oint\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot d\mathbf{A} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\times 4\pi r^2 = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0} . \]

The radius cancels: the \(1/r^2\) of the field exactly balances the \(r^2\) of the area. Every sphere round the charge has the same flux. This is the heart of Gauss’s law: the electric flux out of any closed surface is the charge inside divided by \(\varepsilon_0\) (taught in full in chapter 1).

Example: a high-voltage terminal. A sphere of radius 1 m at 1 MV, far from anything else, has potential \(V = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 R)\), so \(Q = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 RV = 1.11 \times 10^{-4}\) C, and the field at its surface is \(V/R = 1\) MV/m. Chapter 6 is about how big such a terminal must be before that surface field breaks the gas down.

10. Divergence, and Gauss’s theorem

Divergence: flux out of a point. Shrink a closed surface round a point until it is tiny, and divide its outward flux by the volume it encloses. The limit is the divergence of the field there, the flux out per unit volume. Take a small box with sides \(dx\), \(dy\), \(dz\). Through its two faces at right angles to \(x\), the outward flux is \(F_x(x + dx)\,dy\,dz - F_x(x)\,dy\,dz = \dfrac{\partial F_x}{\partial x}\,dx\,dy\,dz\). Adding the three pairs of faces and dividing by the volume \(dx\,dy\,dz\):

\[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial F_z}{\partial z} . \]

The notation is the dot product of \(\nabla\) with \(\mathbf{F}\). A positive divergence is a source, where field lines begin; a negative one is a sink, where they end; zero divergence means as much flows in as out.

Gauss’s theorem. Fill any volume \(V\) with small boxes. Where two boxes touch, the flux out of one is the flux into the other, and they cancel. Only the faces on the outer surface \(S\) survive. So the total divergence inside equals the flux out through the surface:

\[ \oint_S\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{A} = \int_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\,dV . \]

This is F1’s fundamental theorem in three dimensions: the integral of a derivative over a region equals the field’s values on the boundary.

Check. The field \(\mathbf{F} = (x, y, z) = \mathbf{r}\) has divergence \(1 + 1 + 1 = 3\). Over a ball of radius \(R\), the right side is \(3 \times \tfrac43\pi R^3 = 4\pi R^3\). On the surface, \(\mathbf{F}\) points straight out with size \(R\), so the flux is \(R \times 4\pi R^2 = 4\pi R^3\). The two agree.

Gauss’s law, at a point. The electric flux out of any surface is the charge inside over \(\varepsilon_0\). Write the charge as the integral of the charge density \(\rho\) over the volume, and use Gauss’s theorem on the left: \(\int\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\,dV = \int(\rho/\varepsilon_0)\,dV\) for every volume, which can only be if

\[ \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} . \]

This is the first of Maxwell’s equations, in the form F4 will use.

Example: the field of a beam. A long round beam of radius \(a\) carries charge \(\lambda\) per metre. Take a cylinder of radius \(r \ge a\) and length \(L\) round it. By symmetry the field points straight out from the axis, so the flux through the curved side is \(\mathcal{E}\times 2\pi rL\) and through the flat ends zero. Gauss’s law gives \(\mathcal{E}\,2\pi rL = \lambda L/\varepsilon_0\), so \(\mathcal{E} = \lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 r)\). A 10 mA electron beam at 2 kV moves at \(2.65 \times 10^7\) m/s, so it carries \(\lambda = I/v = 3.77 \times 10^{-10}\) C/m, and at the edge of a 1 mm beam its own field is \(6.8\) kV/m. This field pushes the beam’s electrons apart: the space charge that limits every intense low-energy beam (chapter 2, chapter 22).

11. Curl, and Stokes’s theorem

Curl: circulation round a point. Shrink a small flat loop round a point and divide the circulation \(\oint\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{l}\) by the area enclosed. The result depends on which way the loop faces. The curl is the vector whose component along a direction is the circulation per unit area of a small loop facing that way (going round it by the right-hand rule). For a small rectangle in the \(x\)-\(y\) plane with sides \(dx\) and \(dy\), going round anticlockwise seen from \(+z\): the two sides along \(x\) contribute \(F_x(y)\,dx - F_x(y + dy)\,dx = -\dfrac{\partial F_x}{\partial y}dx\,dy\), and the two along \(y\) contribute \(\dfrac{\partial F_y}{\partial x}dx\,dy\). So the \(z\) component is \(\partial F_y/\partial x - \partial F_x/\partial y\), and in full,

\[ \nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \left(\frac{\partial F_z}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial z},\ \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial F_z}{\partial x},\ \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y}\right), \]

the cross product of \(\nabla\) with \(\mathbf{F}\), with the same pattern as section 3. A field with zero curl everywhere is irrotational. A curl does not need the field lines to go round in circles; it needs the field to be stronger on one side of a point than on the other, as a stream flows faster in the middle than at the banks, which would turn a paddle-wheel placed off centre.

Stokes’s theorem. Cover a surface \(S\) with small loops, all going round the same way. Each inner edge is run along twice, in opposite directions, and cancels; only the outer edge \(C\) survives. So

\[ \oint_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \int_S(\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot d\mathbf{A} . \]

The circulation round a loop equals the flux of the curl through any surface it bounds.

Check. The field \(\mathbf{F} = (-y, x, 0)\) swirls round the \(z\) axis. Its curl is \((0, 0, 1 - (-1)) = (0, 0, 2)\). Round a circle of radius \(R\) the field has size \(R\) and runs along the path, so the circulation is \(R \times 2\pi R = 2\pi R^2\). The flux of the curl through the disc is \(2 \times \pi R^2\). They agree.

Two identities. For any smooth scalar field \(f\) and vector field \(\mathbf{F}\):

\[ \nabla\times(\nabla f) = 0, \qquad \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}) = 0 . \]

Both follow from writing out components and using the fact (F1, section 9) that the order of partial derivatives does not matter. The first says a gradient has no circulation, which is section 7’s result again. Read the other way, it is the test promised there: a field with zero curl everywhere (in a region without holes) is a gradient, and has a potential. A static electric field has zero curl; the magnetic field does not, since by Ampère’s law (F4) its curl is set by the current density:

\[ \nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{J}, \qquad \mu_0 = 1.2566 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{T m/A} . \]

Example: the field round a wire. Apply Stokes’s theorem to a circle of radius \(r\) round a long straight wire carrying current \(I\). By symmetry \(\mathbf{B}\) runs round the circle with the same size everywhere, so the circulation is \(B\times 2\pi r\), and the flux of \(\mu_0\mathbf{J}\) through the disc is \(\mu_0 I\). So \(B = \mu_0 I/(2\pi r)\): 20 μT for 1 A at 1 cm. Chapter 17 builds accelerator magnets from this.

12. The Laplacian

Divergence of a gradient. Combine the two: \(\nabla\cdot(\nabla f) = \nabla^2 f\), the Laplacian:

\[ \nabla^2 f = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial z^2} . \]

It measures how much \(f\) at a point differs from the average of \(f\) round it: zero means the value at every point is the average of its neighbours, so the field has no bumps or dips of its own.

Laplace’s and Poisson’s equations. With \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\nabla V\) and \(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = \rho/\varepsilon_0\),

\[ \nabla^2 V = -\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} . \]

This is Poisson’s equation; in empty space, where \(\rho = 0\), it is Laplace’s equation, \(\nabla^2 V = 0\). The quadrupole potential of section 6 obeys it: \(\partial^2/\partial x^2\) of \(V_0(x^2 - y^2)/a^2\) is \(2V_0/a^2\), and \(\partial^2/\partial y^2\) is \(-2V_0/a^2\); they cancel. So do \(x\), \(xy\), and \(1/r\) away from the origin. Designing electrodes and magnets is largely solving Laplace’s equation with the right boundaries, which chapter 2’s pre-requisite teaches. One consequence is already visible: since a potential obeying Laplace’s equation has no hills or valleys of its own, no static field can trap a charge in all three directions at once (Earnshaw’s theorem). The quadrupole shows it: focusing in \(x\) means defocusing in \(y\).

13. Coordinates that fit round things

Beams, pipes and magnets are round, and \(x, y, z\) make round problems clumsy. Two other systems are used throughout the course.

Cylindrical coordinates \((r, \phi, z)\): the distance \(r\) from the \(z\) axis, the angle \(\phi\) round it, and the height \(z\). Then \(x = r\cos\phi\), \(y = r\sin\phi\). A small step has length components \(dr\), \(r\,d\phi\) and \(dz\) (an angle \(d\phi\) at radius \(r\) sweeps an arc \(r\,d\phi\)), so the volume element is \(dV = r\,dr\,d\phi\,dz\), and the disc of section 8 is \(\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^R r\,dr\,d\phi = \pi R^2\).

Spherical coordinates \((r, \theta, \phi)\): the distance \(r\) from the origin, the angle \(\theta\) down from the \(z\) axis, and the angle \(\phi\) round it. A small step has components \(dr\), \(r\,d\theta\) and \(r\sin\theta\,d\phi\), so \(dV = r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi\), and integrating the angles gives the shell area \(4\pi r^2\) of section 8.

The derivatives in cylindrical coordinates. Derived by changing variables with the chain rule (worked through in Griffiths, appendix A):

\[ \nabla f = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial r},\ \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi},\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right), \qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial(rF_r)}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial F_\phi}{\partial\phi} + \frac{\partial F_z}{\partial z} . \]

The factor \(1/r\) in the angle term turns an angle into a length. The extra \(r\) inside the radial divergence accounts for the circles growing with \(r\). For the beam of section 10, inside the beam with uniform density \(\rho\), symmetry leaves only \(\mathcal{E}_r(r)\), and \(\dfrac{1}{r}\dfrac{d(r\mathcal{E}_r)}{dr} = \dfrac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}\) integrates to \(\mathcal{E}_r = \dfrac{\rho r}{2\varepsilon_0}\): the field grows in proportion to the distance from the axis, a linear push outwards that chapter 22 treats as a defocusing lens.

Exercises

  1. Find the size of \(\mathbf{A} = (2, -1, 2)\), a unit vector along it, and the angle between \(\mathbf{A}\) and the \(z\) axis.
Answer

\(|\mathbf{A}| = \sqrt{4 + 1 + 4} = 3\). The unit vector is \((2/3, -1/3, 2/3)\). Its dot product with \(\hat{\mathbf{z}}\) is \(2/3 = \cos\theta\), so \(\theta = 48.2°\).

  1. A force \(\mathbf{F} = (0, 0, 10)\) N acts on a body that moves from \((0, 0, 0)\) to \((3, 4, 5)\) m along a straight line. How much work does it do? Would the work be different along a curved path between the same points? Why?
Answer

\(W = \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{d} = 10 \times 5 = 50\) J. A constant force is the gradient of \(f = 10z\), so by section 7 the work is \(f(B) - f(A) = 50\) J along any path.

  1. Compute \((1, 0, 0)\times(0, 1, 0)\) and \((0, 1, 0)\times(1, 0, 0)\), and \((2, 1, 0)\times(1, 3, 0)\). What is the area of the parallelogram spanned by the last pair?
Answer

\(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\times\hat{\mathbf{y}} = (0, 0, 1)\), and the reverse is \((0, 0, -1)\). \((2, 1, 0)\times(1, 3, 0) = (1 \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot 3,\ 0 \cdot 1 - 2 \cdot 0,\ 2 \cdot 3 - 1 \cdot 1) = (0, 0, 5)\). The area is 5.

  1. A proton moves at \(10^7\) m/s along \(x\) through a field \(\mathbf{B} = (0, 0, 0.5)\) T. Find the force (size and direction), the radius of its circle, and its revolution frequency.
Answer

\(\mathbf{F} = q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B} = q(v, 0, 0)\times(0, 0, B) = q(0 \cdot B - 0,\ 0 - vB,\ 0) = (0, -qvB, 0)\): \(1.602 \times 10^{-19} \times 10^7 \times 0.5 = 8.0 \times 10^{-13}\) N along \(-y\). \(R = m_pv/(qB) = 1.673 \times 10^{-27} \times 10^7/(1.602 \times 10^{-19} \times 0.5) = 0.209\) m. \(f = qB/(2\pi m_p) = 7.62\) MHz.

  1. Find the gradient of \(f = x^2 + 3yz\) at the point \((1, 2, 1)\). What is the slope of \(f\) in the direction \((0, 0.6, 0.8)\) there?
Answer

\(\nabla f = (2x, 3z, 3y) = (2, 3, 6)\). The slope along the unit vector \((0, 0.6, 0.8)\) is \(0 + 1.8 + 4.8 = 6.6\). (The steepest slope is \(|\nabla f| = 7\).)

  1. Show that \(V = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)\) obeys Laplace’s equation away from the origin. (Use \(\partial r/\partial x = x/r\).)
Answer

\(\partial(1/r)/\partial x = -x/r^3\). Differentiating again, \(\partial^2(1/r)/\partial x^2 = -1/r^3 + 3x^2/r^5\). Adding the three: \(-3/r^3 + 3(x^2 + y^2 + z^2)/r^5 = -3/r^3 + 3/r^3 = 0\).

  1. Find the divergence and the curl of (a) \(\mathbf{F} = (x, y, 0)\) and (b) \(\mathbf{G} = (y, 0, 0)\). Which could be an electrostatic field in empty space?
Answer
  1. \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = 2\), \(\nabla\times\mathbf{F} = 0\). (b) \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{G} = 0\), \(\nabla\times\mathbf{G} = (0, 0, 0 - 1) = (0, 0, -1)\). An electrostatic field must have zero curl, and in empty space zero divergence too, so neither: (a) needs a charge density \(2\varepsilon_0\) to make it, and (b) has circulation, so it is not electrostatic at all. (\(\mathbf{G}\) is the kind of shear flow that turns a paddle-wheel.)
  1. Verify Gauss’s theorem for \(\mathbf{F} = (0, 0, z)\) on a cube with corners at \((0,0,0)\) and \((1,1,1)\).
Answer

\(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = 1\), so the volume integral is 1. On the top face (\(z = 1\), outward normal \(+z\)) the flux is \(1 \times 1 = 1\); on the bottom (\(z = 0\)) \(F = 0\); on the four sides \(\mathbf{F}\) is along the face, giving zero. Total 1.

  1. A long straight conductor of radius 1 cm carries 100 A, spread evenly over its cross-section. Use Stokes’s theorem to find \(B\) at its surface and at 5 mm from its axis.
Answer

At the surface, the loop encloses all 100 A: \(B = \mu_0 I/(2\pi r) = 1.2566 \times 10^{-6} \times 100/(2\pi \times 0.01) = 2.0\) mT. At 5 mm the loop encloses a quarter of the cross-section, 25 A: \(B = 1.2566 \times 10^{-6} \times 25/(2\pi \times 0.005) = 1.0\) mT. Inside, \(B\) grows in proportion to \(r\).

  1. A uniform round beam of radius \(a\) has charge density \(\rho\). Using Gauss’s law on a cylinder of radius \(r < a\), show that the field inside is \(\rho r/(2\varepsilon_0)\). For the 10 mA, 2 kV, 1 mm beam of section 10, what is \(\rho\), and does this formula agree with the field at the edge?
Answer

The cylinder of radius \(r\) and length \(L\) holds charge \(\rho\pi r^2L\); the flux is \(\mathcal{E}\,2\pi rL\). So \(\mathcal{E} = \rho r/(2\varepsilon_0)\). With \(\lambda = 3.77 \times 10^{-10}\) C/m over an area \(\pi(10^{-3})^2\), \(\rho = 1.20 \times 10^{-4}\) C/m\(^3\), and at \(r = a\), \(\mathcal{E} = 1.20 \times 10^{-4} \times 10^{-3}/(2 \times 8.854 \times 10^{-12}) = 6.8\) kV/m, the same as \(\lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 a)\).
