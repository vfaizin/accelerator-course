In one page

The wall. In a static electric field a particle’s energy gain is \(q\) times the potential difference it crosses, and that difference exists somewhere in the machine, across some insulator. Breakdown caps it at a few megavolts (chapter 4, chapter 5, chapter 6). In the late 1920s the practical limit was a few hundred kilovolts.

The idea. Use a time-varying voltage, and use it many times. Put a row of metal tubes on the axis of an evacuated pipe and connect alternate tubes to the two terminals of a radio-frequency (RF) oscillator, a circuit that makes a steady alternating voltage at millions of cycles a second. Inside a tube there is no field, so a particle coasts. If it takes exactly half a period to coast through, it meets an accelerating field at every gap. No point in the machine holds more than the oscillator’s peak voltage \(\hat{V}\), yet after \(n\) gaps the particle has gained about \(nq\hat{V}\). Gustaf Ising, in Stockholm, proposed a version of this in 1924. Rolf Wideroe built the first working one at Aachen for his doctorate (degree 28 November 1927): one drift tube, two gaps, sodium and potassium ions, about 20 to 25 kV from a valve oscillator (valve is the British word for a vacuum tube, here a triode of chapter 6) and about 40 to 50 keV out. David Sloan and Ernest Lawrence at Berkeley reached 1.26 MeV with mercury ions in 1931.

The equations to remember. With \(\lambda = c/f_{RF}\) the free-space wavelength (m), \(\beta_n c\) the speed in tube \(n\), \(g\) the gap length (m; in this chapter \(g\) is a length, not the quadrupole gradient of later chapters) and \(\phi_s\) the design phase:

\[ \begin{aligned} L_n &= \frac{\beta_n \lambda}{2} \quad \text{(synchronism: length of tube } n\text{, centre to centre of its gaps)} \\ W_n &= W_0 + n\,q\hat{V}T_t\sin\phi_s \quad \text{(energy after } n \text{ gaps)} \\ L_n &\approx L_1\sqrt{n} \quad \text{(non-relativistic, } W_0 \ll W_n\text{)} \\ T_t &= \frac{\sin(\pi g/\beta\lambda)}{\pi g/\beta\lambda} \quad \text{(transit-time factor of a uniform gap)} \\ \omega_0 &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}, \qquad Q_0 = \frac{\omega_0 U}{P} \quad \text{(the resonator that drives the tubes)} \end{aligned} \]

Numbers worth knowing by heart. Wideroe, 1927 and 1928: about 20 to 25 kV, about 40 to 50 keV, two gaps. Sloan and Lawrence, 1931: 30 tubes, 42 kV, wavelength 30 m (10 MHz), Hg\(^+\) at 1.26 MeV, \(10^{-7}\) A. Sloan and Coates, 1934: 36 tubes, 79 kV, 2.85 MeV. A mercury ion at 1.26 MeV has \(\beta = 0.0037\), so its last tube at 10 MHz is 5.5 cm long. A 1 MeV proton at 10 MHz would need a tube 0.69 m long, and a 10 MeV proton 2.2 m. Wideroe’s valve oscillator could not work effectively above about \(10^7\) Hz. That is why the first linacs accelerated heavy ions, and why proton linacs waited for wartime radar power at 200 MHz.

The wall

Pre-requisite (continued): Faraday induction, inductance and magnetic energy

Pre-requisite. Chapter 1 gave Faraday’s law, \(\oint\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = -d\Phi_B/dt\), and magnetic flux in webers, and stated briefly the inductance \(L = N\Phi_B/I\), the voltage \(L\,dI/dt\) and the energy \(\tfrac12LI^2\); chapter 8 used them for the inductance of a toroidal winding and the current transformer. This section works out, step by step and with the long solenoid as the example, the two facts about a coil that the RF circuit needs: the voltage across it, \(V = L\,dI/dt\), and the energy it stores, \(\tfrac12LI^2\). The next section uses Faraday’s law for the field that pushes a charge round a closed path; section 7 uses both facts for the coil of the resonator that drives the drift tubes, whose stored energy is all magnetic at the moment the current peaks. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, §4.1 (Faraday’s law of induction) and §4.1.2 (inductance and magnetostatic energy), with the solenoid’s field from §3.1.

The idea. A current in a coil makes a magnetic field, and that field threads the coil’s own turns. Change the current and the flux through the turns changes, so by Faraday’s law an electric field appears along the wire, pushing against the change. To change the current you must push back with a voltage, and the work you do is stored in the field. Let the current fall and the field gives the energy back.

(Part 0 met all of this first, in F4, section 14 and section 15; what follows works it through for the coil this chapter needs.)

Inductance. By the Biot-Savart law (F4, section 11) the field is proportional to the current that makes it. So the flux through each turn is proportional to the current, and so is the total over all \(N\) turns, the flux linkage \(\Lambda = N\Phi_1\) (Wb), where \(\Phi_1\) is the flux through one turn. The constant of proportionality is the self-inductance \(L\):

\[ \Lambda = LI , \]

in henries (1 H = 1 Wb/A). \(L\) depends only on the shape and size of the winding.

The voltage across a coil. Faraday’s law, applied to the closed path that runs along the wire of all \(N\) turns, gives an electromotive force \(-d\Lambda/dt = -L\,dI/dt\). It opposes the change (Lenz’s rule). If the wire’s resistance is negligible, whatever drives the current must apply the opposite voltage across the coil’s terminals:

\[ V = L\frac{dI}{dt} . \]

For a sinusoidal current \(I = \hat{I}\sin\omega t\) this gives, since the derivative of \(\sin\omega t\) is \(\omega\cos\omega t\) (F1, section 3), \(V = \omega L\hat{I}\cos\omega t\): amplitude \(\omega L\hat{I}\), a quarter period ahead of the current. That is the impedance \(j\omega L\) of chapter 4, now with its origin shown.

A long solenoid. Wind \(N\) turns evenly on a cylinder of cross-section \(A\) (m\(^2\)) and length \(l\) (m), with \(l\) much larger than the diameter. Ampère’s law gives a uniform field inside, \(B = \mu_0NI/l\), and almost none outside (chapter 2). Each turn holds the flux \(BA\), so

\[ \Lambda = NBA = \frac{\mu_0N^2A}{l}\,I \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad L = \frac{\mu_0N^2A}{l} . \]

The \(N^2\) is one \(N\) for the field and one for the number of turns it threads. The approximation is that the ends are ignored; a short, fat coil has less inductance than this formula says.

The energy stored. The power delivered to the coil is

\[ P = VI = LI\frac{dI}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12LI^2\right). \]

The last step is the chain rule read backwards (F1, section 2): the derivative of \(\tfrac12LI^2\) with respect to time is \(\tfrac12L\times2I\times dI/dt\).

Raising the current from zero to \(I\) therefore takes the work

\[ U_B = \tfrac12LI^2 , \]

and this is returned when the current falls back to zero. The energy is in the field. For the long solenoid, \(\tfrac12LI^2 = \tfrac12(\mu_0N^2A/l)I^2\), and since \(B = \mu_0NI/l\) this equals \((B^2/2\mu_0)\times Al\): an energy density \(B^2/2\mu_0\) (J/m\(^3\)) filling the coil’s volume, the magnetic partner of the electric density \(\varepsilon_0\mathcal{E}^2/2\) of chapter 1.

At work: the resonator’s coil. Section 7 takes drift tubes and leads of 50 pF at 42 kV and 10 MHz, tuned by a coil of 5.07 μH, and finds a current of amplitude \(\hat{I} = 132\) A. When the current peaks, the coil holds

\[ U_B = \tfrac12 \times 5.07\times10^{-6}\ \text{H} \times (132\ \text{A})^2 = 0.0441\ \text{J}, \]

exactly the \(\tfrac12C\hat{V}^2 = 0.0441\) J that the tubes hold a quarter period earlier, when the voltage peaks. The same energy passes from the tubes’ electric field into the coil’s magnetic field and back, twice a cycle. What does such a coil look like? A winding 10 cm across and 20 cm long needs \(N = \sqrt{Ll/(\mu_0A)} = 10.1\), so about ten turns by the long-solenoid formula; a coil only twice as long as it is wide has noticeably less inductance than that formula gives, so in practice a turn or so more. At the peak current its field is \(B = \mu_0N\hat{I}/l = 8.4\) mT. And the voltage across it: the current changes fastest as it passes through zero, at \(\omega\hat{I} = 8.3\times10^9\) A/s, and \(L\,dI/dt\) is then \(5.07\ \mu\text{H}\times8.3\times10^9\ \text{A/s} = 42\) kV, the gap voltage, as it must be, since coil and tubes are connected across each other.

Exercise A1. A coil of 20 turns is wound 5 cm across and 10 cm long. Estimate its inductance with the long-solenoid formula, the frequency at which it resonates with 50 pF, and the energy it stores at 10 A.

Answer

\(A = \pi(0.025\ \text{m})^2 = 1.96\times10^{-3}\) m\(^2\), so \(L = \mu_0N^2A/l = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\times400\times1.96\times10^{-3}/0.10 = 9.87\) μH. With 50 pF, \(f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC}) = 7.16\) MHz. At 10 A, \(U_B = \tfrac12LI^2 = 0.49\) mJ. A coil only twice as long as it is wide has rather less inductance than the formula gives, so the true frequency is somewhat higher.

Exercise A2. In a loop of \(L\) and \(C\) with no loss, the voltage swings with amplitude \(\hat{V}\) at \(\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}\). Show that the current amplitude is \(\hat{V}\sqrt{C/L} = \omega_0C\hat{V}\), and that the peak magnetic and peak electric energies are equal.

Answer

The coil’s voltage is \(L\,dI/dt\). For \(I = \hat{I}\sin\omega_0t\) its amplitude is \(\omega_0L\hat{I}\), and this must equal \(\hat{V}\), so \(\hat{I} = \hat{V}/(\omega_0L) = \hat{V}\sqrt{LC}/L = \hat{V}\sqrt{C/L}\). Since \(\sqrt{C/L} = \omega_0C\), this is also \(\omega_0C\hat{V}\). Then \(\tfrac12L\hat{I}^2 = \tfrac12L\hat{V}^2C/L = \tfrac12C\hat{V}^2\).

Exercise A3. In the 10 MHz resonator above, the current falls from 132 A to zero in a quarter period. What is the average voltage across the 5.07 μH coil during that quarter period, and how does it compare with the 42 kV peak?

Answer

A quarter period is 25 ns. The average of \(L\,dI/dt\) is \(L\,\Delta I/\Delta t = 5.07\times10^{-6}\times132/(25\times10^{-9}) = 26.7\) kV. The voltage is a quarter cycle of a cosine of peak 42 kV, whose average is \((2/\pi)\times42 = 26.7\) kV. The two agree.

Why a static field cannot do it

A static electric field is the gradient of a potential, \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\nabla V\) (F2, section 6; F4, section 5). The line integral of a gradient along any path is just the difference of \(V\) between its two ends (F2, section 7), and a closed path ends where it began, so \(\oint \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0\): the symbol \(\oint\) means the integral once round a closed loop. A charge that goes from one point to another gains \(q\) times the potential difference between them, whatever its path. To give a proton 5 MeV with static fields, some electrode in the machine must stand 5 MV above another, and the insulation between them must hold that. The previous chapters met this wall from three sides: gas breaks down at a few MV/m (chapter 4), insulator surfaces in vacuum hold one to a few MV/m along their length, and the cascade and the belt generator reached the order of a megavolt in the early 1930s (chapter 5, chapter 6).

Fields that vary in time give two ways out. First, by Faraday’s law, \(\oint \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = -d\Phi_B/dt\), where \(\Phi_B\) is the magnetic flux through the loop. A changing flux makes a field that pushes a charge around a closed path, again and again. That is the induction accelerator, Wideroe’s other idea, in chapter 11. Second: let the field oscillate, and hide the particle inside a metal tube while the field points the wrong way. That is this chapter.

Who stood at the wall

Rutherford, as president of the Royal Society in 1927, asked publicly for a “copious supply” of particles more energetic than the natural alpha and beta particles (Heilbron and Seidel 1989, chapter 2). Lawrence, in his Nobel lecture of 11 December 1951, listed what he considered in 1928 and 1929 at Berkeley: transformers and rectifiers, which seemed out of the question, partly for cost; the surge generator of Brasch and Lange; the electrostatic generator that Van de Graaff brought to practice; and the Tesla coil of Tuve, Breit and Hafstad. Each attacked the voltage head on. Each needed an accelerating tube that would hold the whole voltage, and tubes failed first.

Ising’s proposal, 1924

Gustaf Ising (1883 to 1960), a Swedish physicist at Stockholm, published a four-page note in 1924: “Prinzip einer Methode zur Herstellung von Kanalstrahlen hoher Voltzahl” (the principle of a method for producing canal rays of high voltage), in Arkiv för Matematik, Astronomi och Fysik 18, number 30. Canal rays were positive-ion beams (chapter 2). In Ising’s plan, ions travel along a straight evacuated glass tube through a row of hollow cylindrical electrodes. Each electrode is connected through its own lead to an oscillatory circuit excited by a spark gap. The leads deliver the voltage to the electrodes in turn, so that the accelerating field moves down the tube with the ions. Inside an electrode an ion feels no force; at each gap it is pushed again (Heilbron and Seidel 1989, figure 2.13; Kaiser describes the scheme as ions riding on the front of a travelling wave fed through several electrodes).

Ising did not build it. A spark discharge gives a damped train of oscillations with a broad spread of frequencies and an amplitude that changes from spark to spark, so it cannot keep ions in step with a precise timing. Lawrence learned of Ising’s paper only years after he started the cyclotron, and in his Nobel lecture called Ising “the father of the developments of the methods of multiple acceleration”.

Wideroe at Karlsruhe and Aachen

Rolf Wideroe (Widerøe in Norwegian), born in Kristiania (now Oslo) on 11 July 1902, studied electrical engineering at the Technische Hochschule in Karlsruhe from 1920 to 1924. There he worked out what he called a ray transformer (Strahlentransformator): replace the secondary winding of a transformer by a ring-shaped vacuum tube and let the induced electric field accelerate electrons round and round it. Heilbron and Seidel date the idea to as early as 1922; his notebooks of 1923 to 1928 hold the sketches (Waloschek 1994). Wolfgang Gaede, the authority on high vacuum at Karlsruhe, judged that the vacuum would be too poor and the electrons would be lost on the residual gas (chapter 3), and the project could not go ahead there.

In 1926 Walter Rogowski, professor of electrical engineering at Aachen and a builder of high-speed cathode-ray oscillographs, took the project on. Wideroe built the ray transformer in a cramped basement with a transformer yoke from the city’s power station. It failed: the electrons went round about one and a half times and then hit the glass. Kaiser gives two causes: scattered electrons charged the glass walls, and the field shape, although its ratio was right, did not hold the orbit stable. Rogowski would not accept that as a dissertation. Wideroe then turned to Ising’s proposal (How it was researched and built).

The physics

Pre-requisite (continued): Laplace and Poisson equations, uniqueness, boundary-value problems

Pre-requisite. This continues Laplace and Poisson equations from chapter 2, which with its continuations in chapters 4, 6 and 7 gave Laplace’s equation in charge-free space, conductors as equipotentials, the uniqueness of the solution, and solutions that depend on one coordinate. It adds two things this chapter takes for granted: why there is no field inside a closed conductor, and how far a gap’s field reaches into an open tube. The second needs Laplace’s equation in cylindrical coordinates and its radial solution, the Bessel function \(J_0\). Sections 1 and 2 use them for the field-free drift tube; section 5 for the shape of the gap field. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, §2.2.6 (no maximum or minimum of the potential in empty space), §2.4 (the Faraday cage), §2.4.2 (uniqueness) and appendix B.5.2 (Bessel functions from Laplace’s equation in cylindrical coordinates), and Wangler, §1.12 (the function \(J_0\) and its first zero, 2.405, in a cylindrical cavity) and §2.3 (the Bessel equation for the field of a gap).

Why electrostatics holds in an RF gap. The drift-tube voltages change in time, so strictly the fields obey the wave equation, not Laplace’s. The extra term is the second time derivative divided by \(c^2\). For a field oscillating at \(\omega\) it is smaller than the space derivatives over a region of size \(s\) by the factor \((\omega s/c)^2 = (2\pi s/\lambda)^2\). For tubes and gaps of 5 cm at a wavelength of 30 m this is \(1.1\times10^{-4}\). So at each instant the potential \(V\) obeys Laplace’s equation with the electrodes at their instantaneous voltages. This is the quasi-static approximation, and it holds whenever the apparatus is much smaller than a wavelength.

No field inside a closed conductor. Take a hollow conductor, closed on all sides, with no charge in the space inside. The whole conductor is at one potential \(V_0\). Inside, \(V\) obeys Laplace’s equation with \(V = V_0\) on the boundary. The constant \(V = V_0\) obeys both. Uniqueness says there is only one solution, so this is it, and \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\nabla V = 0\) everywhere inside, whatever fields there are outside. Another way to see it: a solution of Laplace’s equation has no maximum or minimum inside a charge-free region, so it cannot rise above or fall below its boundary value. This is the Faraday cage. At radio frequencies it holds at each instant, by the quasi-static argument.

An open tube. A drift tube is not closed: it has a hole at each end, and the gap field leaks in. How far? Take a long tube of inner radius \(a\) (m), on the \(z\) axis, with its open end at \(z = 0\) and its inside at \(z > 0\). Measure potentials from the tube’s own potential, so \(V = 0\) on its wall. Everything is symmetric about the axis, so \(V\) depends on the radius \(r\) and on \(z\) only. Laplace’s equation is \(\nabla^2V = \nabla\cdot(\nabla V) = 0\) (F2, section 12). In cylindrical coordinates (F2, section 13) the gradient of a \(V\) that does not depend on the angle has the components \((\partial V/\partial r,\ 0,\ \partial V/\partial z)\), and the divergence of a vector \(\mathbf{F}\) with no angle component is \((1/r)\,\partial(rF_r)/\partial r + \partial F_z/\partial z\). Put the one into the other:

\[ \nabla^2V = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r\frac{\partial V}{\partial r}\right) + \frac{\partial^2V}{\partial z^2} . \]

The product rule (F1, section 2) opens the first term: \(\partial(r\,\partial V/\partial r)/\partial r = \partial V/\partial r + r\,\partial^2V/\partial r^2\). Dividing by \(r\), Laplace’s equation in cylindrical coordinates reads

\[ \frac{\partial^2V}{\partial r^2} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial V}{\partial r} + \frac{\partial^2V}{\partial z^2} = 0 . \]

The middle term is there because the circles of radius \(r\) grow with \(r\); it is the cylindrical counterpart of the \((2/r)\,\partial V/\partial r\) term in the spherical form. (A partial derivative such as \(\partial V/\partial r\) is the rate of change with \(r\) while \(z\) is held fixed: F1, section 9.)

Separating the variables. Try \(V = R(r)Z(z)\), a function of \(r\) alone times a function of \(z\) alone; primes below mean derivatives of each with respect to its own variable. Then \(\partial^2V/\partial r^2 = R''Z\), \(\partial V/\partial r = R'Z\) and \(\partial^2V/\partial z^2 = RZ''\). Substitute and divide by \(RZ\):

\[ \frac{1}{R}\left(R'' + \frac{R'}{r}\right) = -\frac{Z''}{Z} . \]

The left side depends only on \(r\) and the right only on \(z\). Change \(z\) alone and the left side cannot change, so neither can the right; change \(r\) alone, likewise. So both equal one constant, written \(-k^2\) (with \(k\) in rad/m). Then \(Z'' = k^2Z\): a function whose second derivative is \(k^2\) times itself. The exponentials do this, since \(d^2e^{\pm kz}/dz^2 = k^2e^{\pm kz}\) (F1, section 4), so the solutions are \(e^{-kz}\) and \(e^{+kz}\). The second grows without limit into the tube, which no gap can cause, so \(Z = e^{-kz}\). The radial equation is

\[ R'' + \frac{1}{r}R' + k^2R = 0 , \]

and with \(x = kr\) it becomes \(d^2R/dx^2 + (1/x)\,dR/dx + R = 0\), Bessel’s equation of order zero. (Each \(d/dr\) is \(k\,d/dx\), so every term gains a factor \(k^2\), which cancels.)

Its solution, \(J_0\). Look for a power series, \(R = \sum_m c_mx^{2m}\) (even powers, since the potential must be smooth across the axis). Differentiate term by term: the term \(c_mx^{2m}\) contributes \((2m)(2m-1)c_mx^{2m-2}\) to \(R''\) and \(2m\,c_mx^{2m-2}\) to \(R'/x\), while in \(R\) itself the power \(x^{2m-2}\) comes from the term \(c_{m-1}x^{2m-2}\). The equation must hold for every \(x\), so the coefficients of each power add to zero. For \(x^{2m-2}\): \((2m)(2m-1)c_m + 2m\,c_m + c_{m-1} = 0\), that is \((2m)^2c_m = -c_{m-1}\), so \(c_m = -c_{m-1}/(2m)^2\). Starting from \(c_0 = 1\):

\[ J_0(x) = \sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^m}{(m!)^2}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2m} = 1 - \frac{x^2}{4} + \frac{x^4}{64} - \frac{x^6}{2304} + \cdots \]

It starts at 1 on the axis and swings like a cosine whose swings slowly shrink. Its first zeros are at \(x = 2.405\), 5.520 and 8.654. (The equation has a second solution, \(Y_0\), which is infinite on the axis; a field-free axis rules it out.)

The boundary condition picks \(k\). On the wall, \(V(a, z) = 0\) for every \(z\), so \(J_0(ka) = 0\), and \(k\) can only be \(2.405/a\), \(5.520/a\), and so on. The potential inside is a sum of such terms,

\[ V(r, z) = \sum_n A_n\,J_0\!\left(\frac{j_nr}{a}\right)e^{-j_nz/a}, \qquad j_1 = 2.405,\ j_2 = 5.520,\ \ldots, \]

with the amplitudes \(A_n\) set by the potential across the mouth. Each term dies away along the tube, the higher ones faster. Beyond a short distance only the first is left. The axial field \(\mathcal{E}_z = -\partial V/\partial z\) falls in the same way.

The result. The field inside an open tube falls by a factor \(e\) every \(a/2.405 = 0.416a\). It falls to a tenth of its value at the mouth in \(0.96a\), about one radius, and to a hundredth in \(1.91a\), about one bore (one diameter). The second term has already fallen to 0.044 of the first after one radius. So a tube is field-free only if it is several bores long, and a gap’s field reaches about a radius into each tube on either side, so the gap behaves as if it were somewhat longer than its metal-to-metal distance. That matters for the transit-time factor of section 5, whose field profile is this one.

At work. The first tube of the 1931 machine (section 3) was about 1.01 cm long, so its centre lies 5 mm from each mouth. Relative to its value at the mouth, the field reaching that centre from each end is, for a bore radius of

bore radius \(a\) (mm) 6.5 5 3 2
\(e^{-2.405\times5\ \text{mm}/a}\) 0.16 0.090 0.018 0.0024

A bore much wider than a few millimetres leaves the short first tubes far from field-free; the thirtieth tube, 5.5 cm long, is field-free for any of these. Narrow bores in the first tubes are what make the drift-tube picture of sections 1 and 2 true.

1. One gap and the phase convention

The picture. Two coaxial metal tubes face each other across a short gap of length \(g\). The oscillator holds a voltage between them that swings sinusoidally. The electric field is concentrated in the gap; inside each tube it is nearly zero, because the tube is a Faraday cage whose length is much greater than its bore. A particle crossing the gap gains or loses energy depending on the sign of the voltage at that moment.

Let the voltage across the gap be \(V(t) = \hat{V}\sin(\omega_{RF} t)\), with \(\hat{V}\) the amplitude in volts and \(\omega_{RF} = 2\pi f_{RF}\) in rad/s. A particle of charge \(q\) that crosses a very short gap at time \(t_c\) has the RF phase \(\phi = \omega_{RF} t_c\), and gains the kinetic energy

\[ \Delta W = q\hat{V}\sin\phi . \]

This is the rule of F4, section 5 and chapter 1, section 3.1: a charge that falls through a potential difference \(V\) gains the energy \(qV\), the work the field does on it (F3, section 4). If the crossing is quick, the voltage it falls through is whatever the gap holds at that moment, \(\hat{V}\sin\phi\). For a singly charged ion, \(q = e\), and a gap of 42 kV at its crest gives 42 keV.

This is the course’s convention: \(\phi\) grows for later arrival, \(\phi = 90^\circ\) is the crest, and \(0 < \phi < 90^\circ\) is the rising slope. Linear-accelerator texts usually write \(\Delta W = q\mathcal{E}_0T L\cos\phi\) with the crest at \(\phi = 0\) and stable phases between \(-90^\circ\) and \(0\). The two are related by \(\phi_{\text{here}} = \phi_{\text{linac}} + 90^\circ\), since \(\sin(\phi_{\text{linac}} + 90^\circ) = \cos\phi_{\text{linac}}\).

The approximation: the gap is so short that the voltage does not change while the particle crosses it. Section 5 removes it.

2. The drift tube and the synchronism condition

The picture. Wideroe’s arrangement connects tubes 1, 3, 5, … to one terminal of the oscillator and tubes 2, 4, 6, … to the other. At any instant, then, adjacent gaps have opposite fields: if gap 1 pushes forward, gap 2 pushes backward. A particle accelerated at gap 1 must reach gap 2 after the voltage has reversed. The first chance is half a period later.

Figure 9.2. Wideroe’s arrangement in side view, not to scale except that the tubes lengthen as \(\sqrt{n}\) (section 3). Tubes 0, 2, 4, 6 hang on one wire, tubes 1, 3, 5 on the other, and the oscillator and a coil \(L\) (section 7) sit across the two wires. At the instant drawn the even tubes are positive, so the field (blue, from \(+\) to \(-\)) points forward in gaps 1, 3, 5 and backward in gaps 2, 4, 6. The ion in gap 3 is pushed forward. Half a period later every arrow has reversed, by which time the ion is in gap 4. Inside a tube there is no field, whatever the tube’s voltage.

Derivation. Let the particle cross gap \(n\) at time \(t_n\) and then coast through tube \(n\), of length \(L_n\) (measured between the centres of the gaps at its two ends), at constant speed \(v_n = \beta_n c\). It reaches gap \(n+1\) at

\[ t_{n+1} = t_n + \frac{L_n}{v_n}. \]

Gap \(n+1\) carries the voltage \(-\hat{V}\sin(\omega_{RF} t)\), the opposite of gap \(n\). Since \(-\sin x = \sin(x + \pi)\) (shifting a sine by half a cycle turns it upside down), this voltage is \(\hat{V}\sin(\omega_{RF}t + \pi)\). For the particle to meet the same accelerating phase there, we need \(\sin(\omega_{RF} t_{n+1} + \pi) = \sin(\omega_{RF} t_n)\), so \(\omega_{RF}(t_{n+1} - t_n) = \pi\) (taking the shortest of the solutions \(\pi, 3\pi, 5\pi, \ldots\)). Then

\[ \frac{L_n}{v_n} = \frac{\pi}{\omega_{RF}} = \frac{1}{2f_{RF}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad L_n = \frac{v_n}{2f_{RF}} = \frac{\beta_n c}{2 f_{RF}} = \frac{\beta_n\lambda}{2}, \]

with \(\lambda = c/f_{RF}\) the free-space wavelength of the oscillator. In words: in half a period light travels \(\lambda/2\), and the particle travels \(\beta\) times that. This is why such a structure is called a \(\beta\lambda/2\) structure (Humphries 1986, section 14.2). Choosing \(3\pi\) instead gives tubes three times as long, which only wastes length.

Figure 9.3. The synchronism condition in time. The voltage that pushes an ion forward is \(+\hat{V}\sin\omega_{RF}t\) at the odd gaps and its opposite at the even ones. An ion that crosses gap 1 at the crest and then spends exactly half a period inside each tube (shaded and unshaded bands) meets the crest at every gap.

The condition contains no mass and no charge directly: only the speed. Any particle at the right speed is in step. That is why ion species could be changed by changing the oscillator.

Assumptions. The field inside each tube is zero, so the speed is constant there. The gap is short compared with the tube. And the particle follows the design: it has the design speed and arrives at the design phase.

3. The energy after \(n\) gaps, and tubes growing as \(\sqrt{n}\)

The picture. Each gap adds the same energy. The speed grows as the square root of the energy (non-relativistically), so the tubes grow as the square root of how many gaps came before.

Derivation. A particle that enters with kinetic energy \(W_0\) and crosses every gap at the design phase \(\phi_s\) has, after \(n\) gaps,

\[ W_n = W_0 + n\,q\hat{V}T_t\sin\phi_s , \]

where \(T_t \le 1\) is the transit-time factor of section 5 (set \(T_t = 1\) for a very short gap). The speed follows from \(\gamma_n = 1 + W_n/(mc^2)\) and \(\beta_n = \sqrt{1 - 1/\gamma_n^2}\), the relations of chapter 1, section 2 (the kinetic energy is \((\gamma - 1)mc^2\), and \(\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}\) solved for \(\beta\)). For ions in this chapter, \(W_n \ll mc^2\) (a mercury ion at 1.26 MeV has \(\gamma - 1 = 6.7\times10^{-6}\)), so the ordinary \(W_n = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_n^2\) holds (F1, section 8 shows that \((\gamma - 1)mc^2\) becomes \(\tfrac12mv^2\) at low speed). Solving for the speed, \(v_n = \sqrt{2W_n/m}\), and putting it into \(L_n = v_n/2f_{RF}\),

\[ L_n = \frac{1}{2f_{RF}}\sqrt{\frac{2W_n}{m}} . \]

If the injection energy is small compared with the energy gained, \(W_0 \ll n q\hat{V}T_t\sin\phi_s\), then

\[ L_n \approx \frac{1}{2f_{RF}}\sqrt{\frac{2 n q\hat{V}T_t\sin\phi_s}{m}} = L_1\sqrt{n}, \qquad L_1 = \frac{1}{2f_{RF}}\sqrt{\frac{2q\hat{V}T_t\sin\phi_s}{m}} . \]

The total length of \(N\) tubes is \(\sum_{n=1}^{N} L_1\sqrt{n} \approx \tfrac{2}{3}L_1 N^{3/2}\). The sum is close to the area under the curve \(\sqrt{x}\) from 0 to \(N\), which is \(\int_0^N\sqrt{x}\,dx = \tfrac23N^{3/2}\) (F1, section 7); for \(N = 30\) the sum is 112.1 and the integral 109.5, 2 per cent less. The energy grows as \(N\) and the length as \(N^{3/2}\), so the mean gradient, energy gain per metre, falls as \(1/\sqrt{N}\). This is the first sign of the limit in Where it stops.

Their abstract (Sloan and Lawrence 1931, Physical Review 38, 2021) gives 42 kV at a wavelength of 30 m on 30 tubes, and singly charged mercury ions at 1.26 MeV. That is \(1.26\ \text{MeV}/30 = 42\) keV per gap: each gap gave the full peak voltage, so the ions crossed at the crest. Pre-requisite (continued): Fourier series and transforms

Pre-requisite. This continues Fourier series and transforms from chapter 8, which gave the Fourier transform of a signal in time, its peak at the signal’s frequency, and the transform of a Gaussian pulse by completing the square. Here the same tool is turned on a profile in space, the field along a gap. The section gives the integrals of odd and even functions over symmetric limits, the cosine transform of a symmetric profile, the transform of a uniform (rectangular) profile, which is the sinc function, and the transform of a Gaussian, again a Gaussian, by a second route that stays with real integrals. Section 5 uses all four for the transit-time factor, and exercise 5 the last. Taught from Wangler, §2.3 (the gap field as a Fourier cosine integral) and §2.4 (transit-time-factor models), Tong, Quantum Mechanics, §2.1.3 (the Gaussian wavepacket, whose transform is a Gaussian) and §3.2.5 (the Fourier transform), and Penrose, §9.4.

Odd and even. A function is even if \(f(-z) = f(z)\), like \(\cos kz\) or \(z^2\), and odd if \(f(-z) = -f(z)\), like \(\sin kz\) or \(z\). Products follow the signs: even times even is even, even times odd is odd. Over limits symmetric about zero,

\[ \int_{-a}^{a}f_{\text{odd}}(z)\,dz = 0, \qquad \int_{-a}^{a}f_{\text{even}}(z)\,dz = 2\int_0^af_{\text{even}}(z)\,dz , \]

because for an odd function the area at \(-z\) cancels the area at \(+z\), and for an even one it doubles it.

The transform of a profile in space. For a function of position \(f(z)\) the Fourier transform at wavenumber \(k\) (rad/m) is

\[ F(k) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(z)\,e^{-jkz}\,dz = \int f(z)\cos kz\,dz - j\int f(z)\sin kz\,dz . \]

It measures how much of \(f\) goes like a wave of wavelength \(2\pi/k\) (the wavenumber \(k\) is to space what \(\omega\) is to time: F5, section 9; the idea of building a function from waves is F5, section 16). The second form uses Euler’s formula, \(e^{-jkz} = \cos kz - j\sin kz\) (F5, section 2; \(j\) is \(\sqrt{-1}\), as in the complex-numbers section below). (Books differ on where they put the sign, the \(2\pi\) and the letter for \(\sqrt{-1}\); nothing below depends on that.) If \(f\) is even, the sine integrand is odd and its integral vanishes. What remains is the cosine transform, real and even in \(k\):

\[ F(k) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(z)\cos kz\,dz . \]

At \(k = 0\) it is just the area, \(F(0) = \int f\,dz\). The ratio \(F(k)/F(0)\) is 1 at \(k = 0\) and falls as \(k\) grows. In section 5, \(f\) is the gap field \(\mathcal{E}_z(z)\), \(k = 2\pi/\beta\lambda\) is the rate at which the RF phase advances per metre of the particle’s travel, and the ratio is the transit-time factor \(T_t\).

A uniform profile: the sinc function. Let \(f = 1\) for \(|z| < g/2\) and 0 outside. Then

\[ F(k) = \int_{-g/2}^{g/2}\cos kz\,dz = \frac{2}{k}\sin\frac{kg}{2}, \qquad \frac{F(k)}{F(0)} = \frac{\sin(kg/2)}{kg/2} . \]

The function \(\sin x/x\) is called sinc. It is 1 at \(x = 0\), \(2/\pi = 0.637\) at \(x = \pi/2\), and zero at \(x = \pi\), that is when \(g = 2\pi/k\): one whole wavelength of the cosine fits in the profile and its positive and negative halves cancel. Note the reciprocity: the narrower the profile, the further in \(k\) its transform stays near 1.

A Gaussian profile. Let \(f = e^{-z^2/2\sigma^2}\), with \(\sigma\) (m) its rms width (root-mean-square: the square root of the mean of \(z^2\) weighted by \(f\), the standard deviation of F6, section 3 and the width of the Gaussian of F6, section 5). Call its cosine transform \(F(k)\) and differentiate under the integral sign:

\[ F'(k) = -\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z\,e^{-z^2/2\sigma^2}\sin kz\,dz . \]

(The derivative of \(\cos kz\) with respect to \(k\), with \(z\) held fixed, is \(-z\sin kz\).) Since \(z\,e^{-z^2/2\sigma^2} = -\sigma^2\,\frac{d}{dz}e^{-z^2/2\sigma^2}\) (the chain rule), integrate by parts, \(\int u\,v'\,dz = [uv] - \int u'v\,dz\) (F1, section 7). The boundary term vanishes because the Gaussian does at \(\pm\infty\), and the derivative of \(\sin kz\) brings down \(k\cos kz\):

\[ F'(k) = \sigma^2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{d}{dz}e^{-z^2/2\sigma^2}\right)\sin kz\,dz = -\sigma^2k\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-z^2/2\sigma^2}\cos kz\,dz = -\sigma^2k\,F(k) . \]

This first-order equation, \(dF/dk = -\sigma^2kF\), is of the kind solved in F1, section 10: divide by \(F\) and integrate, \(\ln F = -k^2\sigma^2/2 + \text{constant}\), so it has the solution \(F(k) = F(0)\,e^{-k^2\sigma^2/2}\), and \(F(0) = \sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma\) is the Gaussian integral of chapter 2. So

\[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-z^2/2\sigma^2}\cos kz\,dz = \sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma\,e^{-k^2\sigma^2/2}, \qquad \frac{F(k)}{F(0)} = e^{-k^2\sigma^2/2} . \]

The transform of a Gaussian of width \(\sigma\) in \(z\) is a Gaussian of width \(1/\sigma\) in \(k\): the same reciprocity, in its cleanest form.

Only the width matters when the profile is short. For \(kz\) small, \(\cos kz \approx 1 - k^2z^2/2\) (the first two terms of its Taylor series, F1, section 8), so for any even profile

\[ \frac{F(k)}{F(0)} \approx 1 - \frac{k^2\langle z^2\rangle}{2}, \qquad \langle z^2\rangle = \frac{\int z^2f\,dz}{\int f\,dz} , \]

where \(\langle z^2\rangle\) is the mean square width of the profile. The uniform profile has \(\langle z^2\rangle = g^2/12\), and its sinc expands as \(1 - (kg)^2/24\), the same thing. When a gap is short compared with \(\beta\lambda\), its shape hardly matters; its rms length does.

At work. In the first cell of the 1931 machine, Hg\(^+\) at 42 keV moves \(\beta\lambda = 2.01\) cm per period, so \(k = 2\pi/\beta\lambda = 312\) rad/m. A uniform gap field 5 mm long gives \(F/F(0) = 0.901\). A Gaussian with the same rms width, \(\sigma = 5\ \text{mm}/\sqrt{12} = 1.44\) mm, gives 0.903, and the small-\(k\) rule gives 0.898. The three agree to half a per cent.

5. The transit-time factor

The picture. A real gap has a length. While the particle crosses it, the voltage changes. If the crossing takes a sizeable part of a period, the particle sees the field at a range of phases and gains less than \(q\hat{V}\). In the extreme, if the crossing takes a whole period, the gains and losses cancel.

Derivation. Let the gap run from \(z = -g/2\) to \(z = +g/2\), and let its field be uniform in space at each instant (true if \(g \ll \lambda\), which holds by a factor of thousands here: gaps of centimetres, wavelength of 30 m):

\[ \mathcal{E}_z(z,t) = \frac{\hat{V}}{g}\sin(\omega_{RF}t + \phi), \qquad |z| < g/2 . \]

Here \(t = 0\) is the moment the particle is at the centre of the gap, so \(\phi\) is its phase there. Assume the speed changes little across the gap, so \(z = vt\) (this is good when the energy gained in one gap is small compared with the energy already held; it fails at the very first gap of a machine that starts from rest). The energy gained is the work done:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta W &= q\int_{-g/2}^{g/2} \mathcal{E}_z\left(z, \frac{z}{v}\right) dz = \frac{q\hat{V}}{g}\int_{-g/2}^{g/2}\sin\left(\frac{\omega_{RF} z}{v} + \phi\right) dz \\ &= \frac{q\hat{V}}{g}\left[\sin\phi\int_{-g/2}^{g/2}\cos\frac{\omega_{RF} z}{v}\,dz + \cos\phi\int_{-g/2}^{g/2}\sin\frac{\omega_{RF} z}{v}\,dz\right]. \end{aligned} \]

The work is force times distance, summed along the path (F3, section 4); the force is \(q\mathcal{E}_z\), and the field is read at the time \(t = z/v\) when the particle is at \(z\). The second line uses \(\sin(a + b) = \sin a\cos b + \cos a\sin b\) with \(a = \omega_{RF}z/v\) and \(b = \phi\).

The second integral vanishes because its integrand is odd. The first is worked out with the antiderivative of \(\cos(\kappa z)\), which is \(\sin(\kappa z)/\kappa\), here with \(\kappa = \omega_{RF}/v\): it is \((v/\omega_{RF})[\sin(\omega_{RF}g/2v) - \sin(-\omega_{RF}g/2v)] = (2v/\omega_{RF})\sin(\omega_{RF} g/2v)\). Multiplied by \(q\hat{V}/g\), the gain is \(q\hat{V}\sin\phi\) times \(\sin(\omega_{RF}g/2v)/(\omega_{RF}g/2v)\). With \(\omega_{RF}/v = 2\pi f_{RF}/(\beta c) = 2\pi/(\beta\lambda)\),

\[ \Delta W = q\hat{V}T_t\sin\phi, \qquad T_t = \frac{\sin(\pi g/\beta\lambda)}{\pi g/\beta\lambda}. \]

The quantity \(\theta = 2\pi g/(\beta\lambda)\) is the transit angle, the RF phase that passes while the particle crosses the gap, and \(T_t = \sin(\theta/2)/(\theta/2)\). The phase still enters as \(\sin\phi\): a finite gap scales the gain down but does not shift the best phase.

For a gap field of any shape \(\mathcal{E}_z(z)\), symmetric about the centre, the same steps give

\[ T_t = \frac{\int \mathcal{E}_z(z)\cos(2\pi z/\beta\lambda)\,dz}{\int \mathcal{E}_z(z)\,dz}, \]

the Fourier transform of the field profile at the wavenumber \(2\pi/(\beta\lambda)\). Real gaps have fields that bulge into the tube bores and vary with radius; those refinements are in chapter 13 and chapter 14 (Humphries 1986, section 14.4, derives the uniform case).

6. Phase: why only part of a continuous beam is accelerated

The picture. The ion source gives a continuous stream. Ions arrive at the first gap at all phases. Those that arrive near the crest are accelerated and, if their speed matches the design, reach the next gap in step. Those that arrive when the voltage is negative are slowed or turned back. Those in between gain the wrong energy, so they arrive at later gaps at the wrong time, and the error grows.

A short derivation. Write the phase and energy of a particle as \(\phi_n = \phi_s + \Delta\phi_n\) and \(W_n = W_{s,n} + \Delta W_n\). Its speed (non-relativistic, \(v \propto \sqrt{W}\), and with \(\Delta W \ll W_s\)) is \(v = v_s\sqrt{1 + \Delta W/W_s} \approx v_s(1 + \Delta W/2W_s)\), by the binomial approximation \((1 + x)^n \approx 1 + nx\) for small \(x\) (F1, section 8), here with \(n = \tfrac12\). The same rule with \(n = -1\) gives \(v_s/v \approx 1 - \Delta W/2W_s\), which is used next. Over a drift tube, built for \(v_s\), the phase it reaches at the next gap is

\[ \Delta\phi_{n+1} = \Delta\phi_n + \pi\left(\frac{v_s}{v} - 1\right) \approx \Delta\phi_n - \frac{\pi}{2}\,\frac{\Delta W_n}{W_{s,n}} , \]

since the drift lasts half a period (\(\pi\) of phase) for the design particle, and a particle at speed \(v\) takes \(v_s/v\) times as long over the same tube. A particle with too little energy is slow and arrives later: its phase increases. At the next gap its energy error changes by

\[ \Delta W_{n+1} - \Delta W_n = q\hat{V}T_t\left[\sin(\phi_s + \Delta\phi_{n+1}) - \sin\phi_s\right] \approx q\hat{V}T_t\cos\phi_s\,\Delta\phi_{n+1} . \]

The last step is the small-change rule \(f(x + \delta) - f(x) \approx f'(x)\,\delta\) (F1, section 5) applied to \(\sin\), whose derivative is \(\cos\), with \(\Delta\phi\) in radians.

For the error to correct itself, a late particle (\(\Delta\phi > 0\)) must gain more than the design particle, so \(\cos\phi_s > 0\): the design phase must be on the rising slope, \(0 < \phi_s < 90^\circ\). At the crest, \(\phi_s = 90^\circ\), the first-order restoring term is zero and phase errors are not corrected. This is phase stability, found in principle by Veksler (1944) and McMillan (1945) and treated in full in chapter 12. In 1928 to 1931 nobody designed for it. The machines were set for the crest, and Sloan’s synchronization was found by trial-and-error adjustment (Heilbron and Seidel 1989, chapter 2).

Pre-requisite. This continues Complex numbers and complex functions from chapter 7, which gave complex numbers in polar form, Euler’s formula and analytic functions for the Pierce gun, and the phasors of chapter 4. It gathers the tools behind phasors: Euler’s formula again, in the engineers’ notation, the complex exponential \(e^{j\omega t}\) as a turning arrow, the complex amplitude and its real part, the complex conjugate, why a time derivative becomes multiplication by \(j\omega\), and why the mean power is \(\tfrac12\mathrm{Re}[\tilde{V}\tilde{I}^*]\). Section 7 uses all of them. Taught from Penrose, §5.1 (the geometry of complex algebra) and §5.3 (the exponential series and the Cotes–Euler formula), and Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 1, §21 (an oscillation as the real part of a complex expression, and when the “Re” may be dropped).

Part 0 taught these from the beginning, in F5, sections 1 to 3 (complex numbers, Euler’s formula, oscillations as turning arrows) and section 6 (circuits with phasors); this section is a reminder in the form section 7 uses.

Notation. Engineers write \(j\) for \(\sqrt{-1}\), because \(i\) is a current; this section and section 7 follow them. A complex number \(z = x + jy\) has a real part \(x\) and an imaginary part \(y\), and is drawn as the point \((x, y)\) in a plane. Its size is \(|z| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\) and its angle \(\alpha\), measured from the real axis, satisfies \(\tan\alpha = y/x\).

Euler’s formula. Let \(f(\theta) = \cos\theta + j\sin\theta\). Differentiate:

\[ f'(\theta) = -\sin\theta + j\cos\theta = j(\cos\theta + j\sin\theta) = j\,f(\theta), \]

using \(j^2 = -1\). The only function with \(f' = jf\) and \(f(0) = 1\) is \(e^{j\theta}\), just as the only real function with \(f' = af\) and \(f(0) = 1\) is \(e^{a\theta}\). So

\[ e^{j\theta} = \cos\theta + j\sin\theta . \]

(The power series of \(e^x\), \(\cos x\) and \(\sin x\) show the same thing term by term.) Every complex number can then be written \(z = |z|e^{j\alpha}\), and multiplying two of them multiplies the sizes and adds the angles.

A turning arrow. \(e^{j\omega t}\) is a point on the unit circle at angle \(\omega t\): an arrow turning anticlockwise at \(\omega\) radians per second. Its real part, the shadow on the real axis, is \(\cos\omega t\). Multiply by a fixed complex number \(\tilde{V} = \hat{V}e^{j\alpha}\), the complex amplitude: the arrow is stretched to length \(\hat{V}\) and turned ahead by \(\alpha\), and

\[ \mathrm{Re}\left[\tilde{V}e^{j\omega t}\right] = \hat{V}\cos(\omega t + \alpha) . \]

The size of \(\tilde{V}\) is the amplitude and its angle the phase. The gap voltage of section 1, \(\hat{V}\sin\omega t = \hat{V}\cos(\omega t - 90^\circ)\), has the complex amplitude \(\hat{V}e^{-j\pi/2} = -j\hat{V}\).

The conjugate and the real part. The complex conjugate of \(z = x + jy\) is \(z^* = x - jy\): the same point reflected in the real axis, with the angle reversed. Two facts follow at once:

\[ \mathrm{Re}\,z = \frac{z + z^*}{2}, \qquad zz^* = |z|^2 . \]

What may be done inside the “Re”. Adding, multiplying by real constants, differentiating and integrating in time all commute with taking the real part. So one can do a whole linear calculation with complex amplitudes and take the real part at the end. Differentiation is the reason it pays:

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\left(\tilde{V}e^{j\omega t}\right) = j\omega\,\tilde{V}e^{j\omega t} . \]

A derivative multiplies the complex amplitude by \(j\omega\): the size by \(\omega\), and, since \(j = e^{j\pi/2}\), the phase forward by a quarter cycle. A differential equation with constant coefficients becomes algebra.

What may not: products. The real part of a product is not the product of real parts, so power, which is a product of voltage and current, needs care. With \(V(t) = \mathrm{Re}[\tilde{V}e^{j\omega t}]\) and \(I(t) = \mathrm{Re}[\tilde{I}e^{j\omega t}]\), write each real part as half the sum of the number and its conjugate:

\[ \begin{aligned} V(t)I(t) &= \tfrac14\left(\tilde{V}e^{j\omega t} + \tilde{V}^*e^{-j\omega t}\right)\left(\tilde{I}e^{j\omega t} + \tilde{I}^*e^{-j\omega t}\right) \\ &= \tfrac12\mathrm{Re}\left[\tilde{V}\tilde{I}^*\right] + \tfrac12\mathrm{Re}\left[\tilde{V}\tilde{I}\,e^{2j\omega t}\right] . \end{aligned} \]

The second term oscillates at \(2\omega\) and averages to zero over a cycle. So the mean power is

\[ \langle P\rangle = \tfrac12\,\mathrm{Re}\left[\tilde{V}\tilde{I}^*\right] = \tfrac12\hat{V}\hat{I}\cos(\alpha_V - \alpha_I) . \]

Only the part of the current in phase with the voltage carries power on average. The factor \(\tfrac12\) is the mean of \(\cos^2\).

At work. Put the gap voltage \(\tilde{V} = -j\hat{V}\), with \(\hat{V} = 42\) kV at 10 MHz, across the 50 pF of section 7’s drift tubes. The current is \(\tilde{I} = j\omega C\tilde{V} = \omega C\hat{V}\): real, so \(I(t) = 132\ \text{A}\times\cos\omega t\), a quarter cycle ahead of the voltage. The mean power is \(\tfrac12\mathrm{Re}[(-j\hat{V})(\omega C\hat{V})] = 0\): 132 A flows and no power is used. Add a resistance of 63.7 kΩ across the tubes; its current \(\tilde{V}/R\) is in phase with the voltage and takes \(\tfrac12\hat{V}^2/R = 13.9\) kW. That is section 7’s power bill.

Pre-requisite. This continues RLC circuits and phasors from chapter 4, which gave the impedances \(R\), \(j\omega L\) and \(1/(j\omega C)\) and the ringing series loop, and its continuation in chapter 5, which gave the reactive current \(\omega CV\) through a capacitor and resonance at \(\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}\). Taught from Wangler, §5.1 (the shunt resonant circuit as the model of a cavity), Livingston and Blewett, §6-8 (the equivalent circuit of a cyclotron’s resonant system), and Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 1, §21.

Kirchhoff’s laws for complex amplitudes. Kirchhoff’s two laws for direct current are in F4, section 9, and F5, section 6 carried them over to phasors; here is why that is allowed. At every instant, the currents flowing into a junction add to zero (charge does not pile up in a wire), and the voltages round any closed loop add to zero (outside the coils the quasi-static field has no circulation, and a coil’s induced emf is counted as its voltage \(L\,dI/dt\)). If every current is \(\mathrm{Re}[\tilde{I}_ke^{j\omega t}]\), the current law says \(\mathrm{Re}[(\sum_k\tilde{I}_k)e^{j\omega t}] = 0\) for all \(t\). At \(t = 0\) that makes the real part of \(\sum\tilde{I}_k\) zero, and a quarter period later the imaginary part. So

\[ \sum_k\tilde{I}_k = 0 \quad\text{at a junction}, \qquad \sum_k\tilde{V}_k = 0 \quad\text{round a loop}, \]

and with \(\tilde{V} = Z\tilde{I}\) for each element, an AC circuit is solved like a DC one, with complex numbers.

Elements in parallel. Elements connected across the same two points share one voltage \(\tilde{V}\), and their currents add. It is then easier to use the admittance \(Y = 1/Z\) (in siemens, S), with \(\tilde{I} = Y\tilde{V}\): admittances in parallel add, as impedances in series do. For a resistance \(R_p\), an inductance \(L\) and a capacitance \(C\) in parallel,

\[ Y(\omega) = \frac{1}{R_p} + j\omega C + \frac{1}{j\omega L} = \frac{1}{R_p} + j\left(\omega C - \frac{1}{\omega L}\right). \]

The parallel resonator. At \(\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}\) the bracket vanishes and \(Y = 1/R_p\). The capacitor’s current \(j\omega_0C\tilde{V}\) and the coil’s \(\tilde{V}/(j\omega_0L) = -j\omega_0C\tilde{V}\) are equal and opposite: they circulate round the loop of \(L\) and \(C\), and the source supplies only \(\tilde{V}/R_p\), in phase with the voltage. Away from \(\omega_0\) the bracket is not zero, \(|Y|\) grows, and the impedance \(|Z| = 1/|Y|\) falls from its peak \(R_p\). Below resonance the coil’s current is larger and the circuit looks inductive; above it, capacitive.

The same thing in time. Kirchhoff’s current law at the top of the circuit, with a source current \(I_s(t)\) fed in, is

\[ C\frac{dV}{dt} + \frac{V}{R_p} + \frac{1}{L}\int V\,dt = I_s(t) , \]

the three terms being the currents in \(C\), \(R_p\) and \(L\) (the coil’s current from \(V = L\,dI_L/dt\)). Differentiating once,

\[ C\ddot{V} + \frac{\dot{V}}{R_p} + \frac{V}{L} = \dot{I}_s . \]

With no source this is the equation of section 7. Replacing \(d/dt\) by \(j\omega\) turns it back into \(Y\tilde{V} = \tilde{I}_s\).

Power. By the result of the complex-numbers section above, the mean power taken by the circuit is \(\tfrac12\mathrm{Re}[\tilde{V}\tilde{I}_s^*] = \tfrac12|\tilde{V}|^2\,\mathrm{Re}[Y^*] = \hat{V}^2/(2R_p)\): only the resistance takes power. \(L\) and \(C\) only store it.

A coil’s own resistance. Real loss sits mostly in the coil’s wire and in the tubes’ surfaces, in series with the current, not across the circuit. A coil of inductance \(L\) with series resistance \(r \ll \omega L\) has admittance

\[ \frac{1}{r + j\omega L} = \frac{r - j\omega L}{r^2 + \omega^2L^2} \approx \frac{r}{(\omega L)^2} + \frac{1}{j\omega L} , \]

a pure inductance in parallel with a resistance \(R_p = (\omega L)^2/r\). A small series resistance is a large parallel one, and the parallel model holds near resonance.

Pre-requisite (continued): Simple and damped harmonic oscillator

Pre-requisite. This continues Simple and damped harmonic oscillator from chapter 7, which solved \(x'' + k^2x = 0\), showed that a restoring force proportional to displacement gives oscillation, and ended with the damped equation \(\ddot{x} + 2\Gamma\dot{x} + \omega_0^2x = 0\), its decaying solution and \(Q_0 = \omega_0/2\Gamma\). Here the same mathematics is carried over to the parallel \(L\), \(C\) and \(R_p\) of the section above. The section gives the energy passing between the capacitor and the coil every quarter period, the solution with loss, a sinusoid decaying as \(e^{-t/2R_pC}\), and why light damping leaves the frequency almost exactly at \(\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}\). The method, a trial solution \(e^{st}\) and its characteristic equation, is the one chapter 4 used for a series loop. Section 7 uses the results for the resonator, and At the bench for what happens when the drive stops. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 1, §21 (free oscillations and their energy) and §25 (damped oscillations), Tong, Classical Mechanics, §2.5.1 (the damped harmonic oscillator), and Wangler, §5.1.

Without loss. Leave out \(R_p\) and the source. The capacitor’s current \(C\,dV/dt\) is the coil’s current \(I_L\) with the sign reversed (they form one loop), and \(V = L\,dI_L/dt\). Differentiating the first and using the second,

\[ C\ddot{V} + \frac{V}{L} = 0, \qquad \ddot{V} + \omega_0^2V = 0, \qquad \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} , \]

the equation of chapter 7’s oscillator. With \(V = \hat{V}\cos\omega_0t\), the coil’s current is \(I_L = (1/L)\int V\,dt = (\hat{V}/\omega_0L)\sin\omega_0t = \omega_0C\hat{V}\sin\omega_0t\).

Energy passing back and forth. The capacitor stores \(\tfrac12CV^2\) (chapter 1) and the coil \(\tfrac12LI_L^2\) (the inductance section at the start of this chapter). Their sum is

\[ \tfrac12C\hat{V}^2\cos^2\omega_0t + \tfrac12L\,\omega_0^2C^2\hat{V}^2\sin^2\omega_0t = \tfrac12C\hat{V}^2\left(\cos^2\omega_0t + \sin^2\omega_0t\right) = \tfrac12C\hat{V}^2 , \]

using \(L\omega_0^2C = 1\). The total is constant. At \(t = 0\) it is all electric, in the capacitor; a quarter period later, when \(V = 0\) and the current peaks, it is all magnetic, in the coil; after another quarter it is back in the capacitor with the voltage reversed. It is the mass on a spring of F3, section 9, with the electric store as one of the two energies (the spring’s) and the magnetic store as the other (the mass’s kinetic energy).

With loss. Put \(R_p\) back. The current law gives the equation of section 7:

\[ C\ddot{V} + \frac{\dot{V}}{R_p} + \frac{V}{L} = 0 . \]

Try \(V = e^{st}\), with \(s\) a number to be found (in s\(^{-1}\), possibly complex). Each derivative brings down a factor \(s\), so \(\dot{V} = se^{st}\) and \(\ddot{V} = s^2e^{st}\). Every term keeps the factor \(e^{st}\), which cancels, leaving the characteristic equation

\[ Cs^2 + \frac{s}{R_p} + \frac{1}{L} = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad s = -\Gamma \pm j\sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \Gamma^2}, \qquad \Gamma = \frac{1}{2R_pC} . \]

The roots come from the quadratic formula: dividing by \(C\) gives \(s^2 + 2\Gamma s + \omega_0^2 = 0\), so \(s = -\Gamma \pm \sqrt{\Gamma^2 - \omega_0^2}\), and when \(\Gamma < \omega_0\) the square root is of a negative number, \(j\sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \Gamma^2}\).

Here \(\Gamma\) (s\(^{-1}\)) is the damping rate, as in chapter 7 (the \(\lambda\) of Landau and Lifshitz; not the relativistic \(\gamma\) of section 3). When \(\Gamma < \omega_0\), light damping, the roots are a complex pair, and adding the two solutions to make a real one gives

\[ V(t) = \hat{V}e^{-\Gamma t}\cos(\omega_dt + \alpha), \qquad \omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \Gamma^2} , \]

with \(\hat{V}\) and \(\alpha\) fixed by how the oscillation started. (By Euler’s formula, \(e^{st} = e^{-\Gamma t}e^{\pm j\omega_dt}\), a decaying exponential times a turning arrow; the real part is the cosine. F5, section 4 does the same for a mass on a spring, and F3, section 10 without complex numbers.) It is the lossless oscillation inside a shrinking envelope \(e^{-t/2R_pC}\). The energy, proportional to the square of the amplitude, falls as \(e^{-2\Gamma t} = e^{-t/R_pC}\).

Light damping leaves the frequency alone. Write \(Q_0 = \omega_0R_pC\), the quality factor that the next section defines from energy. Then \(\Gamma = \omega_0/2Q_0\) and

\[ \omega_d = \omega_0\sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{4Q_0^2}} \approx \omega_0\left(1 - \frac{1}{8Q_0^2}\right). \]

The shift is of second order in \(1/Q_0\). For \(Q_0 = 200\) it is 3 parts in a million. So for any resonator worth the name, \(\omega \approx \omega_0\).

At work. Section 7’s resonator has \(R_pC = 3.18\) μs, so if its drive stops (a spark at a gap, a valve that cuts out) its voltage decays with time constant \(2R_pC = 6.37\) μs, 64 cycles at 10 MHz, and is down to 1 per cent after 29 μs. The beam vanishes much sooner. A spark-excited circuit like Ising’s is the opposite case. Take a circuit with \(Q_0 = 10\), an illustrative value for a spark gap with its losses. Its amplitude falls by \(e^{-\pi/Q_0} = 0.73\) every cycle. A mercury ion in a 30-gap machine at 10 MHz spends 29 half-periods, 14.5 cycles, between the first gap and the last; over that time the amplitude would fall by a factor \(e^{-14.5\pi/10} = 0.011\). No ion could stay in step with a voltage falling a hundredfold while it passed. A steady source is not a refinement; it is what makes the scheme work.

Pre-requisite (continued): Driven oscillator, resonance and quality factor

Pre-requisite. This continues Driven oscillator, resonance and quality factor from chapter 7, which showed that a force in step with the motion gains energy every cycle when the drive frequency equals the natural one, and gave the resonance curve of a damped oscillator and its half-power width \(f_0/Q_0\) (chapter 8 added the response to a single pulse). Here the oscillator is the resonator circuit, and the same results are worked out for it: how its quality factor \(Q_0\) is defined from energy, how fast stored energy decays, how the response falls off away from resonance, what the bandwidth \(f_0/Q_0\) means, and how a source holds the amplitude steady by replacing the energy lost each cycle. Section 7 uses all of them; At the bench uses the resonance curve for frequency and voltage tolerance. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 1, §26 (forced oscillations under friction, and the range near resonance), Tong, Classical Mechanics, §2.5.2 (the driven oscillator and resonance), and Wangler, §5.1 (the shunt resonant circuit, detuning and half-power points) and §5.6 (transient behaviour of a resonant cavity).

The quality factor. Define, for a resonator holding energy \(U\) (J) and losing power \(P\) (W) at its resonant frequency \(\omega_0\),

\[ Q_0 = \frac{\omega_0U}{P} = 2\pi\,\frac{U}{P/f_0} , \]

\(2\pi\) times the energy stored over the energy lost in one cycle. It is a pure number and does not care whether the resonator is a coil and capacitor, a cavity or a tuning fork. The subscript 0 marks the unloaded value, counting only the resonator’s own losses; a beam or a coupled line adds losses and lowers it (chapter 13). For the parallel circuit, \(U = \tfrac12C\hat{V}^2\) and \(P = \hat{V}^2/2R_p\), so

\[ Q_0 = \omega_0R_pC = \frac{R_p}{\omega_0L} , \]

the two forms being equal because \(\omega_0C = 1/\omega_0L\).

Decay of stored energy. With no drive, the stored energy is spent on the loss: \(dU/dt = -P = -(\omega_0/Q_0)U\). So

\[ U(t) = U(0)\,e^{-\omega_0t/Q_0}, \qquad \hat{V}(t) = \hat{V}(0)\,e^{-\omega_0t/2Q_0} , \]

the amplitude falling half as fast as the energy, since \(U \propto \hat{V}^2\). This is the damping rate \(\Gamma = 1/2R_pC = \omega_0/2Q_0\) of the section above, now read from energy alone.

Driven near resonance. (F5, section 5 did this for a driven mass on a spring; the circuit follows the same mathematics.) Feed the parallel circuit a current \(\tilde{I}_s\) at frequency \(\omega\) (a valve’s anode current acts much like such a source). Then \(\tilde{V} = \tilde{I}_s/Y\), and the admittance of the RLC section, rewritten with \(Q_0\), is

\[ Y = \frac{1}{R_p}\left[1 + j\,Q_0\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_0} - \frac{\omega_0}{\omega}\right)\right], \]

since \(\omega CR_p = Q_0\omega/\omega_0\) and \(R_p/\omega L = Q_0\omega_0/\omega\). Near resonance put \(\omega = \omega_0 + \delta\omega\) with \(\delta\omega \ll \omega_0\); then \(\omega/\omega_0 = 1 + \delta\omega/\omega_0\) and, by the binomial approximation, \(\omega_0/\omega \approx 1 - \delta\omega/\omega_0\), so \(\omega/\omega_0 - \omega_0/\omega \approx 2\delta\omega/\omega_0\), the same step Landau and Lifshitz take in §26. So

\[ \tilde{V} = \frac{R_p\tilde{I}_s}{1 + jy}, \qquad y = \frac{2Q_0\,\delta\omega}{\omega_0}, \qquad |\tilde{V}| = \frac{R_p|\tilde{I}_s|}{\sqrt{1 + y^2}}, \qquad \text{phase} = -\arctan y . \]

Here \(\tilde{V} = R_p\tilde{I}_s/(1 + jy)\) follows from \(\tilde{V} = \tilde{I}_s/Y\), and its size from \(|1 + jy| = \sqrt{1 + y^2}\); its angle is minus the angle of \(1 + jy\), whose tangent is \(y\). The number \(y\) is the detuning in units of the resonance’s half-width.

Half-power points and bandwidth. The power, proportional to \(|\tilde{V}|^2\), falls to half its peak when \(y = \pm1\), that is at

\[ \omega = \omega_0\left(1 \pm \frac{1}{2Q_0}\right), \]

where the voltage is \(1/\sqrt2\) of its peak and \(45^\circ\) out of phase with the drive. The full width between them is \(\Delta\omega = \omega_0/Q_0\), or

\[ \Delta f = \frac{f_0}{Q_0} . \]

This is also how \(Q_0\) is measured: sweep a weak signal through the resonance and divide the centre frequency by the width at half power.

Holding the amplitude. At resonance the drive current is in phase with the voltage and supplies \(\tfrac12\hat{V}\hat{I}_s = \tfrac12\hat{V}^2/R_p = P\): exactly the loss. Each cycle the source puts in \(2\pi U/Q_0\), a small fraction of what is stored, and the stored energy swinging between \(C\) and \(L\) is untouched. Switched on from rest, the amplitude grows as \(1 - e^{-\omega_0t/2Q_0}\) towards its steady value, with the same time constant \(2Q_0/\omega_0\) as the free decay. (Wangler, §5.6, works this out for a cavity fed through a line; there the time constant is \(2Q_L/\omega_0\), with the loaded \(Q_L\) of chapter 13, because the line adds its own loss.) A resonator is a store whose leak the source keeps topped up.

At work. Section 7’s resonator has \(Q_0 = 200\) at 10 MHz: bandwidth 50 kHz, half-power points at 9.975 and 10.025 MHz, energy decay time \(Q_0/\omega_0 = 3.2\) μs, and each cycle the source replaces 1.39 mJ of the 44.1 mJ stored, \(2\pi/200 = 3.1\) per cent. Now suppose the frequency were set by a separate master oscillator and the resonator drifted 19 kHz off it (0.19 per cent, the frequency tolerance found in At the bench). Then \(y = 2\times200\times0.0019 = 0.76\), and for the same drive the gap voltage falls to \(1/\sqrt{1 + 0.76^2} = 0.80\) of its peak, with a \(37^\circ\) phase shift. At the bench shows that 5 per cent too little voltage already loses every ion at full energy. Even 10 kHz of drift (\(y = 0.4\)) costs 7 per cent of the voltage. A resonator must be kept on tune to a small part of its own bandwidth.

Figure 9.8. The resonance curve of section 7’s resonator (\(R_p = 63.7\) kΩ, \(Q_0 = 200\), 10 MHz), computed from the admittance \(Y\) without the near-resonance approximation. Its impedance, and so the gap voltage for a fixed drive current, falls to \(1/\sqrt2\) of the peak at 9.975 and 10.025 MHz, 50 kHz apart. For comparison, a copper cavity at 200 MHz with \(Q_0\) of order 50 000 (an illustrative value) has a bandwidth of only 4 kHz and a filling time constant \(2Q_0/\omega_0\) of about 80 μs (half that once a matched feed line is coupled in).

Pre-requisite (continued): Transmission lines, matching, S-parameters

Pre-requisite. This continues Transmission lines, matching, S-parameters from chapter 8, which derived, for pulses on 50 Ω cables, the line equations from the inductance \(L'\) and capacitance \(C'\) per metre, the wave speed \(1/\sqrt{L'C'}\), the characteristic impedance \(Z_0 = \sqrt{L'/C'}\), the coaxial line, and the reflection at a mismatched, open or shorted end. This section recalls those steps briefly, for vacuum between the conductors, where the coaxial line has \(Z_0 = (1/2\pi)\sqrt{\mu_0/\varepsilon_0}\ln(b/a)\), and adds what a line does at one frequency: standing waves on a shorted line, with voltage nodes half a wavelength apart (Lecher wires); why a shorted quarter-wave line looks like a parallel resonator; and delay lines. Section 7 uses all of these, and At the bench the Lecher wires. Taught from Wangler, §1.11 (the coaxial resonator, built from waves on a coaxial line), Livingston and Blewett, §6-8 (the quarter-wave coaxial lines that resonate a cyclotron’s electrodes), and Tong, Electromagnetism, §2.1.3 (the field of a line charge), §3.1.1 (the field of a long straight wire) and §4.3 (the wave equation).

The idea. Two long conductors side by side, such as a coaxial cable or a pair of parallel wires, have inductance and capacitance spread along their length. A change of voltage at one end cannot appear at the other at once: it has to charge the capacitance ahead of it through the inductance, piece by piece, and so it travels as a wave.

The line equations (recalled from chapter 8). Cut the line into pieces of length \(\Delta z\). Each has a series inductance \(L'\Delta z\) (with \(L'\) in H/m) and a capacitance \(C'\Delta z\) (with \(C'\) in F/m) between the conductors; take the line as lossless. The voltage drop along a piece is the inductance times the rate of change of current, and the current lost along it charges the capacitance:

\[ V(z) - V(z + \Delta z) = L'\Delta z\,\frac{\partial I}{\partial t}, \qquad I(z) - I(z + \Delta z) = C'\Delta z\,\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} . \]

As \(\Delta z \to 0\),

\[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial z} = -L'\frac{\partial I}{\partial t}, \qquad \frac{\partial I}{\partial z} = -C'\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} . \]

Differentiate the first with respect to \(z\) and use the second:

\[ \frac{\partial^2V}{\partial z^2} = L'C'\,\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial t^2} , \]

the wave equation, the same as for a stretched string (F5, section 8). Any shape \(V = f(z - vt)\) travelling forward, or \(g(z + vt)\) travelling back, solves it, with speed

\[ v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{L'C'}} . \]

The characteristic impedance. For the forward wave \(V = f(z - vt)\), the first line equation gives \(\partial I/\partial t = -f'/L'\), and since \(\partial f/\partial t = -vf'\), the current is \(I = f/(L'v) = V\sqrt{C'/L'}\). The ratio of voltage to current in a single travelling wave is fixed by the line:

\[ Z_0 = \frac{V}{I} = \sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}} . \]

A backward wave has \(I = -V/Z_0\). A resistor equal to \(Z_0\) at the end draws just the current the forward wave carries, so nothing is reflected: chapter 8’s matched termination.

The coaxial line. Inner conductor radius \(a\), outer \(b\) (m), vacuum or air between. With charge \(q'\) per metre on the inner conductor, Gauss’s law (F4, section 4) gives \(\mathcal{E} = q'/(2\pi\varepsilon_0r)\) between them, and integrating from \(a\) to \(b\) gives the voltage \(V = (q'/2\pi\varepsilon_0)\ln(b/a)\). So

\[ C' = \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0}{\ln(b/a)} . \]

With current \(I\) on the inner conductor and \(-I\) on the outer, Ampère’s law gives \(B = \mu_0I/(2\pi r)\) between them, and the flux per metre of length between the conductors is \(\int_a^bB\,dr = (\mu_0I/2\pi)\ln(b/a)\). So

\[ L' = \frac{\mu_0}{2\pi}\ln\frac{b}{a} . \]

(At radio frequencies the current flows in a thin layer, the skin, on the conductor surfaces, a fraction of a millimetre deep, so there is no field inside the metal to add; chapter 13 explains why.) Then \(L'C' = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\), so \(v = c\) whatever the radii, and

\[ Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\mu_0}{\varepsilon_0}}\,\ln\frac{b}{a} = 59.96\ \Omega\times\ln\frac{b}{a} . \]

A ratio \(b/a = 2.30\) gives 50 Ω, with \(C' = 66.8\) pF/m, \(L' = 0.167\) μH/m and a delay of 3.34 ns per metre.

Standing waves on a shorted line. (Standing waves and reflection at the end of a string are in F5, sections 10 and 11; a line behaves the same way.) At one frequency, write the voltage as phasors, \(\tilde{V}(z) = V_+e^{-jkz} + V_-e^{jkz}\) with \(k = \omega/v = 2\pi/\lambda\), a forward and a backward wave; the current is \(\tilde{I}(z) = (V_+e^{-jkz} - V_-e^{jkz})/Z_0\). Short the line at \(z = 0\). Then \(\tilde{V}(0) = 0\), so \(V_- = -V_+\): the short reflects the wave completely, with its voltage inverted. Adding, with \(e^{-jkz} - e^{jkz} = -2j\sin kz\) and \(e^{-jkz} + e^{jkz} = 2\cos kz\) (from Euler’s formula),

\[ \tilde{V}(z) = -2jV_+\sin kz, \qquad \tilde{I}(z) = \frac{2V_+}{Z_0}\cos kz . \]

The voltage is zero at the short and at every half wavelength from it, \(z = 0, -\lambda/2, -\lambda, \ldots\); the current is largest at those points. Slide a lamp or a shorting bar along a pair of parallel wires driven by the oscillator, mark where the voltage vanishes, and the spacing is \(\lambda/2\). These are Lecher wires, the frequency meter of short-wave radio.

The shorted quarter-wave line as a resonator. Look into the line from a distance \(d\) before the short. The ratio of voltage to current there is

\[ Z_{in} = jZ_0\tan kd . \]

For \(d\) less than a quarter wavelength, \(\tan kd > 0\) and the line looks like an inductance. At \(d = \lambda/4\), \(\tan kd\) is infinite: from its open end, a shorted quarter-wave line at that frequency draws no current at all, exactly like a parallel \(LC\) circuit at resonance. Just off that frequency, with \(\omega = \omega_0 + \delta\omega\), \(kd = \pi/2 + (\pi/2)(\delta\omega/\omega_0)\), since \(k\) is proportional to \(\omega\). Near \(\pi/2\) the tangent behaves as \(\tan(\pi/2 + \epsilon) = -\cos\epsilon/\sin\epsilon \approx -1/\epsilon\) for a small angle \(\epsilon\), so \(\tan kd \approx -2\omega_0/(\pi\,\delta\omega)\), and

\[ Z_{in} \approx \frac{1}{j\pi\,\delta\omega/(2\omega_0Z_0)} . \]

A lossless parallel \(L\) and \(C\) near resonance has \(Z = 1/[j(\omega C - 1/\omega L)] \approx 1/(j\,2C\,\delta\omega)\) (since \(\omega C - 1/\omega L = \omega_0C(\omega/\omega_0 - \omega_0/\omega) \approx 2C\,\delta\omega\), the step of the driven-oscillator section). The two match if

\[ C_{eq} = \frac{\pi}{4\omega_0Z_0}, \qquad L_{eq} = \frac{1}{\omega_0^2C_{eq}} = \frac{4Z_0}{\pi\omega_0} . \]

So near its frequency a shorted quarter-wave line is a parallel resonator, with its capacitance and inductance spread along it. With losses in the walls it has a \(Q_0\) like any other (Wangler, §1.11, gives it for the coaxial resonator).

At work. A 50 Ω shorted line at 10 MHz is a quarter wave at 7.49 m and behaves as 250 pF in parallel with 1.01 μH; at 200 MHz it is 0.375 m long and behaves as 12.5 pF with 50.7 nH. (At 200.1 MHz the exact \(jZ_0\tan kd\) and the equivalent circuit agree to 0.03 per cent.) That is why at 10 MHz the resonator of section 7 is a coil and the tubes’ own capacitance, while at hundreds of megahertz it becomes a piece of line, as in the cyclotron’s quarter-wave electrode supports (Livingston and Blewett, §6-8), and then a closed cavity. On Lecher wires the nodes are 15 m apart at 30 m wavelength, impractical on a bench, and 0.75 m apart at 200 MHz.

Delay lines. A line delays a signal by its length over \(v\): 3.34 ns per metre of air-spaced coaxial cable. Suppose, as in Ising’s scheme, a surge is to reach successive electrodes just as the ions do. Mercury ions at 0.5 MeV move at \(\beta = 0.0023\) and take 72 ns to go 5 cm, so a cable would need 21.6 m between electrodes. A chain of lumped coils and capacitors is a line with \(L'\) and \(C'\) per section instead of per metre, with a delay of \(\sqrt{LC}\) per section and \(Z_0 = \sqrt{L/C}\), as long as the signal changes slowly compared with one section’s delay. With 1 μH and 1 nF per section, the delay is 31.6 ns per section and \(Z_0 = 31.6\) Ω, so between two and three sections per electrode.

Pre-requisite. This continues Vacuum tubes and amplifiers from chapter 6, which gave the triode’s law, its amplification factor \(\mu\), transconductance \(g_m\) and anode resistance \(r_a\), and from chapter 8, amplifiers with feedback. It adds what an RF source for an accelerator asks of the triode: its gain as an amplifier with a tuned load; how feeding part of the output back to the input in the right phase makes it an oscillator; Barkhausen’s condition, that the loop gain is one and the loop phase a whole number of cycles; how the amplitude settles; and what limits the frequency, the electrons’ transit time and the valve’s own leads and electrode capacitance. Section 7 uses all of these. Taught from Chao and Tigner’s Handbook, §7.3.3 (tetrode amplifiers: \(\mu\), \(g_m = \mu/r_a\), and how a finite \(r_a\) lowers the impedance of the cavity it drives), Livingston and Blewett, §6-8 (the self-excited, tuned-plate tuned-grid oscillators that drove cyclotrons), Wangler, §5.8 (gridded tubes as amplifiers below about 300 MHz), the Child–Langmuir law of chapter 2, and the damped oscillator above.

Where chapter 6 left the triode. A heated cathode, a grid close to it and an anode further away; the current is limited by space charge and follows \(I_a = k(V_g + V_a/\mu)^{3/2}\), with \(k\) the perveance and \(\mu\) the amplification factor (a pure number, nothing to do with permeability). For small changes, \(\delta I_a = g_m\,\delta V_g + \delta V_a/r_a\), with the transconductance \(g_m\) (A/V), the anode resistance \(r_a\) (Ω) and \(g_m = \mu/r_a\). The grid is kept negative, so it controls power without taking any. Chapter 6 used the triode as a variable resistance at DC. Here it works at radio frequency into a tuned load.

Gain with a load. Connect the anode to its supply through a load of impedance \(Z\), such as the resonator. The changing anode current flows through \(Z\), so \(\delta V_a = -Z\,\delta I_a\). Put that into chapter 6’s small-change equation, \(\delta I_a = g_m\,\delta V_g - Z\,\delta I_a/r_a\), collect the \(\delta I_a\) terms, and use \(g_m r_a = \mu\):

\[ \delta I_a = \frac{g_m\,\delta V_g}{1 + Z/r_a}, \qquad A = \frac{\delta V_a}{\delta V_g} = -\frac{\mu Z}{r_a + Z} . \]

With a resonator at its resonance, \(Z = R_p\) is real and large and the gain approaches \(-\mu\). The minus sign means the anode swings opposite to the grid, half a cycle out of phase. Off resonance \(Z\) is complex and so is \(A\).

From amplifier to oscillator. Take a fraction of the anode voltage back to the grid, through a small coil coupled to the resonator’s coil (Meissner’s and Armstrong’s arrangement) or from a tap on that coil (Hartley’s), connected so as to undo the amplifier’s sign reversal. Write \(\tilde{V}_g = F\tilde{V}_a\), with \(F\) the feedback fraction. Then

\[ \tilde{V}_a = A\tilde{V}_g = AF\,\tilde{V}_a , \]

and an anode voltage can sustain itself with no input only if

\[ A(\omega)F(\omega) = 1 . \]

This is Barkhausen’s condition, in two parts: the size of the loop gain \(|AF|\) is one, and its phase is zero or a whole number of cycles. The phase part picks the frequency: with \(F\) real and negative, \(AF\) is real and positive only where \(Z\) is real, at the resonator’s own frequency. The size part fixes how much feedback is needed, \(|F| = 1/|A|\).

How the amplitude settles. Write the valve as a current source of size \(g_m|F|V\) feeding the resonator, where \(V\) is the voltage across the resonator; the reversed feedback connection makes this current flow into the resonator in phase with \(V\). Let \(R\) be \(R_p\) in parallel with \(r_a\), which loads it. The current law of the RLC section, with this source on its right side and differentiated once, gives \(C\ddot{V} + \dot{V}/R + V/L = g_m|F|\dot{V}\); moving the source term to the left, it becomes the damped oscillator with a changed damping term:

\[ C\ddot{V} + \left(\frac{1}{R} - g_m|F|\right)\dot{V} + \frac{V}{L} = 0 . \]

If \(g_m|F| > 1/R\), the damping is negative: any small disturbance, even the noise of the electrons, grows as \(e^{+t(g_m|F| - 1/R)/2C}\). It grows until the swings drive the valve to cut-off on part of each cycle and towards saturation on another; the average \(g_m\) falls, and the amplitude settles where the effective loop gain is exactly one. Barkhausen’s condition is the steady state of this growth. The result is what a spark cannot give: a fixed amplitude at the resonator’s frequency, held indefinitely.

At work. Take an illustrative triode with \(\mu = 20\) and \(r_a = 5\) kΩ (\(g_m = 4\) mA/V), loading section 7’s resonator (\(R_p = 63.7\) kΩ, \(C = 50\) pF, 10 MHz). The gain at resonance is \(\mu R_p/(r_a + R_p) = 18.5\), so the oscillator needs \(|F| \ge 1/18.5 = 0.054\). The valve’s \(r_a\) in parallel brings the resonator’s resistance down to 4.64 kΩ and its effective \(Q\) from 200 to 15, which is one reason practical circuits connect the anode to only part of the tank coil. With twice the minimum feedback, \(|F| = 0.1\), the growth rate is \((g_m|F| - 1/R)/2C = 1.8\times10^6\) s\(^{-1}\), an e-folding time of 0.54 μs, and noise of 1 mV reaches 42 kV in about 10 μs. Livingston and Blewett (§6-8) describe exactly this kind of self-excited oscillator, its tuned circuit being the resonant system of the accelerating electrodes, so that it follows any drift of their frequency.

What limits the frequency. Two things, both visible in the numbers of section 7.

Transit time. Electrons from the cathode take a time to reach the grid. Under space-charge-limited flow across a gap \(d\) to a potential \(V_g\) it is \(3d/v\) with \(v = \sqrt{2eV_g/m_e}\) (chapter 2): 1.0 ns for 2 mm and 100 V. The phase that passes meanwhile, the transit angle \(\omega t\), is \(3.6^\circ\) at 10 MHz but \(73^\circ\) at 200 MHz. Once it is large, the anode current no longer follows the grid voltage at once: the gain acquires a phase lag that upsets Barkhausen’s phase condition, and electrons in flight induce currents in the grid that take power from whatever drives it.

Leads and electrode capacitance. The connections inside the valve have inductance, and its electrodes capacitance between them. Together they form a resonator of their own: 50 nH with 10 pF resonates at 225 MHz. Near and above that frequency an outside tuned circuit cannot set the frequency, and much of the RF voltage appears across the leads rather than between the electrodes (at 200 MHz, 50 nH has a reactance of 63 Ω, against 80 Ω for 10 pF). Both limits push towards small electrode spacings and short, wide leads, and in the end to different devices: Wangler (§5.8) puts gridded tubes below about 300 MHz and the klystron above (chapter 13).

The 1920s tools of radio engineering are what made this possible. Here is the minimum.

Phasors. A quantity varying as \(V(t) = \hat{V}\cos(\omega t + \alpha)\) is written \(V(t) = \mathrm{Re}\,[\tilde{V}e^{j\omega t}]\) with the complex amplitude \(\tilde{V} = \hat{V}e^{j\alpha}\) (engineers write \(j\) for \(\sqrt{-1}\)). Differentiation becomes multiplication by \(j\omega\). An inductor \(L\) (henry) obeys \(V = L\,dI/dt\), so \(\tilde{V} = j\omega L\tilde{I}\), an impedance \(Z_L = j\omega L\); a capacitor \(C\) (farad) has \(Z_C = 1/(j\omega C)\); a resistor \(Z_R = R\). Kirchhoff’s laws then hold for the complex amplitudes, and AC circuits become algebra (Humphries 1986, section 12.1). Time-averaged power is \(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{Re}[\tilde{V}\tilde{I}^*]\).

The LC resonator. The picture. A capacitor and a coil in a loop pass energy back and forth: all electric when the capacitor is fully charged, all magnetic a quarter-period later when the current peaks. Without loss it oscillates for ever at one frequency. With loss it rings down, and to hold it at constant amplitude a source must replace exactly the energy lost each cycle.

Derivation. For a parallel \(L\), \(C\) and resistance \(R_p\) (here \(\omega_0\) and \(f_0\) are the circuit’s resonant frequency, not a ring’s revolution frequency as in later chapters), Kirchhoff’s current law gives \(C\ddot{V} + \dot{V}/R_p + V/L = 0\). This is the damped oscillator of Landau and Lifshitz (vol. 1, §25), with solutions \(V \propto e^{-t/(2R_pC)}\cos\omega t\) and, for light damping, \(\omega \approx \omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}\). The stored energy is \(U = \tfrac{1}{2}C\hat{V}^2\), and the power lost in \(R_p\) is \(P = \hat{V}^2/(2R_p)\). Define the unloaded quality factor

\[ Q_0 = \frac{\omega_0 U}{P} = \omega_0 R_p C = \frac{R_p}{\omega_0 L} , \]

\(2\pi\) times the energy stored over the energy lost per cycle. The energy decays as \(e^{-\omega_0 t/Q_0}\) and the voltage as \(e^{-\omega_0 t/2Q_0}\). Driven at frequency \(\omega\) near \(\omega_0\), the response falls to half power at \(\omega_0(1 \pm 1/2Q_0)\), so the full width is \(\Delta f = f_0/Q_0\) (forced oscillations with friction: Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 1, §26). The quantity \(\hat{V}^2/P = 2R_p\) is the shunt impedance \(R_{sh}\) in the linac definition, a measure (in ohms) of how much voltage a structure gives for the power spent; circuit texts use \(\hat{V}^2/2P = R_p\), half of it (chapter 13).

The drift tubes are a capacitor. In a Wideroe structure the two sets of tubes and their leads form the \(C\) of the resonator, and a coil supplies the \(L\). The oscillator must carry the displacement current \(\omega C\hat{V}\) (the current that charges and discharges the tubes’ capacitance each cycle, which inside the gaps flows on as Maxwell’s displacement current, F4, section 16), and the losses in the tubes, leads and coil must be paid for. An illustration, with assumed values: if the tubes and leads present \(C = 50\) pF at 10 MHz and 42 kV, the coil must be \(L = 1/(\omega_0^2C) = 5.07\) μH, the peak current through it is 132 A, the stored energy 0.044 J, and with \(Q_0 = 200\) the oscillator must supply 13.9 kW, with a bandwidth of 50 kHz and \(R_{sh} = 127\) kΩ. The point is the scaling: power goes as \(\omega C\hat{V}^2/Q_0\). Sloan and Coates (1934) attributed their gain over the 42 kV of the 1931 tube principally to less heating of the electrodes, by lowering the electrodes’ resistance and capacitance; they shrank them from 13 mm to 6 mm in diameter.

Transmission lines. Two conductors, such as a coaxial cable or a pair of parallel wires, with inductance \(L'\) and capacitance \(C'\) per metre, carry waves at speed \(1/\sqrt{L'C'}\) (the speed of light for vacuum between them) with a ratio of voltage to current \(Z_0 = \sqrt{L'/C'}\), the characteristic impedance. For a coaxial line of radii \(a\) and \(b\) in vacuum, \(Z_0 = (1/2\pi)\sqrt{\mu_0/\varepsilon_0}\,\ln(b/a) = 59.96\ \Omega \times \ln(b/a)\); a ratio \(b/a = 2.3\) gives 50 Ω. A line shorted at one end and a quarter-wavelength long looks, from its open end, like a parallel resonator. At 10 MHz a quarter wavelength is 7.5 m, too long to be a convenient resonator, so the machines of 1928 to 1934 used lumped coils and capacitors. At 200 MHz a quarter wavelength is 0.37 m, and resonators become pieces of line, then closed cavities (chapter 13). Wideroe recalled that Ising’s leads were delay lines, delivering the surge to successive electrodes at successive times (Waloschek 1994; for transmission lines, Humphries 1986, section 12.6).

The valve oscillator. A triode (de Forest’s Audion of 1906 and 1907, made a true high-vacuum valve by Arnold at AT&T and Langmuir at General Electric by 1913) is an amplifier: a small grid voltage controls a large anode current. Feed part of the output of a tuned amplifier back to its own grid in the right phase and it oscillates at the frequency of its tuned circuit. Several people found this around 1912 and 1913, among them Armstrong and Meissner; Hartley’s circuit followed in 1915. Barkhausen stated the condition in 1921: the gain around the loop must be one, and the phase shift around it a whole number of cycles. By the 1920s such valves had replaced spark transmitters for continuous waves.

What limits the frequency? The electrons must cross from cathode to grid in a small fraction of a period. Under space-charge-limited flow the transit time across a gap \(d\) to a potential \(V_g\) is \(3d/v\) with \(v = \sqrt{2eV_g/m_e}\) (chapter 2); for 2 mm and 100 V it is 1.0 ns, a hundredth of a period at 10 MHz but a fifth at 200 MHz. And the valve’s own lead inductance and electrode capacitance form a resonator of their own: 50 nH with 10 pF resonates at 225 MHz, above which the tube cannot be tuned from outside. (These are illustrative values.) Power valves of the 1920s were large, with long leads, and Heilbron and Seidel put the useful limit of Wideroe’s oscillator at about \(10^7\) Hz. Large power at hundreds of megahertz came only with the wartime work on radar.

8. Measuring the energy: electric and magnetic deflection

Electric deflection. (The same calculation for electrons is chapter 2, section 2, with its figure.) An ion of charge \(q\) and kinetic energy \(W = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2\) passes between parallel plates of length \(l_p\), gap \(d\) and voltage \(V_d\). The transverse force \(qV_d/d\) acts for a time \(l_p/v\), giving, by Newton’s second law (transverse momentum gained = force × time), the transverse velocity \(qV_dl_p/(dmv)\) and an angle, the ratio of transverse to forward velocity (small, so the angle in radians equals its tangent),

\[ \theta_E = \frac{qV_d l_p}{d\,mv^2} = \frac{V_d\,l_p}{2d}\,\frac{q}{W} . \]

The mass has gone: electric deflection measures energy per charge, the voltage through which the ion has effectively fallen. Sodium and potassium ions from the same gaps land at the same place. For plates 5 cm long and 1 cm apart at 1 kV, ions of 20 keV per charge are deflected 125 mrad and ions of 40 keV 62.5 mrad; on a screen 0.3 m beyond the middle of the plates (the exit ray points back to that middle), 37.5 mm against 18.75 mm. Double the energy, half the deflection.

Magnetic deflection. In a uniform field \(B\) the ion moves on a circle of radius \(\rho\) with \(B\rho = p/q\) (Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 2, §21), the magnetic rigidity of chapter 1, section 4.1: the force \(qvB\) (F4, section 10) supplies the centripetal force \(mv^2/\rho\). Here \(p = mv = \sqrt{2mW}\) depends on the mass. Momenta are quoted in MeV\(/c\) (F7, section 3), and for a singly charged particle \(B\rho\) in T m is 3.3356 times \(p\) in GeV\(/c\). For Hg\(^+\) at 1.26 MeV, \(p = 686\) MeV/\(c\) and \(B\rho = 3.3356\times0.686 = 2.29\) T m: a radius of 4.6 m in 0.5 T. A proton of the same energy has \(B\rho = 0.162\) T m. Heavy, slow ions are stiff, and magnetic analysis of them needs large magnets. Electric and magnetic deflection together give \(m/q\).

9. Wideroe’s other idea: the ray transformer

In the ray transformer, a magnet whose flux changes in time induces an electric field round a ring, \(\oint\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = -d\Phi_B/dt\), and the same magnet’s field at the ring bends the electrons into a circle. For the radius to stay fixed while the momentum grows, the field at the orbit must be half the average field inside it, the condition Wideroe derived (quoted here; chapter 11 derives it). His tube held the electrons for only about one and a half turns because nothing focused them. Kerst made the idea work at Illinois in 1940, and it became the betatron: chapter 11.

How it was researched and built

Wideroe’s tube, 1927 and 1928

Having failed with the ray transformer, Wideroe turned in the autumn of 1927 to Ising’s paper, which he had read in the library at Karlsruhe, and simplified it (Kaiser; Waloschek 1994). Instead of a spark circuit he used a valve oscillator, a modern transmitting valve giving a steady sinusoidal voltage. Instead of a row of electrodes he used one tube with two gaps: ions from a heated source fell through the first gap while the tube was at its most negative, coasted through the tube while the voltage reversed, and fell through the second gap as they left. Leaving the grounded exit tube, they passed between deflecting plates and struck a fluorescent screen or a photographic plate. From the deflection Wideroe showed the ions had twice the energy of one passage. A spark gap in the circuit served to calibrate the voltage, not to generate it (Heilbron and Seidel 1989, figure 2.14, from the translation in Livingston 1966, p. 102).

The two accounts consulted give different round numbers. Heilbron and Seidel describe 20 kV across each gap and ions of 40 keV. Kaiser describes an available voltage of about 25 kV and ions at the energy of about 50 kV. Both agree on the ions, sodium and potassium, and on the factor of two. Kaiser reports that the hardest part was an ion source that would work, and that once it did, Wideroe soon matched the ions’ initial speed, the oscillator’s voltage and frequency, and the lengths of the two accelerating paths. Neither account gives his frequency; Craddock (2010), citing Wideroe’s own memoir, gives a 1 MHz, 25 kV oscillator, and so do the textbooks of Wangler (2008, section 1.1) and Lee (chapter 1), with potassium ions at 50 keV. None of these is a record from 1927, so the 1 MHz is the figure later accounts agree on, not one seen in the dissertation. At 1 MHz, a drift tube for potassium after 20 kV would be 16 cm long, and for sodium after 25 kV 23 cm.

Wideroe did not think the method promising for his purpose. He wanted a high-voltage source for engineers, which he called a kinetic-voltage transformer, and the currents were so small that he saw no prospect of much more than a milliampere (Heilbron and Seidel). The dissertation, “Über ein neues Prinzip zur Herstellung hoher Spannungen” (on a new principle for the production of high voltages), carries both the failed ray transformer and the working linac. He received his doctorate on 28 November 1927 (Wideroe’s own chronology in Waloschek 1994; Kaiser gives 1928, but Heilbron and Seidel also put the dissertation in 1927) and in March 1928 took a job at AEG’s transformer works in Berlin; the paper appeared in the issue of Archiv für Elektrotechnik 21 dated 17 December 1928 (Heilbron and Seidel). On the same day, Leo Szilard applied in Berlin for a patent on a similar device with grids instead of tubes, at a planned \(10^8\) Hz; it was not granted (Heilbron and Seidel).

Lawrence reads the diagrams

The issue reached the Berkeley library on 23 January 1929 (Heilbron and Seidel). Lawrence came across it early in 1929. By his own account he could not read German easily, studied the diagrams and photographs, and estimated at once that a proton linac for more than 1 MeV would be some metres long, awkward for a laboratory. That estimate led him to bend the path into a spiral in a magnetic field: the cyclotron, chapter 10.

In Paris, Jean Thibaud, in Maurice de Broglie’s laboratory, accelerated positive ions through 11 gaps between cylindrical electrodes at \(3\times10^6\) Hz to 145 keV, and reported it in the Comptes rendus in 1932. He judged that alpha-particle energies would need a tube 10 m long and an oscillator of 100 kW, and he too turned to the cyclotron, which he later said he had made work in November 1930 (Heilbron and Seidel).

Sloan and Lawrence at Berkeley, 1930 and 1931

In the autumn of 1930 David Sloan, who had come to Berkeley from General Electric’s research laboratory on a Coffin fellowship, took up Wideroe’s linac while M. Stanley Livingston took up the cyclotron (Heilbron and Seidel, chapter 2, “Double Play”). The sequence:

  1. Eight electrodes. A 75-watt oscillator run at 11 kV, about five times its rating, gave mercury ions of 90 keV: eight gaps of about 11 kV.
  2. Twenty-one electrodes. Thirteen more gave 100 keV, then 200 keV, with less than 10 kV on the oscillator. Reported to the National Academy of Sciences in December 1930 and published in January 1931 as “The production of high speed canal rays without the use of high voltages” (Lawrence and Sloan, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 17, 64).
  3. Thirty electrodes. By the end of May 1931, more than 1 MeV with 0.01 μA. In the final form, with a commercial oscillator that could put up to 90 kV on an electrode, 42 kV at 30 m wavelength gave Hg\(^+\) at 1.26 MeV and \(10^{-7}\) A (Sloan and Lawrence, Physical Review 38, 2021, received 19 October 1931). That current is \(6.2\times10^{11}\) ions a second, a beam power of 0.13 W.

The work taught two things. Synchronization in the first short tubes had to be found by trial and error. And the beam was focused without any lens: the field bulging out of each gap pushes an ion towards the axis in the first half of the gap and away in the second, and an ion near the crest, being faster in the second half, spends less time being pushed out. The net effect focuses (Heilbron and Seidel). The abstract credits simple and effective methods of focusing and synchronizing for the results, and predicts 10 MeV ions as practical.

Sloan and Coates, 1932 to 1934

Sloan then planned 36 electrodes, each to hold 80 kV, driven by two oscillators at 27 m, expecting 4.5 MeV; 10 MeV would need a system about 40 feet long and eight power oscillators (Heilbron and Seidel). With the graduate student Wesley Coates he finished the 36-electrode tube. It gave Hg\(^+\) at 2.85 MeV with about \(10^{-8}\) A; the tube held 79 kV against 42 kV before (Sloan and Coates, Physical Review 46, 539, 1934). \(36 \times 79\) keV \(= 2.84\) MeV: again the full voltage per gap. The changes were the ones section 7 points to: less electrode heating through lower resistance and capacitance, electrodes 6 mm across instead of 13 mm, each 25 per cent longer, and gaps of 20 per cent of the electrode length.

Coates found soft X-rays from targets struck by the mercury ions (Heilbron and Seidel). But a beam of slow heavy ions could not enter nuclei, and from late 1931 Sloan’s main work was a resonance transformer and the X-ray tube that grew from it (Heilbron and Seidel, chapter 3). For protons the cyclotron won the 1930s.

How such a machine is designed today

The Wideroe structure is still the \(\beta\lambda/2\) structure. Humphries (1986, section 14.2) notes it operates at 1 to 10 MHz and still serves for the first acceleration of heavy ions, and gives the two reasons it cannot simply be run faster: at high frequency the gaps carry large displacement currents that load the generator, and the tubes radiate like dipole antennas: two conductors with charge sloshing between them at a frequency whose wavelength is not much longer than they are send electromagnetic waves out into the room (F4, section 17), and that power is lost. The cure, enclosing the gaps in a resonant tank, leads to the drift-tube linac of Alvarez and its relatives (chapter 14). The design steps are those of this chapter, done in order:

  1. Choose the frequency, the ion’s charge-to-mass ratio, the injection energy and the gap voltage.
  2. Choose the design phase on the rising slope (for example \(60^\circ\) in this course’s convention, \(-30^\circ\) in the linac convention), trading energy gain per gap against phase acceptance (chapter 12).
  3. Compute the fields of the gap geometry with an electromagnetic code (Humphries shows a SUPERFISH calculation), and from them \(T_t\) for each cell.
  4. Lay out cell lengths \(\beta_n\lambda/2\) from \(W_n\), cell by cell, with \(T_t\) updated as \(\beta\) grows (a cell is one tube with its gap, the repeating unit of the structure).
  5. Track a distribution of particles through the structure with a beam-dynamics code, transverse (across the axis) and longitudinal (along it, in energy and phase) together, and adjust.

At the bench

Setting and measuring the frequency

Radio practice of the time stated wavelength: Sloan and Lawrence give 30 m, not 10 MHz (the two are linked by \(f = c/\lambda\)). The usual instrument was an absorption wavemeter: a small LC circuit with a calibrated variable capacitor, loosely coupled to the oscillator, with a lamp or meter that peaks when its resonance matches. Its readings are only as good as its calibration, against a standard frequency. Lecher wires, which measure \(\lambda/2\) as the distance between voltage nodes on a parallel-wire line, are practical at short wavelengths; at 30 m the nodes are 15 m apart.

How precise must it be? From section 6, a frequency error \(\delta f/f\) shifts the phase by \(\pi\,\delta f/f\) per tube for the design particle, and the shift accumulates. Over the 29 drifts of a 30-gap machine, \(10^\circ\) of slip needs \(\delta f/f = 0.19\) per cent. In the model of section 6 (crest design), a frequency 0.2 per cent high leaves 5.6 per cent of the beam at full energy instead of 7.3; at 0.5 per cent, none. A valve oscillator drifts as it warms, so the operator retunes while watching the collector current.

The gap voltage matters as much. In the same model a gap voltage 5 per cent low leaves no ions at full energy: they arrive later and later. Five per cent high leaves 18 per cent, more than at the design voltage: the ions arrive earlier and slide down to the rising slope, near \(\sin\phi = 1/1.05\), \(\phi \approx 72^\circ\), where they gain the design energy per gap and are phase-stable (section 6). A spark gap, such as a sphere gap (chapter 4), can calibrate the voltage; Wideroe had a spark gap in his circuit for calibration.

Cutting the drift tubes

The table of section 3 is the cutting list, computed from the design energy at each tube: for Sloan’s parameters, from 1.01 cm for the first to 5.51 cm for the thirtieth. A length error \(\delta L\) shifts the phase by \(\pi\,\delta L/L\), because the drift time, and so the \(\pi\) of phase it is cut for, grows in proportion to the length. An error of 0.1 mm costs \(1.8^\circ\) on the first tube but \(0.33^\circ\) on the last; random errors of 0.1 mm on all 29 add (in quadrature, as the square root of the sum of squares, since independent errors partly cancel: F6, section 8) to \(3.6^\circ\), and 1 mm errors to \(36^\circ\). The short first tubes demand the precision. A uniform error, every tube 0.1 mm long, adds \(17^\circ\), but this one can be cancelled by retuning the frequency; random errors cannot.

Measuring the ions’ energy

Wideroe measured by electric deflection onto a fluorescent screen and a photographic plate: a spot that moves to half its deflection when the energy doubles (section 8). Because the deflection depends on \(W/q\), a spread of energies draws a streak; ions that crossed only one gap in phase, or crossed the second at a poor phase, fill the streak between the single-gap and double-gap marks. Neutral atoms, made when an ion picks up an electron from residual gas (charge exchange), go straight on undeflected. A magnetic analyser instead sorts by momentum, \(B\rho = \sqrt{2mW}/q\), separating isotopes and charge states as well; for MeV mercury ions it needs metres of radius at half a tesla.

The small captured fraction

The current at the collector is a small part of what leaves the source. Half of a continuous beam meets a retarding voltage at the first gap. Of the rest, only a band of phases stays in step. In the model, about 7 per cent of a continuous beam reaches full energy in a crest-tuned 30-gap machine, and about 10 per cent reaches nearly double energy in Wideroe’s two gaps; real machines lose more on the walls. Sloan’s collector read \(10^{-7}\) A at 1.26 MeV. The test that the ions are really resonant: switch off the oscillator, and the high-energy current vanishes; detune the frequency or voltage a few per cent, and it falls sharply while low-energy ions remain.

Pulsed or continuous

Ising’s spark circuit would have given pulses: a damped wave train after each spark, then nothing until the next. Wideroe’s and Sloan’s valve oscillators ran continuously (continuous wave), so the beam came out as a train of bunches, short clumps of ions, one for each RF cycle, 10 million times a second, and the electrodes and oscillator carried their full load all the time. That is why electrode heating mattered so much in the 1931 tube. Radar transmitters were pulsed: very high power for microseconds, a small fraction of the time. The postwar proton linacs took this over, high peak voltage at a low average power (chapter 13, chapter 14).

How it fails, and what it looks like

  • Sparking across a gap. A flash in the tube, the voltage collapses, the oscillator jumps in frequency or stops, and the beam vanishes. Rough or dirty electrode edges and high fields cause it (chapter 4).
  • Electrode heating. Displacement currents heat the tubes and leads; gas comes off them, and the pressure rises. The beam fades as gas scatters it. Sloan and Coates traced most of their gain over the 42 kV of 1931 to less of this heating.
  • Frequency drift. Nothing visible changes, but the full-energy current falls while the voltages read the same.
  • Wrong voltage. Too low and the ions slip later at every gap; the high-energy spot disappears. Retune.
  • Ion source failure. No beam at any setting. Wideroe found the source the hardest part of the whole experiment.
  • Poor vacuum. Charge exchange makes neutrals (an undeflected spot) and scattering broadens the beam.

Where it stops

The synchronism condition is the limit. At a fixed frequency the tube length grows with the speed, \(L = \beta\lambda/2\). For protons at 10 MHz, 1 MeV needs 0.69 m tubes and 10 MeV needs 2.2 m; with 100 kV gaps, reaching 10 MeV takes 100 gaps and 146 m, a mean gradient of 0.07 MV/m. And since the length of \(N\) tubes grows as \(N^{3/2}\), the gradient falls as \(1/\sqrt{N}\).

Raising the frequency shortens the tubes in proportion: at 200 MHz the same 10 MeV takes 7.3 m, with the last tube 11 cm. But a Wideroe structure cannot run there. The gaps’ displacement currents grow as \(\omega C\hat{V}\), and the tubes radiate as antennas (Humphries 1986, section 14.2). Two ideas get past it.

  1. Bend the path. Use one gap over and over, with a magnetic field bringing the particle back to it in step. That is Lawrence’s response in 1929, the cyclotron: chapter 10.
  2. Enclose the gaps. Put the drift tubes inside a resonant cavity that stores the energy and does not radiate, and drive it with the high-power, high-frequency sources built for radar. Alvarez’s proton linac at Berkeley, about 200 MHz, reached 31.5 MeV in 1947 (the design: Alvarez, Physical Review 70, 799, 1946; the machine: Alvarez and others, Review of Scientific Instruments 26, 111, 1955): chapter 13 and chapter 14.

Both also need what the 1931 machines lacked by design: phase stability, so that a bunch stays together over thousands of gaps, chapter 12. And a linac that is phase-stable sees a field rising during each gap crossing, which defocuses transversely: the conflict is resolved with external focusing in chapter 14.

Exercises

1. Sloan and Coates (1934) report 36 electrodes, 79 kV and Hg\(^+\) at 2.85 MeV. What energy per gap does that imply, and does it fit crest acceleration?

Answer

\(2.85\ \text{MeV}/36 = 79.2\) keV per gap. A singly charged ion at the crest gains \(e \times 79\) kV \(= 79\) keV per gap, and \(36 \times 79\) keV \(= 2.844\) MeV. The figures fit full-voltage gaps at the crest with \(T_t \approx 1\).

2. Craddock gives Wideroe’s oscillator as 1 MHz. Take that value. How long must the drift tube be for Na\(^+\) after a 25 kV gap, and for K\(^+\) after 20 kV? What frequency would make a 10 cm tube right for K\(^+\) at 20 keV?

Answer

\(v = \sqrt{2W/m}\). For \(^{23}\)Na\(^+\) at 25 keV, \(v = 4.58\times10^5\) m/s and \(L = v/2f = 22.9\) cm. For \(^{39}\)K\(^+\) at 20 keV, \(v = 3.15\times10^5\) m/s and \(L = 15.7\) cm. For \(L = 0.10\) m, \(f = v/2L = 1.57\) MHz.

3. Show that for \(W_0 \ll W_n\) the tube lengths grow as \(\sqrt{n}\), and check with the 1931 machine (Hg\(^+\), 42 kV, \(\lambda = 30\) m).

Answer

\(W_n = nq\hat{V}\sin\phi_s\) and \(L_n = v_n/2f = (1/2f)\sqrt{2W_n/m} = (1/2f)\sqrt{2q\hat{V}\sin\phi_s/m}\,\sqrt{n}\). For Hg\(^+\) (200.59 u) at the crest, \(L_1 = 1.006\) cm and \(L_{30} = 5.509\) cm; the ratio is 5.477, equal to \(\sqrt{30}\) to four figures. The relativistic correction is of order \(W/mc^2 \sim 10^{-5}\).

4. Compute \(T_t\) for a uniform gap with \(g/\beta\lambda = 0.1\), \(0.25\) and \(0.5\). For which gap length does the gap give no energy at all, and why?

Answer

\(T_t = \sin(\pi x)/(\pi x)\): 0.984, 0.900 and 0.637. It is zero at \(g = \beta\lambda\): the crossing takes one full period, and the field’s contributions over the period cancel whatever the phase.

5. A gap field has a Gaussian profile, \(\mathcal{E}_z \propto e^{-z^2/2\sigma^2}\). Derive \(T_t\) and evaluate it for \(\sigma = 0.1\beta\lambda\).

Answer

\(T_t = \int e^{-z^2/2\sigma^2}\cos(kz)\,dz / \int e^{-z^2/2\sigma^2}\,dz\) with \(k = 2\pi/\beta\lambda\). The Fourier transform of a Gaussian gives \(T_t = e^{-k^2\sigma^2/2} = \exp(-2\pi^2\sigma^2/(\beta\lambda)^2)\). For \(\sigma = 0.1\beta\lambda\), \(T_t = e^{-0.197} = 0.821\) (checked by numerical integration).

6. Show that electric deflection cannot tell sodium from potassium ions of the same energy per charge, but magnetic deflection can. What is \(B\rho\) of Hg\(^+\) at 1.26 MeV, and its radius in 0.5 T?

Answer

\(\theta_E = V_dl_p q/(2dW)\) contains no mass. \(B\rho = \sqrt{2mW}/q\) does: at the same \(W\), K\(^+\) has a radius \(\sqrt{38.96/22.99} = 1.30\) times that of Na\(^+\). For Hg\(^+\) at 1.26 MeV, \(p = 686\) MeV/\(c\), so \(B\rho = 3.3356 \times 0.686 = 2.29\) T m and \(\rho = 4.58\) m in 0.5 T.

7. Derive the phase slip per drift for a particle whose energy differs by \(\Delta W\) from the design, and the frequency tolerance that keeps the accumulated slip below \(10^\circ\) over 29 drifts.

Answer

The drift time is \(L/v\) and the design takes half a period. Then \(\Delta\phi = \omega_{RF}L/v - \pi = \pi(v_s/v - 1) \approx -(\pi/2)\Delta W/W_s\). A frequency error makes every drift last \(\pi(1 + \delta f/f)\) of phase for the design particle, a slip of \(\pi\,\delta f/f\) per drift. For \(29\pi\,\delta f/f < 10^\circ = 0.1745\) rad, \(\delta f/f < 0.0019\): 0.19 per cent, or 19 kHz at 10 MHz.

8. Protons from rest to 10 MeV with 100 kV gaps, crest acceleration: how long is the last tube and the whole machine at 10 MHz and at 200 MHz?

Answer

100 gaps. At 10 MeV, \(\beta = 0.145\), so the last tube is \(\beta\lambda/2 = 2.17\) m at 10 MHz and 0.109 m at 200 MHz. Summing \(\beta_n\lambda/2\) over \(n = 1\) to 100 gives 146 m at 10 MHz and 7.3 m at 200 MHz: mean gradients of 0.068 and 1.37 MV/m.

9. A Wideroe structure at 10 MHz presents 50 pF and must hold 42 kV. Find the coil inductance, the peak current, the stored energy, and the power for \(Q_0 = 200\). What is \(R_{sh}\) in the linac definition?

Answer

\(L = 1/(\omega^2C) = 5.07\) μH. \(I = \omega C\hat{V} = 132\) A. \(U = \tfrac{1}{2}C\hat{V}^2 = 0.0441\) J. \(P = \omega U/Q_0 = 13.9\) kW. \(R_p = Q_0/(\omega C) = 63.7\) kΩ, so \(R_{sh} = \hat{V}^2/P = 2R_p = 127\) kΩ. The bandwidth is \(f_0/Q_0 = 50\) kHz.

  • G. Ising, “Prinzip einer Methode zur Herstellung von Kanalstrahlen hoher Voltzahl”, Arkiv för Matematik, Astronomi och Fysik 18 (30), 1–4 (1924). English translation in M. S. Livingston (ed.), The Development of High-Energy Accelerators (Dover, New York, 1966), pp. 88–90.
  • R. Wideröe, “Über ein neues Prinzip zur Herstellung hoher Spannungen”, Archiv für Elektrotechnik 21, 387–406 (1928), doi:10.1007/BF01656341. Translated as “A new principle for the generation of high voltages” in Livingston (1966), pp. 92–114.
  • R. Wideröe, “Some memories and dreams from the childhood of particle accelerators”, Europhysics News 15 (2), 9 (1984) (cited from Heilbron and Seidel and from Waloschek; the volume, issue and page also in Chao and Tigner’s Handbook, section 1.6.3, reference 1; not read).
  • E. O. Lawrence and D. H. Sloan, “The production of high speed canal rays without the use of high voltages”, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 17 (1), 64–70 (1931) (free: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1075996/).
  • D. H. Sloan and E. O. Lawrence, “The production of heavy high speed ions without the use of high voltages”, Physical Review 38, 2021 (1931), doi:10.1103/PhysRev.38.2021. The 42 kV, 30 m, 30 tubes, 1.26 MeV and \(10^{-7}\) A.
  • D. H. Sloan and W. M. Coates, “Recent advances in the production of heavy high speed ions without the use of high voltages”, Physical Review 46, 539 (1934), doi:10.1103/PhysRev.46.539. The 36 electrodes, 79 kV, 2.85 MeV.
  • J. Thibaud, “Production d’ions positifs de vitesse élevée par accélérations multiples”, Comptes rendus 194, 360 (1932) (cited from Heilbron and Seidel; not read).
  • L. W. Alvarez, “The design of a proton linear accelerator”, Physical Review 70, 799 (1946) (cited from Humphries, section 14.2, and Wangler, section 1.1, reference 8; not read).
  • L. W. Alvarez, H. Bradner, J. Franck, H. Gordon, J. D. Gow, L. C. Marshall, F. F. Oppenheimer, W. K. H. Panofsky, C. Richman and J. R. Woodyard, “Berkeley proton linear accelerator”, report UCRL-236 (revised, October 1953) (abstract: https://www.osti.gov/biblio/4375045), published in Review of Scientific Instruments 26, 111–133 (1955), doi:10.1063/1.1771253. The 31.5 MeV and the 202.5 MHz.
  • E. O. Lawrence, “The evolution of the cyclotron”, Nobel lecture, 11 December 1951 (free: https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/lawrence-lecture.pdf). His reading of Wideroe and his tribute to Ising.

Histories:

  • J. L. Heilbron and R. W. Seidel, Lawrence and His Laboratory: A History of the Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory, vol. I (University of California Press, 1989), chapter 2, sections “Resonance Acceleration” and “Double Play” (free: https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/public/book/lawrence-and-his-laboratory-a-history-of-the-lawrence-berkeley-laboratory-volume-i.html). Ising’s scheme, Wideroe’s 20 kV and 40 keV, the \(10^7\) Hz limit, caesium, Szilard, Thibaud, the Sloan sequence and the focusing.
  • W. Kaiser, “Rolf Wideröe, Doktorand Walter Rogowskis und Erbauer des ersten Linearbeschleunigers”, RWTH Themen (RWTH Aachen) (free, archived: https://web.archive.org/web/20070617061426/http://www.histech.rwth-aachen.de/content/1585/Rolf_Wideroe.pdf). Karlsruhe, Gaede, Rogowski, the failed ray transformer, about 25 kV and 50 keV, Na and K, the ion source, AEG. It gives the degree as 28 November 1928; Wideroe’s own chronology (Waloschek, below) gives 1927.
  • P. Waloschek (ed.), The Infancy of Particle Accelerators: Life and Work of Rolf Wideröe (Vieweg, Braunschweig and Wiesbaden, 1994; also DESY report 94-039) (free: https://www-library.desy.de/elbooks/wideroe/WiE-BOOK.htm). Wideroe’s own account, chapter 3, and the chronological survey: Ising’s paper read at Karlsruhe, Ising’s delay lines, kicks of up to 25 kV and ions at 50 kV, the degree of 28 November 1927, AEG from March 1928, the notebooks of 1923 to 1928.
  • M. K. Craddock, “Eighty years of cyclotrons”, Proceedings of Cyclotrons 2010, Lanzhou, paper MOM1CIO02 (free: https://proceedings.jacow.org/Cyclotrons2010/papers/mom1cio02.pdf). Wideroe’s 1 MHz, 25 kV oscillator and ions of about 50 keV, citing Waloschek (1994), p. 41.
  • Wikipedia, “Triode”, “Electronic oscillator” and “Hartley oscillator” (free), for the dates of the Audion, the high-vacuum triode and the feedback oscillator; “Gustaf Ising” for his dates. Secondary sources, used only for dates.

Textbooks:

  • M. S. Livingston and J. P. Blewett, Particle Accelerators (McGraw-Hill, 1962): section 10-1, “Early designs” (pp. 312–313), for Wideroe’s three coaxial electrodes with K\(^+\) and Na\(^+\) at twice the single-gap energy, and Sloan and Lawrence’s built tube (30 drift tubes, the first of effective length 1 cm, 1.14 m overall, 10 megacycles or 30 m, 42,000 V peak, 1.26 MeV Hg\(^+\), energy measured by electrostatic deflection) and Coates’s soft X-rays; section 7-1 for Kerst’s 2.3 MeV betatron at Illinois in 1940; chapter 1 for Rutherford’s 1927 Royal Society speech (the words “copious supply” are not quoted there); the references of chapter 9 for Veksler (1944) and McMillan (1945). It dates Ising’s proposal 1925; the paper itself is in volume 18 of 1924, as Wangler also gives.
  • T. P. Wangler, RF Linear Accelerators (2nd edition, Wiley-VCH, 2008): section 1.1 (pp. 3–5) for Ising’s spark-gap pulses and delay lines, Wideroe’s demonstration at Aachen in 1927 with 25 kV at 1 MHz and K\(^+\) to 50 keV, and Sloan and Lawrence’s 30 tubes, 42 kV, 10 MHz, 1.26 MeV; section 4.2 for the \(\beta\lambda/2\) cell of the Wideroe structure. It gives Sloan and Lawrence’s current as 1 μA; their abstract gives \(10^{-7}\) A, used here.
  • S. Y. Lee, Accelerator Physics: chapter 1, section on the linac, and chapter 3, section VIII.1, which also give Wideroe 1 MHz, 25 kV and potassium at 50 keV. Its figures for the 1931 machine (45 kV in one place, about 7 MHz in another) disagree with the primary paper and were not used.
  • S. Humphries, Principles of Charged Particle Acceleration (Wiley, 1986) (free: https://www.fieldp.com/cpa.html): section 12.1 (complex notation and impedance), 12.2 (the lumped-circuit model of a resonator), 12.6 (transmission lines), 14.2 (the Wideroe accelerator and the \(\beta\lambda/2\) structure), 14.4 (the transit-time factor).
  • L. D. Landau and E. M. Lifshitz, Mechanics (Course of Theoretical Physics, vol. 1, 3rd edition): §25 (damped oscillations) and §26 (forced oscillations under friction), the mathematics of the resonator and its \(Q_0\).
  • L. D. Landau and E. M. Lifshitz, The Classical Theory of Fields (vol. 2, 4th edition): §17 (equations of motion of a charge in a field) and §21 (motion in a constant uniform magnetic field).
