07 Ion sources and electron guns

1886 to today: from Goldstein’s canal rays and Richardson’s hot wires to the Pierce gun, the caesiated H⁻ source, the ECR source and the RF photoinjector; the wall is getting many particles out of matter and into a narrow, cold beam.

A note on symbols in this chapter. \(\mathcal{E}\) is an electric field in V/m, as everywhere in the course, and \(Q\) a bunch charge in C (not a quality factor). \(T\) is a temperature, as everywhere in the course; \(T_e\) and \(T_i\) are the electron and ion temperatures of a plasma, usually given as \(k_B T_e\) in eV. \(\phi_w\) is a work function in eV. \(J\) is a current density in A/m², \(d\) an electrode gap, \(K\) the perveance. \(\phi\) and \(\varphi\) are electrostatic potentials in V (no RF phase appears in this chapter). \(M\) is the mass of an ion. \(n\) is a number density (no field index appears). \(\sigma_x\) and \(\sigma_{p_x}\) are rms spreads of position and transverse momentum.

In one page

The wall. An accelerator can only accelerate what its source gives it. The source sets the current, and it sets the beam’s quality for good: no later magnet can make a beam narrower and more parallel at the same time than it was born. Hot cathodes give a few amperes per square centimetre at a temperature of 0.1 to 0.2 eV; plasmas give ions with a spread of energies and a boundary that moves with the plasma’s density; and space charge pushes a dense beam apart before it has energy enough to resist.

The idea. Treat the beam as a cloud of points in position and angle. Its area there, the emittance, can be kept but not reduced (Liouville). So a source is judged by its brightness, current per emittance squared, and a source designer’s work is to get many particles out at a low temperature and accelerate them before space charge spoils them. For electrons that led from hot wires to the Pierce gun and then to laser-lit cathodes in RF cavities at 100 MV/m. For ions it led to dense plasmas held by magnetic fields, with an extraction gap shaped to the plasma’s edge.

Equations to remember.

\[ \varepsilon_n = \sigma_x\sqrt{\frac{k_B T}{m c^2}}, \qquad \varepsilon_n = \sigma_x\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega - \phi_{\mathrm{eff}}}{3 m c^2}} \]

the rms normalised emittance (m rad) of a thermionic cathode at temperature \(T\) and of a photocathode lit by photons of energy \(\hbar\omega\), for a spot of rms size \(\sigma_x\).

\[ J = A_G T^2 e^{-\phi_w/k_B T}, \qquad J_{CL} = \frac{4\varepsilon_0}{9}\sqrt{\frac{2q}{m}}\,\frac{V^{3/2}}{d^2} \]

Richardson’s law for emission from a hot surface (\(A_G \approx 0.6\times10^6\) A m⁻² K⁻² for tungsten) and the space-charge limit across a gap \(d\) at voltage \(V\).

\[ \lambda_D = \sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_0 k_B T_e}{n e^2}}, \qquad J_{Bohm} \approx 0.61\, n e \sqrt{\frac{k_B T_e}{M}} \]

the Debye length of a plasma and the ion current density it can give up across its sheath.

\[ B_{ECR} = \frac{2\pi f_{RF}\, m_e}{e} \]

the field at which electrons gyrate in step with microwaves of frequency \(f_{RF}\).

Numbers worth knowing. \(k_B T = 0.1\) eV at 1160 K. A cathode at that temperature has \(\varepsilon_n/\sigma_x = 0.44\) µm per mm (1 µm = 1 mm mrad); at 2500 K, 0.65 µm per mm. The Child-Langmuir constant is 2.33 µA/V\(^{3/2}\) for electrons and 42.9 times less for protons. ECR: 87.5 mT at 2.45 GHz, 1.00 T at 28 GHz. A copper photocathode has a quantum efficiency of a few \(10^{-5}\); caesiated GaAs, a few per cent. The LCLS gun: 1 nC, 1.2 µm, 115 MV/m on the cathode. Accelerator H⁻ sources give 45 to 100 mA at 0.07 to 1.6 A/cm² with normalised rms emittances of 0.2 to 0.4 µm.

The wall

The first sources were discharge tubes. In 1886 Eugen Goldstein bored holes in the cathode of a discharge tube and saw faint rays stream backwards through them, away from the anode. He called them Kanalstrahlen, canal rays. They were positive ions of the gas, made in the discharge and pulled through the holes. Wilhelm Wien and then J. J. Thomson deflected them with fields; Thomson’s book Rays of Positive Electricity (1913; second edition 1921) made the canal-ray tube an instrument for weighing ions, a forerunner of the mass spectrograph. As a source for an accelerator the tube had two faults. The discharge needs gas at a pressure that a beam cannot cross, and the current through a small hole is microamperes.

Electrons had an easier start. Thomas Edison met the effect while studying the blackening of his lamp bulbs from 1880, and in 1883 measured a current from the hot filament to an extra plate and patented it. Owen Richardson measured in 1901 how that current rises with temperature, and won the 1928 Nobel Prize for it. Arthur Wehnelt found in 1904 that oxides on a metal emit at far lower temperatures; his cylinder around the cathode (1902 to 1903) still shapes electron guns. Then a second limit appeared. Clement Child in 1911 and Irving Langmuir in 1913 showed that the electrons already in a gap repel those leaving the cathode, so the current is capped at \(J \propto V^{3/2}/d^2\) however hot the cathode is (chapter 2). The emitted electrons also leave with thermal motion, a fraction of an eV, and the space charge pushes them sideways. A dense beam spreads.

The builders of the first accelerators needed protons, deuterons and alpha particles by the milliampere, with little gas escaping into the accelerating tube (chapter 5). They tried geometries borrowed from the electron tube and the magnetron. Louis Maxwell at the Bartol Research Foundation of the Franklin Institute published in 1931 (submitted late in 1930) a source of hydrogen ions in what is now called the Penning geometry. E. S. Lamar and O. Luhr at MIT built a proton source with a hot cathode in the early 1930s (Faircloth counts it a magnetron; Livingston and Blewett describe a hot-cathode arc inside a large anode cylinder), and in 1934 Stanley Van Voorhis’s team at Princeton used one as the proton source of an accelerator. Cyclotrons ionised gas at the centre of the dees with an electron stream from a filament (chapter 10). In 1939 M. Stanley Livingston, M. G. Holloway and C. P. Baker at Cornell published a capillary ion source for the cyclotron, in which the arc runs in a narrow capillary.

Three later walls shaped the modern sources. First, the synchrotrons and linacs of the 1950s to 1970s wanted ever more current. Rings that stack protons by charge exchange need H⁻ ions (chapter 21), and until the 1970s H⁻ sources gave currents orders of magnitude below what later became routine. Second, heavy-ion machines wanted ions with many electrons removed, because the energy a field gives an ion is proportional to its charge state. An arc in a gas removes mostly one or two electrons. Third, free-electron lasers of the 1980s wanted electron bunches with hundreds of amperes of peak current and emittances of a few micrometres. J. S. Fraser, R. L. Sheffield, E. R. Gray and G. W. Rodenz at Los Alamos set out the problem in 1985. A dispenser cathode gives less than 10 A/cm². Its thermal emittance alone limits the normalised brightness to \(4.1\times10^{9}\) A m⁻² rad⁻² per A/cm², in their convention. The usual cure, a gun making long pulses followed by subharmonic bunchers to squeeze them, diluted the phase space, and every injector in their survey fell short of the \(3.2\times10^{10}\) A m⁻² rad⁻² an ultraviolet FEL needed.

The physics

What a source must give

A source is judged by four numbers.

  • Current, \(I\) in A, or for a pulsed source the peak current and the charge per pulse.
  • Energy spread, the rms spread of kinetic energy at the exit, in eV. It matters for the first bending magnet and for the first RF cavity (chapter 13).
  • Emittance, the area the beam occupies in position and angle, defined below.
  • Brightness, the current divided by the product of the two transverse emittances.

Behind these sit the practical numbers: the species and charge state, the gas load the source puts on the vacuum (chapter 3), the duty factor (the fraction of the time the beam is on: 1 ms pulses at 50 Hz are a duty factor of 5 per cent), and the lifetime between services.

Pre-requisite: Matrices and determinants

Pre-requisite. This section teaches the little of matrices that phase space needs: a \(2\times2\) matrix as a rule that turns one pair of numbers into another, how to apply two rules in turn (the matrix product, whose order matters), the transpose, and the determinant, which is the factor by which a rule multiplies areas. The next pre-requisite uses the determinant to show that drifts and lenses keep the area a beam fills; the one after uses the product and the transpose to follow a beam’s averages through a beam line. Part 0 has not used matrices, so this starts from the components of vectors in F2, section 1 and the cross product in F2, section 3. Chapter 16 builds whole beam lines from such matrices.

A matrix is a linear rule. Describe a particle by its offset \(x\) from the axis (m) and its angle \(x'\) to the axis (rad), the pair this chapter calls a point in phase space. Many parts of a beam line take that pair to a new one by multiplying and adding:

\[ x_1 = a\,x + b\,x', \qquad x_1' = c\,x + d\,x', \]

with four constants \(a\), \(b\), \(c\), \(d\) set by the element. Put the four constants in a square array, a matrix \(M\), and the pair in a column \(X\):

\[ \begin{pmatrix}x_1\\ x_1'\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}a & b\\ c & d\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\ x'\end{pmatrix}, \qquad\text{or}\qquad X_1 = MX. \]

The rule for reading it: each row of the matrix, times the column entry by entry, added up, gives one entry of the answer. A straight drift of length \(L\) (m) keeps the angle and adds \(L x'\) to the offset, so \(a = 1\), \(b = L\), \(c = 0\), \(d = 1\). The units work because \(b\) is in metres and \(x'\) in radians.

Example. A particle at \(x = 1\) mm with \(x' = 0.5\) mrad drifts 2 m. Then \(x_1 = 1\ \mathrm{mm} + 2000\ \mathrm{mm}\times0.0005 = 2\) mm and \(x_1' = 0.5\) mrad.

Two rules in turn: the product. Apply \(M\) and then a second matrix \(N = \begin{pmatrix}p & q\\ r & s\end{pmatrix}\). Substituting the first rule into the second gives a single rule, the product \(NM\):

\[ \begin{pmatrix}p & q\\ r & s\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a & b\\ c & d\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}pa + qc & pb + qd\\ ra + sc & rb + sd\end{pmatrix}. \]

Each entry is a row of the left matrix times a column of the right one. The element the particle meets first stands on the right, next to the column it acts on. The order matters: a lens followed by a drift is not a drift followed by a lens, and \(NM\) is generally not \(MN\). Exercise T2 shows it with numbers.

The transpose. Swapping a matrix’s rows with its columns gives its transpose, \(M^T = \begin{pmatrix}a & c\\ b & d\end{pmatrix}\). The transpose of a column is a row, \(X^T = (x\ \ x')\). A column times a row, by the same rule, is a \(2\times2\) matrix of all the products:

\[ XX^T = \begin{pmatrix}x\\ x'\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x & x'\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}x^2 & xx'\\ x'x & x'^2\end{pmatrix}. \]

Writing out both sides shows that \((MX)^T = X^TM^T\): transposing a product reverses its order. A matrix equal to its own transpose, like \(XX^T\), is called symmetric.

The determinant is an area factor. The determinant of \(M\) is the number

\[ \det M = ad - bc. \]

Here is what it means. \(M\) sends the point \((1, 0)\) to \((a, c)\) and the point \((0, 1)\) to \((b, d)\): the two columns. So the unit square, with sides along \((1, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\), becomes the parallelogram with sides \((a, c)\) and \((b, d)\) (Figure 7.1). The area of a parallelogram is the size of the cross product of its sides; treating the sides as vectors \((a, c, 0)\) and \((b, d, 0)\), the cross product is \((0, 0, ad - bc)\) (F2, section 3). So the square of area 1 becomes a parallelogram of area \(|ad - bc|\). Because the rule is linear, every small square anywhere in the plane is changed in the same way, and any region, built from small squares, has its area multiplied by \(|\det M|\). (A negative determinant means the map also flips the region over, as a mirror does.)

Figure 7.1. A matrix sends the unit square (blue) to the parallelogram (red) whose sides are its two columns. Left: a general matrix, whose determinant 1.375 is the parallelogram’s area. Right: a drift, which slides the top edge sideways by \(Lx'\) and leaves the area 1.

Two facts follow. Doing two maps in turn multiplies areas twice, so \(\det(NM) = \det N\,\det M\). And \(\det M^T = ad - cb = \det M\).

Example. \(\det\begin{pmatrix}1.5 & 0.5\\ 0.25 & 1\end{pmatrix} = 1.5 - 0.125 = 1.375\): every area grows by 37.5 per cent. The drift has \(\det = 1\times1 - L\times0 = 1\): it shears the plane, sliding each row of points sideways in proportion to its height, and keeps every area. So does \(\begin{pmatrix}2 & 0\\ 0 & 1/2\end{pmatrix}\), which doubles every offset and halves every angle.

Exercise T1. Find the determinant of \(\begin{pmatrix}2 & 3\\ 1 & 4\end{pmatrix}\). A region of the \((x, x')\) plane of area 1 mm mrad goes through this map. What is its area afterwards?

Answer

\(\det = 2\times4 - 3\times1 = 5\), so the area becomes 5 mm mrad. (No element built from fields does this to a beam’s phase space; the next pre-requisite says why.)

Exercise T2. A thin lens of focal length 1 m has matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ -1\ \mathrm{m^{-1}} & 1\end{pmatrix}\) and a 1 m drift has \(\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\ \mathrm{m}\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\). Multiply them in both orders, and follow a particle that starts at \(x = 1\) mm, \(x' = 0\) through each.

Answer

Lens first, then drift: drift times lens \(= \begin{pmatrix}1 - 1 & 1\\ -1 & 1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ -1 & 1\end{pmatrix}\) (in m and m⁻¹). The particle leaves at \(x = 0\), \(x' = -1\) mrad: the lens aimed it at the axis, and it reaches the axis one focal length later. Drift first, then lens: lens times drift \(= \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ -1 & -1 + 1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ -1 & 0\end{pmatrix}\), and the particle leaves at \(x = 1\) mm, \(x' = -1\) mrad. Different orders, different results; both determinants are 1.

Exercise T3. A map multiplies every offset by 3 and every angle by \(1/3\). Write its matrix and its determinant. What does it do to a beam 1 mm wide with angles of 3 mrad?

Answer

\(\begin{pmatrix}3 & 0\\ 0 & 1/3\end{pmatrix}\), determinant \(3\times\tfrac13 = 1\). The beam becomes 3 mm wide with angles of 1 mrad: wider and more parallel, with the same area in phase space. A telescope of lenses does this. It trades width for angle; it does not make the beam better.

Pre-requisite: Phase space and Liouville’s theorem

Pre-requisite. This section gives you phase space, Hamilton’s equations seen as a flow through it, and Liouville’s theorem: the density of particles is constant along the flow, so the volume a beam fills cannot change, and when the planes are uncoupled each plane’s area is kept on its own. It also sorts the forces that obey the theorem from those that do not. The next section uses it to make emittance a property of the source. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §40 and §46; Tong, Classical Mechanics, section 10.2; and Wangler, RF Linear Accelerators, sections 9.1 and 9.5.

Reminder: chapter 1 built the Hamiltonian of a charged particle, and its canonical momentum \(\mathbf{p} + q\mathbf{A}\), for one particle. Here we follow a whole beam of them. F3, section 9 already drew one oscillator as a point going round a circle in the plane of \(x\) and \(\dot x/\omega\); phase space is that plane, grown to hold every coordinate.

Phase space. To predict where a particle goes, you need its position and its momentum now. For one particle that is six numbers, \((x, p_x, y, p_y, z, p_z)\), positions in m and momenta in kg m/s. Treat them as the coordinates of a single point in a six-dimensional space, called phase space. As time passes the point moves. A beam of \(N\) particles is \(N\) points: a cloud that moves and changes shape. Beam particles move, to a first approximation, independently in the same fields, so one shared six-dimensional space holds them all.

Hamilton’s equations as a flow. Take one coordinate and its momentum, and a Hamiltonian \(H(x, p_x, t)\) in joules. Hamilton’s equations are

\[ \dot{x} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_x}, \qquad \dot{p}_x = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}. \]

At every point of the \((x, p_x)\) plane they give a velocity \((\dot x, \dot p_x)\). So the plane is filled with a velocity field, like flowing water, and every particle is carried along by it.

Liouville’s theorem. Mark a small rectangle in the plane, with corners at \((x, p_x)\) and \((x + dx, p_x + dp_x)\) and area \(dA = dx\,dp_x\). Let it flow for a short time \(dt\). Its left edge moves in \(x\) by \(\dot x\,dt\). Its right edge moves by \([\dot x + (\partial\dot x/\partial x)\,dx]\,dt\), a little more or less. So its width becomes \(dx\,[1 + (\partial\dot x/\partial x)\,dt]\). In the same way its height becomes \(dp_x\,[1 + (\partial\dot p_x/\partial p_x)\,dt]\). The rectangle also shears into a parallelogram, but a shear does not change area to first order in \(dt\). So, to first order,

\[ dA' = dA\left[1 + \left(\frac{\partial\dot x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\dot p_x}{\partial p_x}\right)dt\right]. \]

Now put in Hamilton’s equations:

\[ \frac{\partial\dot x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\dot p_x}{\partial p_x} = \frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial x\,\partial p_x} - \frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial p_x\,\partial x} = 0, \]

because the order of two partial derivatives does not matter (F1, section 9). So \(dA' = dA\). Any region of the plane, built from such rectangles, moves and deforms but keeps its area. In six dimensions the same sum runs over the three pairs, each pair cancels by itself, and the six-dimensional volume is kept. That is Liouville’s theorem.

The density is constant. Follow the particles inside a small region. Two particles at different points never come to the same point, because the motion from any point is unique; so the particles inside the region’s boundary stay inside it. Their number is fixed and the region’s volume is fixed, so their number per unit volume of phase space is fixed. If \(f\) is that density, an observer riding with any particle sees \(f\) unchanged: \(df/dt = 0\). The next pre-requisite turns this into an equation for \(f\).

When each plane is kept separately. Suppose the Hamiltonian splits into a sum, \(H = H_x(x, p_x) + H_y(y, p_y) + H_z(z, p_z)\). Then the motion in each plane depends on that plane alone, and the argument above holds for each plane’s area by itself. This is the uncoupled case. Drifts and quadrupoles treat \(x\) and \(y\) separately (to first order), so the area in \((x, p_x)\) and the area in \((y, p_y)\) are each kept. A solenoid couples \(x\) and \(y\), since it rotates the beam about its axis; then only the four-dimensional volume of \((x, p_x, y, p_y)\) is kept, and each plane’s projected area can grow or shrink.

Which forces obey it. The theorem needs a Hamiltonian. These forces have one:

  • electric fields, static or varying in time, including the RF fields of cavities (a field that varies in time needs the vector potential \(\mathbf{A}\) as well as \(\phi\), but it still has a Hamiltonian);
  • magnetic fields, provided the momentum is the canonical one, \(p_x = \gamma m v_x + qA_x\);
  • the beam’s own space charge, when it is treated as a smooth field made by all the particles together (the next pre-requisite).

These do not:

  • collisions with gas atoms or with individual neighbours, which give random kicks;
  • emission of radiation, which carries momentum away;
  • stripping and charge exchange, which change the particle itself (an H⁻ ion becomes a proton, and its Hamiltonian changes at a stroke);
  • deliberate cooling, which takes heat out of the beam’s random motion.

So Liouville’s theorem does not say that emittance can never change. It says that only processes of the second kind can shrink it.

Angles instead of momenta. Accelerator physicists plot the angle \(x' = p_x/p_z\) rather than \(p_x\). At fixed \(p_z\) this only rescales the axis: an area in \((x, x')\) is the area in \((x, p_x)\) divided by \(p_z\), and it too is kept. During acceleration \(p_z\) grows. The area in \((x, p_x)\) stays the same, so the area in \((x, x')\) shrinks as \(1/p_z\), that is as \(1/(\beta\gamma)\). This is why the next section defines a normalised emittance.

At work: a drift and a lens. Through a field-free drift of length \(L\) (m), \(x \to x + Lx'\) and \(x' \to x'\). Through a thin lens of focal length \(f_L\) (m), \(x \to x\) and \(x' \to x' - x/f_L\). Both are shears, and both maps have determinant 1:

\[ \det\begin{pmatrix}1 & L\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} = 1, \qquad \det\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ -1/f_L & 1\end{pmatrix} = 1. \]

The determinant of a linear map is the factor by which it multiplies areas (the previous pre-requisite), so both keep area, as Liouville requires. A beam of rms width 1 mm and rms angle 1 mrad, with no correlation between them, is \(\sqrt{1^2 + (1000 \times 0.001)^2} = 1.41\) mm wide after a 1 m drift: wider, and now correlated (the outer particles are the ones moving outward), but no larger in area.

Figure 7.2. The rms ellipse of the beam in the text, 1 mm by 1 mrad (blue), after a 1 m drift (red) and then a thin lens of 1 m focal length (dashed). The drift tilts and stretches the ellipse, so the beam is 1.41 mm wide; the lens shears it the other way. All three ellipses have the same area, \(\pi\) mm mrad.

At work: acceleration. A gun gives 100 keV electrons (\(\gamma = 1.196\), \(\beta\gamma = 0.656\)) to a linac that takes them to 10 MeV (\(\beta\gamma = 20.5\)). The area in \((x, p_x)\) does not change, so the area in \((x, x')\) falls by \(20.5/0.656 = 31\). The beam’s angles look 31 times better, with no gain in quality.

At work: canonical momentum on a cathode. Inside a solenoid with field \(B\) along the axis, the vector potential is azimuthal, \(A_\theta = Br/2\) at radius \(r\). An electron leaving a cathode at rest at \(r = 1\) mm in \(B = 1\) mT has no mechanical momentum, but its canonical momentum around the axis is \(eA_\theta = eBr/2 = 8.0\times10^{-26}\) kg m/s. A thermal spread of 0.1 eV gives \(\sqrt{m_e k_B T} = 1.2\times10^{-25}\) kg m/s (the next sections show why). The two are comparable. When the beam leaves the field, the canonical momentum becomes real rotation, and the phase-space area that Liouville protects includes it. This is why guns keep the field on the cathode near zero (chapter 2, section 9 works it out as Busch’s theorem).

Exercise L1. Show that a thin lens followed by a drift keeps area.

Answer

Apply the lens first, then the drift. The combined matrix is the drift matrix times the lens matrix:

\[ \begin{pmatrix}1 & L\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ -1/f_L & 1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 - L/f_L & L\\ -1/f_L & 1\end{pmatrix}. \]

Its determinant is \((1 - L/f_L)\times 1 - L\times(-1/f_L) = 1\). The determinant of a product is the product of the determinants, so any chain of drifts and thin lenses has determinant 1.

Exercise L2. A 50 keV proton source gives an emittance (area/\(\pi\) in \((x, x')\)) of 20 mm mrad. What is the normalised emittance, and what is the emittance in \((x, x')\) after an RFQ (a radio-frequency quadrupole, the first linac after most ion sources, which focuses, bunches and accelerates a slow beam at once; chapter 14) has taken the beam to 3 MeV?

Answer

At 50 keV, \(\gamma = 1 + 0.05/938.272 = 1.0000533\) and \(\beta\gamma = \sqrt{\gamma^2 - 1} = 0.01032\). The normalised emittance is \(\varepsilon_n = \beta\gamma\varepsilon = 0.01032 \times 20 = 0.206\) mm mrad (0.206 µm). At 3 MeV, \(\beta\gamma = 0.0800\), so \(\varepsilon = 0.206/0.0800 = 2.58\) mm mrad, assuming no growth in the RFQ. A real RFQ adds some.

Exercise L3. Which of these can reduce the emittance of a beam: a solenoid, a quadrupole triplet, scattering on residual gas, an electron cooler?

Answer

Only the cooler. The solenoid and the quadrupoles are Hamiltonian, so they keep the four-dimensional volume (the solenoid can move area between the \(x\) and \(y\) planes, which a later solenoid can move back). Gas scattering is not Hamiltonian, but its random kicks spread the cloud: it raises the emittance. A cooler removes random motion, which no field can do. Charge-exchange injection into a ring (chapter 21) is the other well-known escape: because stripping is not Hamiltonian, new particles can be laid into phase space already occupied by circulating ones.

Pre-requisite: Distribution functions, Vlasov equation, beam temperature

Pre-requisite. This section describes a beam by a distribution function in phase space and gives the Vlasov equation that carries it forward. It defines the second moments \(\langle x^2\rangle\), \(\langle x'^2\rangle\) and \(\langle xx'\rangle\), shows why linear optics keeps the rms emittance exactly, and shows how nonlinear forces keep the true area while the rms area grows (filamentation). It ends with the transverse temperature of a beam, \(\langle p_x^2\rangle = m k_B T\). The next section uses all four. Taught from Tong, Classical Mechanics, section 10.2.1 (Liouville’s equation); Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §46; and Wangler, sections 9.2, 9.4 and 9.5.

The distribution function. A real beam has \(10^9\) particles or more, too many to follow one by one. Describe it instead by a density in phase space, \(f(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{p}, t)\), defined so that the number of particles in a small box of volume \(d^3x\,d^3p\) is \(f\,d^3x\,d^3p\). It is a probability density in the sense of F6, section 4, in six variables at once and counting particles rather than chances. For one transverse plane accelerator physicists use \(f(x, x', s)\): the number per unit area of the \((x, x')\) plane, as a function of the distance \(s\) (m) along the beam line. Using \(s\) instead of \(t\) suits a beam line, since every particle passes every \(s\).

The Vlasov equation. The pre-requisite on phase space showed that \(f\) is constant along each particle’s path. Write that out with the chain rule for several variables (F1, section 9: a small change of each variable changes \(f\) through its own slope, and the changes add). As a particle moves, \(f\) changes through its explicit time dependence and through the change of the particle’s position and momentum:

\[ \frac{df}{dt} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \dot{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{x}} + \dot{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{p}} = 0. \]

Here \(\dot{\mathbf p}\) is the force on a particle at that point of phase space. In one plane, with \(s\) as the clock,

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial s} + x'\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + x''\frac{\partial f}{\partial x'} = 0, \]

where \(x''\) (rad/m) is the bending the forces give at \((x, x')\). When the forces include the beam’s own space charge, smoothed into a field computed from \(f\) itself through Poisson’s equation, this is the Vlasov equation (Anatoly Vlasov, 1938). It is nonlinear, because the force depends on the distribution it moves, and in practice it is solved by tracking \(10^4\) to \(10^6\) representative particles. Smoothing is fair when a sphere whose radius is one Debye length (the screening distance defined in the plasma section below) holds many particles, so that no single neighbour matters; Wangler (section 9.5) checks that this holds for accelerator beams. Close collisions, which smoothing leaves out, are not Hamiltonian in the beam’s own phase space, and they are what slowly heats a dense beam.

Second moments. Averages over the beam are integrals over \(f\), as averages over any probability density are (F6, sections 3 and 4):

\[ \langle x^2\rangle = \frac{\int x^2 f\,dx\,dx'}{\int f\,dx\,dx'}, \]

and likewise \(\langle x'^2\rangle\) and \(\langle xx'\rangle\), all taken about the beam’s centre. Each is a variance or a covariance: \(\langle x^2\rangle\) is the square of the rms width, and \(\langle xx'\rangle\) is positive when the particles further out are moving further out. Collect them in a symmetric matrix (Matrices and determinants),

\[ \Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}\langle x^2\rangle & \langle xx'\rangle\\ \langle xx'\rangle & \langle x'^2\rangle\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \varepsilon^2 = \det\Sigma = \langle x^2\rangle\langle x'^2\rangle - \langle xx'\rangle^2. \]

So the rms emittance of the next section is the square root of a determinant.

Linear optics keeps the rms emittance. Let a linear element send each particle’s \(X = (x, x')\) to \(X_1 = MX\), with \(M\) a \(2\times2\) matrix. The matrix of second moments is the beam average of the column \(X\) times the row \(X^T\), \(\Sigma = \langle XX^T\rangle\). After the element it is \(\langle (MX)(MX)^T\rangle = \langle MXX^TM^T\rangle\), and since \(M\) is the same for every particle it comes out of the average: \(\Sigma_1 = M\Sigma M^{T}\). The determinant of a product is the product of determinants, so

\[ \varepsilon_1^2 = \det M \cdot \det\Sigma \cdot \det M^T = (\det M)^2\,\varepsilon^2 = \varepsilon^2, \]

since Liouville makes \(\det M = 1\). Every drift, thin lens, quadrupole and solenoid (in its own rotating frame) keeps the rms emittance exactly, whatever the shape of the distribution.

Nonlinear forces: filamentation. Now give each particle a kick that grows as the cube of its offset,

\[ x \to x, \qquad x' \to x' - \kappa x^3, \]

with \(\kappa\) in rad/m³. A lens with spherical aberration does this, and so does the space-charge field of a beam that is denser in its middle than at its edge. The map’s Jacobian is still 1 (it is a shear: \(x\) is unchanged and \(x'\) is moved by an amount that depends only on \(x\)), so the true area of the cloud is kept. But the rms emittance is not. Start with a Gaussian beam, uncorrelated, of rms width \(\sigma\) and rms angle \(\sigma'\), so \(\varepsilon_0 = \sigma\sigma'\). A Gaussian (F6, section 5) has \(\langle x^4\rangle = 3\sigma^4\) and \(\langle x^6\rangle = 15\sigma^6\) (integrate by parts as in chapter 2). After the kick, since \(x\) and \(x'\) are independent and \(\langle x'\rangle = 0\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \langle x'^2\rangle_1 &= \sigma'^2 + \kappa^2\langle x^6\rangle = \sigma'^2 + 15\kappa^2\sigma^6,\\ \langle xx'\rangle_1 &= -\kappa\langle x^4\rangle = -3\kappa\sigma^4,\\ \varepsilon_1^2 &= \sigma^2\left(\sigma'^2 + 15\kappa^2\sigma^6\right) - 9\kappa^2\sigma^8 = \varepsilon_0^2 + 6\kappa^2\sigma^8. \end{aligned} \]

Part of the kick is linear on average, and the subtraction of \(\langle xx'\rangle^2\) removes it: a lens could take it out again. The rest, \(6\kappa^2\sigma^8\), is an S-shaped bend of the cloud that no linear lens can undo. Further nonlinear kicks and drifts wind the arms of the S into spirals, filaments, until the cloud looks like a larger, filled ellipse at any practical resolution.

At work. Take \(\sigma = 2\) mm, \(\sigma' = 0.5\) mrad, so \(\varepsilon_0 = 1\) µm, and a cubic kick of 1 mrad at one rms width: \(\kappa\sigma^3 = 10^{-3}\), \(\kappa = 1.25\times10^5\) rad/m³. Then \(6\kappa^2\sigma^8 = 6(\kappa\sigma^3)^2\sigma^2 = 2.4\times10^{-11}\) m², and \(\varepsilon_1 = \sqrt{10^{-12} + 2.4\times10^{-11}} = 5.0\) µm. A Monte Carlo run with two million particles gives the same 5.00 µm. The beam’s true area has not changed at all, but every measurement and every downstream machine will see five times the emittance. This is why the chapter says that nonlinear forces “keep the true area but twist the cloud”.

Figure 7.3. The example’s beam, drawn as its \(2\sigma\) density contour: before the cubic kick (blue) and after it (red). The kick moves each point straight up or down, so the area inside the contour is unchanged, but the contour is bent into an S. The dashed ellipse has the kicked beam’s second moments; its area, and so the rms emittance, is five times larger.

Beam temperature. At a waist (where \(\langle xx'\rangle = 0\)), the spread of transverse momentum is random motion, like the motion of molecules in a gas. Define the transverse temperature \(T\) of a beam by

\[ k_B T = \frac{\langle p_x^2\rangle}{m}, \]

with \(m\) the rest mass. For a Maxwellian, this is exactly the temperature in the Boltzmann factor, since each momentum component has variance \(m k_B T\) (chapter 2). A transverse momentum is unchanged by a Lorentz boost along the beam, so \(\langle p_x^2\rangle\) is the same in the laboratory and in a frame moving with the beam, and this temperature is the beam’s own. With the normalised emittance of the next section, \(\varepsilon_n = \sigma_x\sqrt{\langle p_x^2\rangle}/(mc)\) at a waist, so

\[ k_B T = m c^2\left(\frac{\varepsilon_n}{\sigma_x}\right)^2. \]

Emittance per unit size is temperature. A beam is not in equilibrium: its two transverse temperatures and its longitudinal one generally differ, and collisions are too rare to share heat among them.

At work. At the LCLS cathode the theoretical 0.15 µm over \(\sigma_x = 0.325\) mm gives \(k_B T = 511\ \mathrm{keV}\times(0.15/325)^2 = 0.11\) eV; the measured 0.3 µm gives 0.44 eV. The BNL magnetron’s 0.4 µm of H⁻ over \(\sigma_x = 0.7\) mm, with \(mc^2 = 939\) MeV, gives an effective 310 eV, against an ion temperature in the plasma of about 1 eV. That beam’s “heat” is almost all aberration, not thermal motion.

Exercise D1. Show directly from the moments that a drift of length \(L\) keeps \(\varepsilon\).

Answer

After the drift \(x_1 = x + Lx'\) and \(x_1' = x'\). Then \(\langle x_1^2\rangle = \langle x^2\rangle + 2L\langle xx'\rangle + L^2\langle x'^2\rangle\), \(\langle x_1x_1'\rangle = \langle xx'\rangle + L\langle x'^2\rangle\), and \(\langle x_1'^2\rangle = \langle x'^2\rangle\). So

\[ \langle x_1^2\rangle\langle x_1'^2\rangle - \langle x_1x_1'\rangle^2 = \langle x^2\rangle\langle x'^2\rangle + 2L\langle xx'\rangle\langle x'^2\rangle + L^2\langle x'^2\rangle^2 - \langle xx'\rangle^2 - 2L\langle xx'\rangle\langle x'^2\rangle - L^2\langle x'^2\rangle^2, \]

which is \(\langle x^2\rangle\langle x'^2\rangle - \langle xx'\rangle^2\).

Exercise D2. In the example above, a better lens gives a cubic kick of only 0.2 mrad at one rms width. What is the emittance after it?

Answer

\(\kappa\sigma^3 = 2\times10^{-4}\), so \(6\kappa^2\sigma^8 = 6\times(2\times10^{-4})^2\times(2\times10^{-3})^2 = 9.6\times10^{-13}\) m². Then \(\varepsilon_1 = \sqrt{1.0\times10^{-12} + 0.96\times10^{-12}} = 1.40\) µm. Exercise D3. What normalised rms emittance would an H⁻ beam of \(\sigma_x = 0.7\) mm have if its transverse temperature were the 1 eV of the plasma ions?

\(\varepsilon_n = \sigma_x\sqrt{k_B T/mc^2} = 0.7\ \mathrm{mm}\times\sqrt{1/939.3\times10^{6}} = 0.7\ \mathrm{mm}\times3.26\times10^{-5} = 0.023\) µm, seventeen times less than the 0.4 µm measured.

Pre-requisite. This section gives the Gaussian beam in a phase-space plane and the fraction of it inside an ellipse of given emittance: 39, 86 and 95 per cent inside one, four and six times the rms emittance. It also gives the uniform ellipse, whose total emittance is four times its rms value. The next section uses both to translate between the conventions laboratories quote. Taught from Wangler, section 9.4, with the integrals of chapter 2.

Reminder: chapter 2 showed that \(\int e^{-u^2/(2\sigma^2)}du = \sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma\) over the whole line, and that the normalised Gaussian has mean square \(\sigma^2\). With \(u = p_x\) and \(\sigma^2 = m k_B T\) that is the statement \(\langle p_x^2\rangle = m k_B T\) for a Maxwellian, which the next section uses. What is new here is a Gaussian in two variables at once. F6, section 5 met one already, a round beam in \(x\) and \(y\); here the two variables are a position and an angle.

A Gaussian beam in one plane. Let positions and angles be independent Gaussians of rms \(\sigma\) (m) and \(\sigma'\) (rad):

\[ f(x, x') = \frac{N}{2\pi\sigma\sigma'}\exp\!\left(-\frac{x^2}{2\sigma^2} - \frac{x'^2}{2\sigma'^2}\right). \]

Its rms emittance is \(\varepsilon = \sigma\sigma'\), since \(\langle xx'\rangle = 0\). The lines of constant density are ellipses \(x^2/\sigma^2 + x'^2/\sigma'^2 = R^2\). Rescale the axes, \(u = x/\sigma\) and \(v = x'/\sigma'\). The ellipse becomes a circle of radius \(R\) and the density becomes \(e^{-(u^2+v^2)/2}\), the same in every direction. The rescaling multiplies every area by \(\sigma\sigma' = \varepsilon\), so the ellipse has area \(\pi R^2\varepsilon\) in the \((x, x')\) plane. Call \(\varepsilon_R = R^2\varepsilon\) its emittance (area divided by \(\pi\)).

The fraction inside. In the \((u, v)\) plane use polar coordinates \(\rho\), \(\theta\), whose area element is \(\rho\,d\rho\,d\theta\) (a step \(d\rho\) outward and an arc \(\rho\,d\theta\) round, F2, section 13). The fraction of the beam inside radius \(R\) is

\[ F = \frac{\int_0^R e^{-\rho^2/2}\rho\,d\rho}{\int_0^\infty e^{-\rho^2/2}\rho\,d\rho} = 1 - e^{-R^2/2}. \]

The factor \(\rho\) from the area element makes the integral elementary, as with the integral of \(p\,e^{-p^2/2mk_BT}\) in chapter 2. In terms of emittance,

\[ F = 1 - \exp\!\left(-\frac{\varepsilon_R}{2\varepsilon}\right). \]

So a “95 per cent emittance” is six times the rms emittance, as the next section says. Note how different this is from one dimension, where \(\pm1\sigma\) holds 68 per cent: in two dimensions the rms ellipse holds only 39 per cent. A Monte Carlo check with two million points gives 86.5 and 95.0 per cent inside \(4\varepsilon\) and \(6\varepsilon\).

A correlated beam. A real beam usually has \(\langle xx'\rangle \ne 0\): its ellipses are tilted. A linear map of determinant 1 (a drift or a lens, run backwards) untilts them without changing any area or any fraction, and it keeps \(\varepsilon\) (previous pre-requisite). So the table holds for any Gaussian beam, with the ellipses taken similar to the rms ellipse.

The uniform ellipse. Some beams are closer to uniform than Gaussian. Take a uniform density inside a circle of radius \(R_0\) in the \((u, v)\) plane. Then

\[ \langle u^2\rangle = \frac{1}{\pi R_0^2}\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{R_0}\rho^2\cos^2\theta\,\rho\,d\rho\,d\theta = \frac{1}{\pi R_0^2}\cdot\pi\cdot\frac{R_0^4}{4} = \frac{R_0^2}{4}. \]

The rms width is half the edge. Applying this to both axes of an ellipse with semi-axes \(a_x\) and \(a_{x'}\) gives \(\varepsilon = a_x a_{x'}/4\), while the total area is \(\pi a_x a_{x'}\). So the total emittance of a uniform ellipse is \(4\varepsilon\), and its rms ellipse encloses a quarter of the beam. This is the reason for Lapostolle’s “4-rms” convention: it gives the full emittance of the uniform beam with the same second moments.

At work. The BNL magnetron’s 4-rms normalised emittance of 1.6 π mm mrad is an rms emittance of 0.4 µm. Were the beam Gaussian, 86 per cent of it would lie inside 1.6 µm and 95 per cent inside 2.4 µm; were it uniform, all of it would lie inside 1.6 µm. The same beam can be quoted as 0.4, 1.6 or 2.4 µm, all correctly.

Exercise G3. What fraction of a uniform beam lies inside its rms ellipse, and why is it less than for a Gaussian?

Answer

The rms ellipse has emittance \(\varepsilon\) and the whole beam \(4\varepsilon\), so with uniform density it holds a quarter, 25 per cent. A Gaussian piles its particles towards the centre, so its rms ellipse holds more, 39 per cent, while its outer tails stretch far beyond.

Phase space and emittance at the level a source needs

Describe each particle at a plane across the beam by its transverse position \(x\) (m) and its angle to the axis \(x' = dx/ds = p_x/p_z\) (rad), where \(p_x\) is the transverse momentum and \(p_z\) the longitudinal one. Plot every particle as a point in the \((x, x')\) plane. A beam is a cloud of such points. A narrow, parallel beam is a small cloud; a beam that is wide, or whose particles cross at large angles, is a big one.

The rms emittance measures the cloud’s area with second moments taken about its centre:

\[ \varepsilon = \sqrt{\langle x^2\rangle\langle x'^2\rangle - \langle x x'\rangle^2} \]

in m rad, usually quoted in mm mrad or µm (the same unit). The subtraction removes a straight-line correlation: a beam that converges to a focus, with \(x'\) proportional to \(-x\), has a thin, tilted cloud and a small emittance even though both \(\langle x^2\rangle\) and \(\langle x'^2\rangle\) are large. For a cloud whose density is uniform inside an ellipse, the ellipse’s area is \(4\pi\varepsilon\). Different laboratories quote \(\varepsilon\), \(4\varepsilon\) (the “4-rms” emittance, after Lapostolle in 1971), or the area of the ellipse holding 90 or 95 per cent of the beam (for a Gaussian beam in two dimensions the 95 per cent emittance is six times the rms value), and some fold \(\pi\) into the number while others write it in the unit as “\(\pi\) mm mrad”. Always ask which.

When a beam is accelerated, \(p_z\) grows while \(p_x\) does not, so every angle shrinks and the emittance falls as \(1/(\beta\gamma)\). To compare beams at different energies one uses the normalised emittance

\[ \varepsilon_n = \beta\gamma\,\varepsilon \approx \frac{1}{m c}\sqrt{\langle x^2\rangle\langle p_x^2\rangle - \langle x p_x\rangle^2} \]

which is an area in position and momentum, divided by \(mc\) to give it the unit of length. Here \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) are the particle’s relativistic factors. The approximation assumes a small spread of \(p_z\).

Liouville’s theorem. For particles moving in fields that derive from a Hamiltonian, the density of points in the six-dimensional phase space \((x, p_x, y, p_y, z, p_z)\) is constant along the flow, so the volume the beam occupies cannot change (Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §46). When the three directions are uncoupled the area in each plane is kept separately. This is what makes emittance a property of the source: lenses can rotate and stretch the cloud, trading width for angle, but they cannot shrink its area. Three cautions. The theorem is about canonical momenta, so in a magnetic field \(p_x\) includes \(qA_x\); a cathode sitting in a solenoid’s field gives a beam extra emittance once it leaves the field (Busch’s theorem, chapter 2, section 9). Collisions, radiation and stripping are not Hamiltonian; cooling (chapter 24) and radiation damping (chapter 20) do shrink emittance. And nonlinear forces, including the space charge of a non-uniform beam, keep the true area but twist the cloud into filaments, so the rms emittance, which an experimenter measures, grows. The full theory of emittance and the optics that transport it is chapter 16.

Brightness. A downstream machine accepts a fixed emittance. What matters then is how much current fits inside it. So define the normalised brightness

\[ \mathcal{B} = \frac{I}{\varepsilon_{n,x}\,\varepsilon_{n,y}} \]

in A m⁻² rad⁻², with rms normalised emittances. Other definitions put factors of \(\pi^2\) or \(8\pi^2\) in front, or use 4-rms emittances; numbers from different papers can differ by an order of magnitude for this reason alone. By Liouville’s theorem, nothing after the source can raise \(\mathcal{B}\) without cooling.

Pre-requisite (continued): Electrons in metals, Fermi-Dirac statistics, work function

Pre-requisite. This section shows how a thin layer of adsorbed atoms, caesium, thorium or barium, lowers a metal’s work function: the layer is a sheet of electric dipoles, and a dipole sheet makes a step in potential. The sections on thermionic cathodes and negative-ion sources use it. Taught from Brown, The Physics and Technology of Ion Sources, section 14.2.1.2, and Tong, Electromagnetism, sections 2.1.4 and 2.2.3 (surface charge and dipoles).

Reminder: chapter 2 gave the free-electron metal, the Fermi-Dirac distribution and its Maxwellian tail, the work function as the step from the Fermi level to the vacuum, and current as a flux across a surface. Section 6 of chapter 2 (Richardson’s law) put them together: the \(T^2\) comes from the flux of the tail through the surface, the exponential from the barrier. Chapter 4 added the image force that lowers the barrier in a strong field. What remains is why a monolayer of another element changes the barrier itself.

A dipole sheet. Recall from F4, section 4 that Gauss’s law gives a flat sheet of charge a uniform field on each side, and from F4, section 3 that a \(+\) and \(-\) charge a small distance apart make a dipole. Take two parallel sheets of charge, \(+\sigma\) and \(-\sigma\) (C/m²), a small distance \(\delta\) (m) apart. Each sheet alone makes a field \(\sigma/(2\varepsilon_0)\) on both sides of itself, pointing away from a positive sheet and towards a negative one. Between the sheets the two fields add to \(\sigma/\varepsilon_0\); outside they cancel. So the field is confined to the thin gap, and crossing the gap the potential steps by

\[ \Delta\phi = \frac{\sigma\delta}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{N_s p}{\varepsilon_0}, \]

where the second form counts the layer as \(N_s\) separate dipoles per square metre, each of dipole moment \(p = q\delta\) (C m), so that \(\sigma\delta = N_s p\). How the charges are arranged within the layer does not matter, as long as the layer is thin: only the dipole moment per unit area enters.

Why it lowers the work function. Put the layer on a metal surface with its positive side outward. Going outward through the layer, the field points back towards the metal, so the potential rises by \(\Delta\phi\). An electron’s potential energy, \(-e\phi\), falls by \(e\Delta\phi\). The vacuum outside sits lower for electrons than it did, and the work function falls by \(e\Delta\phi\). A layer with its negative side outward (oxygen, for example) raises it.

Caesium on a metal. Caesium’s ionisation energy, 3.89 eV, is less than tungsten’s work function, about 4.5 eV. So a lone caesium atom on tungsten gives its outer electron to the metal, which can take it at a lower energy. What remains is a Cs⁺ ion sitting on the surface, attracted by its negative image inside the metal (the image charge of chapter 4). If the ion’s centre is a distance of about its ionic radius, \(r_i \approx 0.17\) nm, above the metal, and the image the same distance below, the pair has dipole moment

\[ p = e(2r_i) = 5.4\times10^{-29}\ \mathrm{C\,m}, \]

16 debye in chemists’ units. Caesium atoms are about 0.53 nm across, so a close-packed layer has about \(4.1\times10^{18}\) atoms per m². (A monolayer is a layer exactly one atom thick; coverage is quoted as a fraction of it.) At 1 per cent of a layer, \(N_s = 4.1\times10^{16}\) m⁻² and

\[ \Delta\phi = \frac{4.1\times10^{16}\times5.4\times10^{-29}}{8.85\times10^{-12}} = 0.25\ \mathrm{V}. \]

A hundredth of a monolayer takes a quarter of a volt off the work function.

Depolarisation, and the minimum. The same linear law would give 25 V at a full layer, which is absurd. As the ions crowd together, each one’s field pushes electron charge back towards its neighbours, the dipoles weaken, and towards a full layer the bonding becomes metallic. Brown (figure 14.2) shows the measured result: the work function falls to a minimum of 1.37 to 1.76 eV at about 0.6 of a monolayer and rises again to 1.56 to 2.15 eV at a full layer. A drop from 4.5 to 1.5 eV at 0.6 of a layer (\(N_s = 2.5\times10^{18}\) m⁻²) means an average dipole of \(3.0\times\varepsilon_0/N_s = 1.1\times10^{-29}\) C m, a fifth of the lone ion’s. The minimum below a full layer is why sources dose caesium carefully: too little and the surface is barely changed, too much and the benefit falls again.

Thorium and barium. Thoriated tungsten works the same way: a monolayer of thorium, diffusing from the bulk to the surface, lowers tungsten’s work function from about 4.5 to about 2.6 eV. At 2000 K, where \(k_B T = 0.172\) eV, the Richardson exponential alone grows by \(e^{1.9/0.172} = 6\times10^4\). Richardson’s law with \(A_G = 0.6\times10^6\) A m⁻² K⁻² gives pure tungsten 1.1 mA/cm² at 2000 K; the table in the section on thermionic cathodes gives thoriated tungsten 5 A/cm² there, about 4,500 times more. The gain is less than the exponential suggests because a partly covered surface also has a smaller constant \(A_G\), but a factor of thousands at the same temperature is what made thoriated filaments the standard of valve transmitters. In a dispenser cathode the layer is barium and oxygen, renewed continuously from inside, giving about 2 eV.

For negative ions. Surface production of H⁻ needs an electron to tunnel from the metal onto a departing hydrogen atom, whose extra electron is bound by only 0.75 eV. That binding energy of an extra electron is the atom’s electron affinity, \(E_a\). Brown (equation 14.3) gives the probability as falling exponentially with \(\phi_w - E_a\), the gap between the work function and the electron affinity \(E_a\). On clean molybdenum that gap is nearly 4 eV; on caesiated molybdenum at 1.5 eV it is 0.75 eV. That is the physics behind the caesium of the surface-plasma sources.

Exercise W1. In the linear regime, what fraction of a caesium monolayer lowers a work function by 1.0 eV?

Answer

\(N_s = \varepsilon_0\Delta\phi/p = 8.85\times10^{-12}\times1.0/5.4\times10^{-29} = 1.64\times10^{17}\) m⁻², which is \(1.64\times10^{17}/4.1\times10^{18} = 4\) per cent of a layer. Depolarisation is already noticeable at this coverage, so somewhat more is needed in practice.

Exercise W2. A dispenser cathode at 1300 K loses part of its barium layer and its work function rises by 0.1 eV. By what factor does its emission fall, if it is temperature limited?

Answer

\(k_B T = 0.112\) eV at 1300 K, so the current falls by \(e^{0.1/0.112} = 2.44\). A change of a tenth of a volt in the surface layer more than halves the current, which is why cathodes are activated and their emission is watched over their life.

Exercise W3. Why does an oxygen layer on tungsten raise the work function?

Answer

Oxygen is strongly electronegative and takes electrons from the metal. The adsorbed atom carries the negative charge, outermost, and the positive image lies in the metal: the dipole layer has its negative side outward. Going outward, the potential falls, an electron’s energy rises, and the work function increases. This is one reason gas poisons cathodes.

The thermal emittance of a cathode

A hot cathode emits electrons with random transverse velocities. Even if every electron left a perfectly flat surface, the beam would have an emittance. Here is its size.

Inside a metal, the electrons that can escape are those in the tail of the Fermi-Dirac distribution, more than the work function \(\phi_w\) above the Fermi level. When \(\phi_w \gg k_B T\) that tail is Maxwellian, the Boltzmann factor of F6, section 13 with the kinetic energy \(p^2/2m\):

\[ f(\mathbf{p}) \propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{p_x^2 + p_y^2 + p_z^2}{2 m k_B T}\right) \]

with \(z\) normal to the surface. An electron escapes if \(p_z^2/2m\) exceeds the barrier; the flux through the surface is also weighted by \(v_z\). Both conditions involve \(p_z\) alone. The distribution factorises, so the selection leaves the transverse part untouched: the emitted electrons have the same Gaussian spread of \(p_x\) as the electrons inside,

\[ \langle p_x^2\rangle = m k_B T. \]

At the cathode, position and transverse momentum are uncorrelated (\(\langle x p_x\rangle = 0\)), so the normalised emittance is

\[ \varepsilon_n = \frac{1}{mc}\sqrt{\sigma_x^2\, m k_B T} = \sigma_x\sqrt{\frac{k_B T}{m c^2}}. \]

\(\sigma_x\) is the rms size of the emitting area in one direction. For a uniform disc of radius \(r_c\), \(\sigma_x = r_c/2\). The result assumes a flat, uniform surface, no field in the gap’s first micrometres strong enough to lower the barrier (the Schottky effect, below), and no magnetic field on the cathode. A rough surface, or patches of differing work function, add to it.

Numbers. Fraser and his colleagues took a typical thermionic emitter at 1160 K, where \(k_B T = 0.100\) eV. Then \(\sqrt{k_B T/mc^2} = 0.44\times10^{-3}\), so each millimetre of rms spot size costs 0.44 µm of normalised emittance. For tungsten at 2500 K (\(k_B T = 0.215\) eV) the factor is 0.65 µm per mm. A dispenser cathode of 5 mm radius (\(\sigma_x = 2.5\) mm) at 1160 K has \(\varepsilon_n = 1.1\) µm, and at 10 A/cm² it gives 7.9 A.

Now the brightness. A disc of radius \(r_c\) at current density \(J\) gives \(I = J\pi r_c^2\), with \(\varepsilon_{n,x} = \varepsilon_{n,y} = (r_c/2)\sqrt{k_B T/mc^2}\). Then

\[ \mathcal{B}_{max} = \frac{J \pi r_c^2}{(r_c^2/4)(k_B T/mc^2)} = \frac{4\pi J m c^2}{k_B T}. \]

The radius cancels. A cathode’s brightness is set only by how much current each square metre gives, divided by its temperature. For \(J = 10\) A/cm² at 0.1 eV, \(\mathcal{B}_{max} = 6.4\times10^{12}\) A m⁻² rad⁻² in this rms convention. In Fraser’s convention (emittance taken as \(4\pi\) times the rms value), the same physics gives their \(4.1\times10^{9}\) A m⁻² rad⁻² per A/cm²; a direct calculation reproduces their coefficient, \(5.0\times10^{-6}\pi\sqrt{I/J}\) m rad (with \(J\) in A/cm²), to two figures. Liouville then says the beam can never be brighter than this. To beat it one needs a larger \(J\) or a colder emitter. The photoinjector, below, gets the first.

Thermionic cathodes

Richardson’s law, in the form Saul Dushman gave it in 1923, is

\[ J = A_G T^2 \exp\!\left(-\frac{\phi_w}{k_B T}\right), \qquad A_G = \lambda_R A_0, \qquad A_0 = \frac{4\pi m_e k_B^2 e}{h^3} = 1.2017\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-2}\,K^{-2}} \]

with \(\lambda_R\) a material factor of order 0.5. The \(T^2\) comes from the flux of a Maxwellian tail through a surface; the exponential from the barrier. The derivation uses the free-electron model of a metal and the Fermi-Dirac distribution (Landau and Lifshitz, Statistical Physics Part 1, §53, gives the distribution). The exponential dominates. The fractional change of \(J\) per kelvin is the derivative of its logarithm (F1, sections 4 and 5): \(\ln J = \ln A_G + 2\ln T - \phi_w/k_BT\). For tungsten with \(\phi_w \approx 4.5\) eV, \(d\ln J/dT = 2/T + \phi_w/(k_B T^2) = 0.0092\) per kelvin at 2500 K, so the current changes by about 1 per cent per kelvin. With \(\lambda_R = 0.5\) the formula gives 0.32 A/cm² at 2500 K and 0.12 A/cm² at 2400 K, in line with the half an ampere per square centimetre that handbooks list for tungsten at 2500 K.

The choice of cathode is a trade of work function against lifetime and robustness. Handbook figures (from the Wikipedia article on hot cathodes, which tabulates them):

Cathode Typical temperature Emission
Pure tungsten 2500 K 0.5 A/cm²
Thoriated tungsten 2000 K 5 A/cm²
Oxide-coated (Wehnelt, 1904) 1000 K 10 A/cm²
Barium aluminate dispenser 1300 K 4 A/cm²

Thoriated tungsten was found in 1914 and made practical by Langmuir in 1923: a monolayer of thorium on the surface lowers the work function. Oxide cathodes emit well at low temperature but are poisoned by gas and spoil under ion bombardment. The dispenser cathode, developed at Philips laboratories around 1950, stores barium compounds in porous tungsten so that a low-work-function layer of barium on the surface is continuously renewed; Roberto Levi of Philips Laboratories in New York published the impregnated form in 1953 and an improved version in 1955. Dispenser cathodes are widely used in klystrons (the high-power microwave amplifiers that drive linacs, chapter 13) and linac guns. Lanthanum hexaboride (J. M. Lafferty, Boride cathodes, 1951) has a work function of about 2.5 eV, is roughly ten times brighter than tungsten and lasts ten to fifteen times longer; it is the cathode of many electron microscopes. It slowly evaporates and coats the electrodes near it.

A strong field at the surface lowers the barrier. The image force and the applied field \(\mathcal{E}\) together give a maximum below the work function by

\[ \Delta\phi_w = \sqrt{\frac{e^3 \mathcal{E}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}} \]

(in joules; divide by \(e\) for eV). At 50 MV/m this is 0.268 eV. It is the Schottky effect, accurate up to fields of about \(10^8\) V/m.

Pre-requisite (continued): Laplace and Poisson equations, uniqueness, boundary-value problems

Pre-requisite. This section adds two things to the boundary-value problems of chapters 2 and 4. First, the Cauchy problem: the potential and its normal derivative both given along one open line, which is the problem Pierce posed, and why it is unstable to fine detail. Second, the self-consistent solution of Poisson’s equation together with the trajectories, which every gun and extraction code performs. The Pierce gun section uses both. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, section 2.4 (uniqueness and boundary-value problems), and Brown, chapter 4 (Spädtke, computer simulation of extraction).

Reminder: chapter 2 solved Poisson’s equation across a planar diode, and chapter 4 showed that the potential given on every conductor (a closed boundary) fixes the solution uniquely.

Two kinds of boundary data. The problems so far gave the potential \(\phi\) (V) on a closed boundary: every electrode, and the walls. That is the Dirichlet problem. It has exactly one solution, and the solution is stable: a harmonic function has no maximum or minimum inside its region, so an error of a volt on the boundary makes at most a volt of error anywhere inside.

Pierce’s problem is of another kind. The electrodes are unknown; they are what we want. What is known is the flow along one open line, the beam’s edge: the potential \(\phi(z, 0)\) that the infinite diode would have there, and the condition that no field crosses the edge, \(\partial\phi/\partial y = 0\). Giving both the value and the normal derivative on a line is the Cauchy problem. Once \(\phi\) is found in the empty region, any equipotential can be made into an electrode at that voltage, since a conductor’s surface is an equipotential and uniqueness then guarantees the same field.

Marching away from the line. Laplace’s equation in two dimensions, \(\partial^2\phi/\partial z^2 + \partial^2\phi/\partial y^2 = 0\), can be read as an instruction: \(\partial^2\phi/\partial y^2 = -\partial^2\phi/\partial z^2\). With \(\phi\) and \(\partial\phi/\partial y\) known on \(y = 0\), it gives the second \(y\)-derivative there, and by differentiating again, every higher one: a Taylor series in \(y\) (F1, section 8). With zero slope on the line, \(\phi_0(z) = \phi(z, 0)\), and primes on \(\phi_0\) meaning derivatives in \(z\), the Taylor series in \(y\) is

\[ \phi(z, y) = \phi_0(z) - \frac{y^2}{2}\phi_0''(z) + \frac{y^4}{24}\phi_0''''(z) - \cdots \]

For Pierce’s edge potential \(\phi_0 = V(z/d)^{4/3}\), the first correction is \(-(y^2/2)V(4/3)(1/3)z^{-2/3}d^{-4/3} = -(2/9)V(y^2/d^2)(z/d)^{-2/3}\). At \(z = d\), \(y = 0.1d\), two terms give \(\phi/V = 1 - 0.00222 = 0.99778\); the exact closed form, found in the section after next, gives 0.99778. The series needs every derivative of \(\phi_0\), so the edge potential must be smooth in the strongest sense (analytic), as \(z^{4/3}\) is for \(z > 0\).

Hadamard’s warning. Suppose the wanted edge potential carries a small ripple, \(\phi_0 = a\cos kz\), with \(k\) in m⁻¹ and zero slope. The solution is

\[ \phi = a\cos(kz)\cosh(ky), \]

where \(\cosh u = (e^u + e^{-u})/2\) is the hyperbolic cosine: it is 1 at \(u = 0\), has zero slope there, grows as \(e^u/2\) for large \(u\), and its second derivative is itself (F1, section 4). Check the solution: \(\partial^2/\partial z^2\) gives \(-k^2\phi\), \(\partial^2/\partial y^2\) gives \(+k^2\phi\), they cancel, and at \(y = 0\) the value is \(a\cos kz\) with zero slope. Away from the beam the ripple grows as \(\cosh ky\). For a ripple of wavelength 2 mm (\(k = 3140\) m⁻¹) the growth is 11.6 at 1 mm from the edge and \(3.3\times10^6\) at 5 mm. For a 10 mm wavelength it is only 11.6 at 5 mm. Read the other way round: electrodes a few millimetres from the beam have almost no control over fine detail at its edge, and asking for fine detail would demand wildly shaped electrodes. The Cauchy problem for Laplace’s equation is ill-posed, in Jacques Hadamard’s word. Pierce electrodes fix the smooth part of the flow; the details are left to numerical checks.

Self-consistency: Poisson’s equation with the trajectories. Inside a beam the charge density \(\rho\) (C/m³) is not zero, and it depends on where the particles go, which depends on the potential. Gun and extraction codes break the circle by iteration:

  1. Guess the potential, for instance the empty-gap (Laplace) solution.
  2. Launch trajectories from the emitter. For a space-charge-limited cathode the current each one carries is set by the field just in front of the cathode, by Child’s law applied locally.
  3. Deposit charge along each trajectory: a current density \(J\) moving at speed \(v\) is a charge density \(\rho = J/v\) (F4, section 8).
  4. Solve Poisson’s equation, \(\nabla^2\phi = -\rho/\varepsilon_0\), with the electrode potentials as boundary values.
  5. Go back to step 2 with the new potential, and stop when nothing changes.

Each pass is a calculation on a mesh of points, done by computer as in F1, section 11. In two and three dimensions codes usually mix only part of each new potential into the old one, because the emitted current itself depends on the field and the iteration can otherwise oscillate. W. B. Herrmannsfeldt’s EGUN at SLAC and the IGUN and KOBRA3 codes used in Brown’s book for ion extraction all work this way; for an ion source the emitter is the plasma meniscus, whose position is found in the same iteration.

At work: the planar diode. Take \(V = 10\) kV across \(d = 10\) mm, and \(J\) fixed at the Child-Langmuir value, 2.33 A/cm². Divide the gap into 400 cells. Start from the straight-line potential, which is 2500 V at a quarter of the gap. One pass (electron speeds from the guess, charge from the speeds, then Poisson) gives 1759 V there; further passes give 1632, 1598, 1585, 1581 V, and after 60 passes 1576 V, against the exact \(10\,\mathrm{kV}\times(1/4)^{4/3} = 1575\) V. The remaining 0.07 per cent is the error of the mesh.

Exercise P1. Write the Taylor series in \(y\) for a uniform field, \(\phi_0 = \mathcal{E}_0 z\) with zero slope on \(y = 0\). What electrodes does it give?

Answer

\(\phi_0'' = 0\), so every correction vanishes and \(\phi = \mathcal{E}_0 z\) everywhere. The equipotentials are the planes of constant \(z\): the flat cathode and flat anode of a planar diode with no space charge. Without space charge the edge needs no special shaping.

Exercise P2. A beam edge is wanted with a ripple of 1 V at a wavelength of 4 mm. How large is the ripple in the potential 3 mm from the edge, where an electrode would have to be shaped to make it?

Answer

\(k = 2\pi/0.004 = 1571\) m⁻¹, \(ky = 4.71\), \(\cosh 4.71 = 55.7\). The electrode would need to carry a ripple of 56 V on its equipotential, and any small error in that ripple would be divided by 56 at the beam edge. In practice nobody tries: the wanted flow is chosen smooth.

Pre-requisite: Complex numbers and complex functions

Pre-requisite. This section teaches complex numbers in Cartesian and polar form, complex powers including fractional ones such as \(w^{4/3}\), what it means for a function of a complex variable to have a derivative (to be analytic), and the Cauchy-Riemann relations, including the rule \(\partial/\partial y = i\,d/dw\) that the Pierce gun section uses. The next pre-requisite turns them into a tool for electrostatics. Taught from Penrose, The Road to Reality, sections 4.1, 5.1, 5.3, 5.4, 7.1 and 10.5.

Reminder: F5, sections 1 and 2 taught complex numbers, the complex plane, modulus and argument, and Euler’s formula \(e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta\). The first two paragraphs below repeat them briefly in the letters the Pierce gun uses. What is new is fractional powers and the derivative of a complex function.

The number \(i\). No real number squares to \(-1\). Admit one that does, \(i\), with \(i^2 = -1\), and keep all the usual rules of algebra. A complex number is \(w = z + iy\), with \(z\) and \(y\) real: \(z\) is its real part, \(\mathrm{Re}\,w\), and \(y\) its imaginary part, \(\mathrm{Im}\,w\). (The letters follow the Pierce gun section, where \(z\) runs along the beam and \(y\) out of it; elsewhere you will see \(x + iy\).) Add complex numbers part by part. Multiply them by expanding the brackets and replacing \(i^2\) by \(-1\): \((1 + 2i)(3 + i) = 3 + i + 6i + 2i^2 = 1 + 7i\).

The complex plane and polar form. Draw \(w = z + iy\) as the point \((z, y)\) in a plane. Its distance from the origin is the modulus \(r = |w| = \sqrt{z^2 + y^2}\), and its angle from the positive real axis is the argument \(\theta\), so \(z = r\cos\theta\) and \(y = r\sin\theta\). Then

\[ w = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) = r e^{i\theta}. \]

The last step is Euler’s formula. One way to see it: the function \(g(\theta) = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta\) has derivative \(-\sin\theta + i\cos\theta = i\,g(\theta)\) and \(g(0) = 1\), which are exactly the two properties of \(e^{i\theta}\). Polar form makes multiplication geometric: \(r_1e^{i\theta_1}\cdot r_2e^{i\theta_2} = r_1r_2\,e^{i(\theta_1 + \theta_2)}\). Moduli multiply and angles add.

Example. \(w = 3 + 4i\) has \(r = 5\) and \(\theta = 53.13°\).

Powers. Repeating the multiplication, \(w^n = r^n e^{in\theta}\): the modulus is raised to the power and the angle multiplied by it. The same formula defines a fractional power,

\[ w^{4/3} = r^{4/3}e^{i\,4\theta/3} = r^{4/3}\left(\cos\frac{4\theta}{3} + i\sin\frac{4\theta}{3}\right), \]

with one caution. The angle of a point is only defined up to whole turns: \(\theta\) and \(\theta + 2\pi\) name the same \(w\), but \(4\theta/3\) and \(4\theta/3 + 8\pi/3\) do not name the same \(w^{4/3}\). So a fractional power is many-valued, and to make it a function one must agree on a range of \(\theta\) and never let a path wind round the origin. This is called choosing a branch, and the line where the agreed range of \(\theta\) jumps is the branch cut (Penrose discusses the many-valuedness in sections 5.3 and 5.4, and branch points in section 8.1). For the Pierce gun the region of interest is the empty space outside the beam, \(0 \le \theta < \pi\), and the angle is simply taken in that range; the cut where the choice jumps can lie inside the beam, where this solution is not used.

Example. For \(w = 3 + 4i\), \(r^{4/3} = 5^{4/3} = 8.55\) and \(4\theta/3 = 70.84°\), so \(w^{4/3} = 8.55(\cos 70.84° + i\sin 70.84°) = 2.806 + 8.076i\). On the positive real axis, \(\theta = 0\) and \(w^{4/3}\) is the ordinary real power \(z^{4/3}\).

Derivatives of complex functions. A function \(f(w)\) takes complex numbers to complex numbers; write \(f = u + iv\) with \(u\) and \(v\) real functions of \(z\) and \(y\). Define its derivative as for real functions,

\[ f'(w) = \lim_{\Delta w \to 0}\frac{f(w + \Delta w) - f(w)}{\Delta w}, \]

but now \(\Delta w\) can shrink to zero from any direction in the plane, and we demand the same limit from all of them. That is a strong demand. A function that meets it in a region is called analytic (or holomorphic) there. Polynomials, \(e^w\) and powers \(w^a\) (away from the origin and the cut) are analytic, and the usual rules hold: \(d(w^a)/dw = a\,w^{a-1}\). The function \(\mathrm{Re}\,w\) is not: its difference quotient is 1 along the real axis and 0 along the imaginary axis.

The Cauchy-Riemann relations. Compare two directions. Along the real axis, \(\Delta w = \Delta z\), and the limit is \(\partial f/\partial z\). Along the imaginary axis, \(\Delta w = i\,\Delta y\), and the limit is \((1/i)\,\partial f/\partial y = -i\,\partial f/\partial y\). Equal limits mean

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} = f'(w), \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = i\,f'(w). \]

The second is the rule \(\partial/\partial y = i\,d/dw\) for an analytic function. Write \(f = u + iv\) and separate real and imaginary parts of \(\partial f/\partial y = i\,\partial f/\partial z\):

\[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial y}, \qquad \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial v}{\partial z}. \]

These are the Cauchy-Riemann equations. The real and imaginary parts of an analytic function are tied to each other at every point.

Example, checked by numbers. For \(f = w^{4/3}\) at \(w = 3 + 4i\), the formula gives \(f' = \tfrac43 w^{1/3} = 2.172 + 0.694i\). Finite differences give \(\partial f/\partial z = 2.172 + 0.694i\) and \(\partial f/\partial y = -0.694 + 2.172i\), which is \(i\) times the first, as the rule says.

A consequence for the Pierce gun. On the real axis (\(y = 0\), \(z > 0\)), \(f'(w) = \tfrac43 z^{1/3}\) is real. Then \(\partial f/\partial y = i f'\) is purely imaginary there, so its real part, \(\partial u/\partial y\), is zero. The real part of \(w^{4/3}\) has zero slope across the real axis. That is the second of Pierce’s two edge conditions, and the next pre-requisite completes the argument.

Pre-requisite (continued): Images and two-dimensional complex potentials

Pre-requisite. This section teaches the half of the subject that chapter 4 left out: in two dimensions the real part of any analytic function solves Laplace’s equation, so a complex function can be a potential, its derivative gives the field, and its equipotentials in polar form can be built as electrodes. The Pierce gun section uses it to find the focus electrode at 67.5° and the shape of the anode. Taught from Penrose, sections 7.1 and 8.2 (holomorphic functions and conformal maps), and Tong, Electromagnetism, section 2.4.

Reminder: chapter 4 taught image charges, and its section on electrons in metals used the image of an electron in a flat metal to derive the Schottky lowering quoted under thermionic cathodes. The complex functions of the previous pre-requisite now give a second way to solve Laplace’s equation, for problems that do not change along one direction.

The real part of an analytic function solves Laplace’s equation. Let \(F(w) = \phi + i\psi\) be analytic in a region of the \(w = z + iy\) plane. Differentiate the Cauchy-Riemann equations once more:

\[ \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial z^2} = \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial z\,\partial y}, \qquad \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial y^2} = -\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial y\,\partial z}. \]

Adding them, the mixed derivatives cancel:

\[ \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial z^2} + \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial y^2} = 0. \]

So \(\phi = \mathrm{Re}\,F\) is a possible electrostatic potential (in V) in any charge-free region where nothing depends on the third coordinate. The same holds for \(\psi\). Every analytic function is a solved two-dimensional field problem; the work is to find the one that fits the boundaries. \(F\) is called the complex potential.

Field lines and the field. The curves \(\psi\) = constant cross the equipotentials \(\phi\) = constant at right angles (Cauchy-Riemann makes \(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\psi = 0\)), so they are the field lines. The field itself comes from the derivative. Since \(F' = \partial F/\partial z = \partial\phi/\partial z + i\,\partial\psi/\partial z = \partial\phi/\partial z - i\,\partial\phi/\partial y\), and \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\nabla\phi\),

\[ \mathcal{E}_z - i\mathcal{E}_y = -F'(w), \qquad |\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}| = |F'(w)|. \]

Equipotentials as electrodes. Since a conductor’s surface is an equipotential, any curve \(\phi = V_1\) can be replaced by a sheet of metal held at \(V_1\) without changing the field on either side of it that we keep (uniqueness). So a complex potential designs electrodes: pick \(F\), draw its equipotentials, and build the ones you like.

The family \(F = A w^n\). In polar form,

\[ \phi = A r^n\cos n\theta, \qquad \psi = A r^n\sin n\theta. \]

The equipotential \(\phi = 0\) is the pair of rays where \(\cos n\theta = 0\), the nearest at \(\theta = \pi/(2n)\). So \(Aw^n\) is the field inside a wedge of metal of opening angle \(\pi/(2n)\) measured from the line \(\theta = 0\), which itself has zero normal field (by the argument of the previous pre-requisite, if \(A\) is real). And \(|\mathcal{E}| = |F'| = nAr^{n-1}\):

  • \(n = 1\): \(\phi = Az\), a uniform field, between flat electrodes.
  • \(n = 2\): \(\phi = A(z^2 - y^2)\), zero on the rays at 45°: the hyperbolic electrodes of an electrostatic quadrupole, with field growing in proportion to \(r\).
  • \(n = 4/3\): zero on the ray \(\theta = 3\pi/8 = 67.5°\), field growing as \(r^{1/3}\).

Pierce’s electrodes, step by step. Pierce needs \(\phi = V(z/d)^{4/3}\) along the beam edge (\(\theta = 0\)) and \(\partial\phi/\partial y = 0\) there. Take \(F = V(w/d)^{4/3}\). Its real part is \(V(r/d)^{4/3}\cos(4\theta/3)\). On \(\theta = 0\) it equals \(V(z/d)^{4/3}\), and the previous pre-requisite showed its \(y\)-slope there is zero. Both conditions hold, so by the Cauchy argument of the Laplace section this is the potential outside the beam. Its equipotentials are the electrodes:

  • the cathode-potential electrode, \(\phi = 0\): the ray \(\theta = 67.5°\) from the cathode edge;
  • the anode, \(\phi = V\): the curve \(r = d\,[\cos(4\theta/3)]^{-3/4}\).

For the planar gap of 6.4 mm that the chapter attaches to Sinclair and Miller’s 50 kV gun, the anode passes through these points, with \(z\) along the beam from the cathode and \(y\) outward from its edge:

The field along the anode’s face grows with distance from the corner: \(|\mathcal{E}| = \tfrac43(V/d)(r/d)^{1/3}\), which is 10.4 MV/m where the anode meets the beam edge (\(r = d\)), against a mean of \(V/d = 7.8\) MV/m. The designer must check such peaks against breakdown (chapter 4). Real electrodes are cut off at a finite size, and round beams need numerical solutions, which is why every gun is then checked with a code.

Space-charge flow and the Pierce gun

Inside a gun the current is usually not set by the cathode but by the gap: once the cathode can give more than the Child-Langmuir current, the electrons in the gap form a cloud that turns the surplus back (chapter 2 derives the result). For a planar gap of width \(d\) at voltage \(V\),

\[ J_{CL} = \chi\,\frac{V^{3/2}}{d^2}, \qquad \chi = \frac{4\varepsilon_0}{9}\sqrt{\frac{2q}{m}} \]

with \(\chi = 2.334\times10^{-6}\) A/V\(^{3/2}\) for electrons and \(5.45\times10^{-8}\) A/V\(^{3/2}\) for protons. The potential in the gap is \(\phi(z) = V (z/d)^{4/3}\), with \(z\) measured from the cathode. A gun whose geometry is fixed and which runs space-charge limited therefore has a current proportional to \(V^{3/2}\), whatever its shape. The constant of proportionality is the perveance

\[ K = \frac{I}{V^{3/2}} \]

in A/V\(^{3/2}\), usually quoted in micropervs (\(10^{-6}\) A/V\(^{3/2}\)). It is a property of the geometry alone. The higher the perveance, the more the beam’s own space charge shapes it, and the more carefully the electrodes must be designed for it.

Numbers. In 1981 Charles Sinclair and Roger Miller at SLAC tested a GaAs photocathode gun designed to deliver 50 A at 50 kV from a 1 cm diameter cathode: a perveance of 4.5 µperv. A planar gap would carry that current from that area at a spacing of 6.4 mm. Their full gun was designed for 15 A at 200 kV, 0.17 µperv, from a 2 cm cathode; a planar gap would need 66 mm.

Pierce’s idea. Cut a slab out of an infinite planar diode to make a beam. The electrons at the edge of the slab now feel nothing pulling them outward from the missing charge beyond, but they still feel their neighbours’ repulsion, and the beam spreads. J. R. Pierce, in Rectilinear electron flow in beams (Journal of Applied Physics, 1940), asked for electrodes outside the beam that would make the potential along the beam’s edge exactly what it would have been inside the infinite diode. Then the edge electrons cannot tell that the rest of the diode is gone, and every electron moves in a straight line.

Take the edge of a sheet beam as the \(z\) axis (cathode at \(z = 0\)), with \(y\) measured outward from the beam into the empty region. Outside the beam there is no charge, so the potential obeys Laplace’s equation, and the boundary conditions on the edge \(y = 0\) are

\[ \phi(z, 0) = V\left(\frac{z}{d}\right)^{4/3}, \qquad \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}(z, 0) = 0. \]

The second says that no field crosses the edge. In two dimensions the real part of any analytic function of \(w = z + iy\) solves Laplace’s equation. Try

\[ \phi = V\,\mathrm{Re}\left[\left(\frac{w}{d}\right)^{4/3}\right]. \]

On \(y = 0\), \(w = z\) is real and \(\phi = V(z/d)^{4/3}\), as required. The derivative of \((w/d)^{4/3}\) with respect to \(w\) is real on the real axis, and \(\partial/\partial y = i\,\partial/\partial w\) for an analytic function, so \(\partial\phi/\partial y = \mathrm{Re}[\text{imaginary}] = 0\) there: the second condition holds too. Write \(w = r e^{i\theta}\), with \(\theta\) the angle from the beam edge into the empty region:

\[ \phi = V\left(\frac{r}{d}\right)^{4/3}\cos\frac{4\theta}{3}. \]

The electrode at cathode potential is the surface \(\phi = 0\), where \(4\theta/3 = \pi/2\), that is

\[ \theta = \frac{3\pi}{8} = 67.5^\circ. \]

So the focus electrode is a plane that leaves the cathode’s edge at 67.5° to the beam’s edge, and the anode is the curve \((r/d)^{4/3}\cos(4\theta/3) = 1\), which leaves the beam edge at right angles at \(z = d\) and then curves away from the cathode, downstream, running out towards the same 67.5° direction far from the beam. Pierce electrodes for round beams and for converging (spherical) flow are found the same way but need numerical solutions; Pierce’s 1949 book Theory and Design of Electron Beams collected them. The method assumes laminar flow, a cathode that emits uniformly at the space-charge limit, and no thermal velocities. The anode has a hole, which acts as a diverging lens; a real gun’s design corrects for it, and today every gun is checked with a ray-tracing code that solves Poisson’s equation and the trajectories together.

Field emission

At fields above about \(10^9\) V/m a metal emits electrons at room temperature by tunnelling through the barrier. Ralph Fowler and Lothar Nordheim explained it in 1928 with the free-electron model. The elementary Fowler-Nordheim law is

\[ J = \frac{a \mathcal{E}^2}{\phi_w}\exp\!\left(-\frac{b\,\phi_w^{3/2}}{\mathcal{E}}\right), \qquad a \approx 1.54\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{A\,eV\,V^{-2}},\quad b \approx 6.83\ \mathrm{eV^{-3/2}\,V\,nm^{-1}} \]

with \(\phi_w\) in eV and \(\mathcal{E}\) in V/nm, which gives \(J\) in A/nm² (1 A/nm² is \(10^{18}\) A/m²). A sharp tip multiplies the field, so a needle at a few kilovolts emits from a tiny area: a very bright source, used in electron microscopes, but at small currents. In accelerators field emission is mostly a nuisance. It is the dark current that flows from every surface of an RF gun or cavity at high gradient, and the first step of breakdown (chapter 4).

Pre-requisite (continued): Photons and the photoelectric effect

Pre-requisite. This section takes the photoelectric effect of chapters 1 and 3 to a photocathode: the threshold wavelengths of cathode materials against the common laser lines, the excess energy that sets the emittance, quantum efficiency as electrons per photon and the laser energy it implies, and why efficiency falls towards threshold. The photocathode section uses all four. Taught from Tong, Quantum Mechanics, section 6.4.2.

Reminder: F7, section 4 and chapter 1 gave the photon’s energy, \(\hbar\omega = hc/\lambda\); a handy form is \(\hbar\omega\,[\mathrm{eV}] = 1239.84/\lambda\,[\mathrm{nm}]\). Chapter 3 gave Einstein’s relation: a photon is absorbed whole by one electron, and the most energetic photoelectron leaves with \(W_{max} = \hbar\omega - \phi_w\), whatever the light’s intensity.

The excess energy. In a gun the cathode sits in a strong field, which lowers the barrier by the Schottky amount, so \(\phi_w\) is replaced by \(\phi_{\mathrm{eff}} = \phi_w - \Delta\phi_w\). Electrons from the Fermi level leave with up to \(\hbar\omega - \phi_{\mathrm{eff}}\), the excess energy, and those from deeper states with less. The excess energy is what sets the photocathode’s emittance in the chapter.

Bands, in brief. The table below and the rest of the chapter speak of semiconductors, which need a word of picture. In a single atom an electron’s energy takes only certain values (F7, section 7). In a crystal the levels of some \(10^{23}\) atoms lie so close together that they merge into bands, ranges of allowed energy, separated by gaps where an electron has no state at all. In a metal the highest occupied band is only partly filled, so an electron at the top, the Fermi level, can move into an empty state just above it: the metal conducts, and its work function is measured from there. In a semiconductor such as gallium arsenide (GaAs) the last filled band, the valence band, is full, and an empty band, the conduction band, lies above it across the band gap, 1.42 eV for GaAs at room temperature. A photon with more than the gap’s energy lifts an electron from the valence band into the conduction band, where it is free to move; the empty place it leaves behind moves like a positive particle and is called a hole. GaAs is a direct-gap semiconductor: the top of its valence band and the bottom of its conduction band sit at the same momentum of the electron in the crystal, so a photon, which brings almost no momentum, can make the jump alone. (Silicon is not; there the jump needs a vibration of the crystal to supply momentum, and light is absorbed weakly near the gap.) Whether a conduction electron can then leave the crystal depends on where the vacuum level lies; the section on polarised electrons takes that up.

Threshold. Emission needs \(\hbar\omega > \phi_w\), so a cathode responds only to wavelengths shorter than \(\lambda_t = hc/\phi_w\):

Surface \(\phi\) (eV) Threshold wavelength
Copper 4.31 288 nm
Copper, Schottky-lowered by 0.29 eV 4.02 308 nm
Caesium 2.14 579 nm
GaAs band gap 1.42 873 nm

And common laser lines. Nd:YAG (a crystal laser, neodymium in yttrium aluminium garnet) at 1064 nm is 1.17 eV. A nonlinear crystal can merge two photons into one of twice the energy, “doubling” the frequency, and repeat the trick: doubled to 532 nm it is 2.33 eV, quadrupled to 266 nm 4.66 eV; Ti:sapphire at 800 nm is 1.55 eV, and its third harmonic near 253 to 266 nm is used on copper. So copper needs ultraviolet, three or four harmonics of an infrared laser, while caesiated semiconductors work in the green or the near infrared.

Quantum efficiency. The quantum efficiency \(\eta_Q\) is electrons emitted per photon arriving. A bunch of charge \(Q\) holds \(Q/e\) electrons and needs \(Q/(e\eta_Q)\) photons, each of energy \(\hbar\omega\). In joules the laser energy is \(U = Q\hbar\omega/(e\eta_Q)\); with \(\hbar\omega\) written in eV the factor \(e\) cancels:

\[ U\,[\mathrm{J}] = \frac{Q\,[\mathrm{C}]\times\hbar\omega\,[\mathrm{eV}]}{\eta_Q}. \]

For the LCLS: 1 nC at 4.90 eV and \(\eta_Q = 3.7\times10^{-5}\) needs \(10^{-9}\times4.90/3.7\times10^{-5} = 132\) µJ on the cathode, \(1.7\times10^{14}\) photons. For GaAs at 8 per cent and 2.33 eV, 29 nJ. Since the ultraviolet itself is made from infrared with a few per cent efficiency, a factor of a thousand in \(\eta_Q\) is the difference between a large laser and a small one.

Why efficiency falls near threshold. Photons excite electrons from a spread of states below the Fermi level. Only those excited above the barrier, and moving towards the surface steeply enough, get out. As \(\hbar\omega\) approaches \(\phi_{\mathrm{eff}}\), both the range of states that qualify and the range of escape directions shrink. For a metal near threshold Fowler’s result (1931) is that the yield grows as the square of the excess energy, \(\eta_Q \propto (\hbar\omega - \phi_{\mathrm{eff}})^2\), which Dowell and Schmerge’s three-step model reproduces. The emittance, on the other hand, goes as the square root of the excess energy. Tuning towards threshold therefore costs efficiency faster than it gains emittance.

At work. The chapter’s copper cathode with \(\phi_{\mathrm{eff}} = 4.56\) eV: at 4.90 eV the excess energy is 0.34 eV. At 4.66 eV (266 nm) it is 0.10 eV. The emittance falls by \(\sqrt{0.10/0.34} = 0.54\), but the quantum efficiency falls by \((0.10/0.34)^2 = 0.087\), so the laser must deliver 11.6 times more photons: starting from the LCLS figure of \(3.7\times10^{-5}\), about 1.5 mJ of ultraviolet for 1 nC.

Pre-requisite: Spin and magnetic moments

Pre-requisite. This section teaches what the photocathode and polarised-source sections need from quantum angular momentum: the quantum numbers \(j\) and \(m_j\), how an orbital angular momentum and a spin combine (Clebsch-Gordan coefficients, built by hand for the case GaAs needs), the photon’s angular momentum and the selection rule \(\Delta m_j = +1\) for circularly polarised light, the 3 to 1 ratio that limits GaAs to 50 per cent polarisation, the definition of polarisation, and the magnetic moment of the electron: its energy in a field, Stern-Gerlach separation in a sextupole, and the hyperfine coupling that hands polarisation from electron to proton. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, Quantum Mechanics, §27, §31, §54 and §106; and Tong, Quantum Mechanics, sections 4.2.1, 7.4, 12.1.6 and 13.3.3.

Reminder: F7, section 9 introduced spin, the Bohr magneton and the Stern-Gerlach experiment in a paragraph each. This section gives them the arithmetic the sources need.

Angular momentum in quantum mechanics. A quantum angular momentum \(\mathbf{J}\) cannot point in an arbitrary direction with an arbitrary size. Its square takes only the values \(\hbar^2 j(j+1)\), with \(j\) a whole or half-whole number, and its component along a chosen axis \(z\) only the values \(\hbar m_j\), with \(m_j\) running from \(-j\) to \(+j\) in steps of one. So \(j = 1\) allows \(m_j = -1, 0, 1\), and \(j = 3/2\) allows four values, \(\pm3/2\) and \(\pm1/2\). Write the state as \(|j, m_j\rangle\); the bracket is only a label for the state, holding its two numbers. In quantum mechanics a measurable quantity such as \(J_z\) is an operator, a rule that acts on a state to give another state; on these states \(J_z\) just multiplies by \(\hbar m_j\). Here \(\hbar = 1.0546\times10^{-34}\) J s.

Orbital motion gives whole \(j\), written \(l\): an electron in an \(s\) state has \(l = 0\), in a \(p\) state \(l = 1\). The electron also carries an intrinsic angular momentum, its spin, with \(s = 1/2\): two states, \(m_s = +1/2\) (“up”, \(\uparrow\)) and \(m_s = -1/2\) (“down”, \(\downarrow\)), along whatever axis we choose.

Stepping operators. The operators \(J_\pm = J_x \pm iJ_y\) raise or lower \(m_j\) by one without changing \(j\) (Landau and Lifshitz, §27):

\[ J_-|j, m\rangle = \hbar\sqrt{j(j+1) - m(m-1)}\,|j, m-1\rangle, \]

and \(J_-\) on the lowest state, \(m = -j\), gives zero.

Adding an orbit and a spin. In gallium arsenide the top of the valence band is made of \(p\)-like orbitals (\(l = 1\)), each holding an electron with spin. Orbit and spin combine to a total \(\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{L} + \mathbf{S}\), with \(j = 3/2\) or \(1/2\); the spin-orbit interaction separates the two, putting \(j = 1/2\) (the “split-off” band) 0.34 eV lower. We need the \(j = 3/2\) states written in terms of the orbital and spin states \(|l, m_l\rangle|m_s\rangle\).

The top state is simple: \(m_j = 3/2\) needs \(m_l = 1\) and \(m_s = +1/2\), so \(|3/2, 3/2\rangle = |1, 1\rangle\!\uparrow\). Now apply \(J_- = L_- + S_-\) to both sides. On the left, \(J_-|3/2, 3/2\rangle = \hbar\sqrt{15/4 - 3/4}\,|3/2, 1/2\rangle = \hbar\sqrt3\,|3/2, 1/2\rangle\). On the right, \(L_-|1, 1\rangle = \hbar\sqrt2\,|1, 0\rangle\) and \(S_-\!\uparrow\, = \hbar\!\downarrow\). So

\[ |3/2, 1/2\rangle = \sqrt{\tfrac23}\,|1, 0\rangle\!\uparrow + \sqrt{\tfrac13}\,|1, 1\rangle\!\downarrow. \]

Apply \(J_-\) once more. The left gives \(\hbar\sqrt{15/4 + 1/4} = 2\hbar\) times \(|3/2, -1/2\rangle\). On the right, \(L_-|1, 0\rangle = \hbar\sqrt2\,|1, -1\rangle\), \(L_-|1, 1\rangle = \hbar\sqrt2\,|1, 0\rangle\), \(S_-\!\uparrow\, = \hbar\!\downarrow\) and \(S_-\!\downarrow\, = 0\). Collecting terms and dividing by \(2\hbar\),

\[ |3/2, -1/2\rangle = \sqrt{\tfrac13}\,|1, -1\rangle\!\uparrow + \sqrt{\tfrac23}\,|1, 0\rangle\!\downarrow, \qquad |3/2, -3/2\rangle = |1, -1\rangle\!\downarrow. \]

The numbers \(\sqrt{1/3}\), \(\sqrt{2/3}\) and 1 are Clebsch-Gordan coefficients; Landau and Lifshitz, §106, tabulates them in general, in the form of 3j-symbols. Their squares are the probabilities of finding each orbital and spin combination inside the state of definite \(j\) and \(m_j\).

The photon’s angular momentum and the selection rule. A circularly polarised photon carries angular momentum \(\pm\hbar\) along its direction of motion; the sign is its helicity. When it is absorbed, angular momentum is conserved, so with \(z\) along the light and positive helicity, \(m_j\) of the absorbing electron rises by one: \(\Delta m_j = +1\). In an electric-dipole transition the light acts on the electron’s orbit, not directly on its spin, so the orbital \(m_l\) rises by one and the spin is carried along unchanged (Tong, section 13.3.3).

Why GaAs gives 50 per cent. The bottom of the conduction band is \(s\)-like, \(|0, 0\rangle\) with spin up or down. To reach \(m_l = 0\) by \(\Delta m_l = +1\), the electron must start in \(m_l = -1\). Look at which valence states contain \(|1, -1\rangle\):

  • \(|3/2, -3/2\rangle = |1, -1\rangle\!\downarrow\): amplitude 1, ending as \(|0, 0\rangle\!\downarrow\), spin down.
  • \(|3/2, -1/2\rangle\): amplitude \(\sqrt{1/3}\) in \(|1, -1\rangle\!\uparrow\), ending as \(|0, 0\rangle\!\uparrow\), spin up.
  • \(|3/2, +1/2\rangle\) and \(|3/2, +3/2\rangle\) contain no \(|1, -1\rangle\) and cannot absorb.

The orbital part of the transition, from \(|1, -1\rangle\) to \(|0, 0\rangle\), is the same in both allowed cases. So the rates are in the ratio of the squared amplitudes, \(1 : 1/3\), that is 3 : 1, spin down to spin up. Photons of energy between the gap (1.42 eV at room temperature) and the gap plus 0.34 eV cannot reach the split-off band, which would spoil the ratio; in wavelength that is 873 nm to 704 nm. Define the polarisation along \(z\) as

\[ P = \frac{N_\uparrow - N_\downarrow}{N_\uparrow + N_\downarrow}, \]

then \(P = (1 - 3)/(1 + 3) = -50\) per cent: half the electrons’ spins are “spare”, and the beam is 50 per cent polarised against the photon’s spin. Reversing the light’s helicity reverses \(P\); a Pockels cell (a crystal that changes the light’s polarisation when a voltage is put across it) does this electrically, pulse by pulse, which is how parity experiments flip the beam’s spin without touching anything else. The heavy-hole (\(m_j = \pm3/2\)) and light-hole (\(m_j = \pm1/2\)) states have the same energy at the band edge in a perfect crystal. Strain splits them; light tuned to excite only the heavy holes would give, in principle, 100 per cent, and strained and layered cathodes have given above 80 per cent.

At work. In a parity experiment the statistical error of an asymmetry measured with \(N\) electrons is about \(1/(P\sqrt N)\), so the running time for a given precision goes as \(1/(P^2 I)\). Raising \(P\) from 50 to 85 per cent saves a factor \((0.85/0.5)^2 = 2.9\) in beam time, as much as tripling the current.

Magnetic moments. A spinning charge is a small magnet. The electron’s magnetic moment is opposite to its spin and, to 0.1 per cent, of size one Bohr magneton,

\[ \mu_B = \frac{e\hbar}{2m_e} = 9.274\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{J/T} = 57.9\ \mathrm{\mu eV/T}. \]

In a field \(B\) its energy is \(U = -\boldsymbol{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{B} = \pm\mu_B B\) for spin along or against the field. A state whose energy rises with \(|B|\) is pushed towards weak field: it is a “weak-field seeker”. The proton’s moment is 658 times smaller.

Stern-Gerlach separation in a sextupole. Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach (1922) split a beam of silver atoms in two with a non-uniform field: the force \(-\nabla U\) pushes the two spin states opposite ways. A polarised-ion source uses a sextupole (taught below), whose field magnitude grows as \(|B| = B_p(r/r_p)^2\) from zero on the axis to \(B_p\) at the pole radius \(r_p\). For hydrogen atoms in the weak-field-seeking state, \(U = \mu_B B_p r^2/r_p^2\) and the radial force is

\[ F_r = -\frac{dU}{dr} = -\frac{2\mu_B B_p}{r_p^2}\,r, \]

a restoring force proportional to the offset: the atoms oscillate about the axis and are focused. The other state feels the same force outward and is thrown to the walls. With \(B_p = 1.0\) T at \(r_p = 5\) mm, the spring constant is \(7.4\times10^{-19}\) N/m and, for a hydrogen atom of mass \(1.67\times10^{-27}\) kg, \(\omega = 2.1\times10^4\) s⁻¹. An atom moving at 1500 m/s oscillates with wavenumber \(\omega/v = 14\) m⁻¹, so atoms leaving a point on the axis return to it after \(\pi/14 = 0.22\) m. The magnet accepts only atoms whose transverse speed is below \(\omega r_p = 105\) m/s, transverse energies below \(\mu_B B_p = 58\) µeV, or 0.67 K: which is why the atoms come from a dissociator (a discharge that splits hydrogen molecules into atoms) with a cold nozzle.

Hyperfine coupling. In a hydrogen atom the electron’s and the proton’s magnetic moments interact. In zero field their spins combine into a total \(F = 1\) (three states) or \(F = 0\) (one), split by \(h\times1420.4\) MHz \(= 5.87\) µeV, the 21 cm line of radio astronomy (Tong, section 12.1.6). The coupling is weak against a field when \(\mu_B B\) exceeds the splitting: the crossover is near \(B_c = 5.87\ \mathrm{\mu eV}/(2\times57.9\ \mathrm{\mu eV/T}) = 51\) mT. Well above \(B_c\), electron and proton spins are separately defined. Label the four states by \((m_s, m_I)\), \(m_I\) being the proton’s: 1 = \((+,+)\), 2 = \((+,-)\), 3 = \((-,-)\), 4 = \((-,+)\). The sextupole passes states 1 and 2: electron spin all up, proton spin half up and half down, so no nuclear polarisation yet. An RF field at the frequency that joins state 2 to state 4, in a carefully shaped static field, moves the atoms of state 2 into state 4. Now states 1 and 4 remain, both with \(m_I = +\): the protons are fully polarised and the electrons not at all. Joining 1 to 3 instead gives the opposite sign. The atoms are then ionised in a strong field, where the spins are uncoupled and the proton keeps its orientation.

Photocathodes

Light a surface with photons of energy \(\hbar\omega\) greater than its work function and it emits electrons, only while the light is on. The beam then copies the laser pulse in time and space: bunches of picoseconds, at any pattern, with no grid and no buncher. The cost is a laser and a cathode that must be kept clean.

Quantum efficiency. The quantum efficiency \(\eta_Q\) is the number of electrons emitted per incident photon. A bunch of charge \(Q\) needs laser energy

\[ U = \frac{Q}{e\,\eta_Q}\,\hbar\omega. \]

Metals have \(\eta_Q\) of order \(10^{-5}\) to \(10^{-4}\) in the ultraviolet, because most excited electrons lose energy before reaching the surface and most light is reflected. The LCLS copper cathode at SLAC reached \(3.7\times10^{-5}\); at 253 nm (\(\hbar\omega = 4.90\) eV) a 1 nC bunch then needs 132 µJ on the cathode. Semiconductors with a caesium-lowered surface do far better. Sinclair and Miller’s GaAs cathodes gave 1 per cent (“a poor performance”) to 8 per cent (“a very good result”); at 8 per cent and 532 nm (2.33 eV) a nanocoulomb needs 29 nJ of light. The price is vacuum: they ran at 2 to 3 × 10⁻¹¹ Torr, and still their room-temperature cathodes slowly lost caesium and needed brief re-caesiation, while cathodes cooled to 77 K lasted one to several days before gas frozen onto them had to be cleaned off. Caesium telluride (sensitive only in the ultraviolet, below about 320 nm) and the alkali antimonides (Fraser’s group chose green light at 532 nm, near the peak response of GaAs and Cs₃Sb) lie between copper and GaAs: far more efficient than copper, less fragile than GaAs.

The thermal emittance of a photocathode. Here is a simple model that gives the standard result. Photons lift electrons from near the Fermi level. Those that escape leave with kinetic energies \(W\) from zero up to the excess energy \(\hbar\omega - \phi_{\mathrm{eff}}\), where \(\phi_{\mathrm{eff}} = \phi_w - \Delta\phi_w\) is the work function lowered by the Schottky effect. Assume the escaping electrons’ \(W\) is spread uniformly over that range, so \(\langle W\rangle = (\hbar\omega - \phi_{\mathrm{eff}})/2\), and that their directions fill the outward hemisphere uniformly, so \(\langle p_x^2\rangle = \frac{1}{3}\langle p^2\rangle = \frac{2}{3}m\langle W\rangle\). Then

\[ \langle p_x^2\rangle = \frac{m(\hbar\omega - \phi_{\mathrm{eff}})}{3}, \qquad \varepsilon_n = \sigma_x\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega - \phi_{\mathrm{eff}}}{3 m c^2}}. \]

David Dowell and John Schmerge (2009) derived the same expression from a full three-step model with the Fermi-Dirac distribution and refraction at the surface, valid when the excess energy is small compared with the Fermi energy. The physics is that the emittance is set by the excess energy, not by the cathode’s temperature. Tune the laser close to threshold and the emittance falls, but so does the quantum efficiency; there is no free lunch.

Numbers. For copper, Dowell and Schmerge use \(\phi_w = 4.31\) eV. At the LCLS, with 4.90 eV photons and a cathode field of 55 to 58 MV/m at the moment of emission, the Schottky lowering is 0.28 to 0.29 eV, so the excess energy would be 0.88 eV and \(\varepsilon_n/\sigma_x = 0.76\) µm per mm. But Dowell and Schmerge point out that the same effective work function must explain both the emittance and the quantum efficiency, and a measured quantum efficiency of \(5\times10^{-5}\) implies \(\phi_{\mathrm{eff}} = 4.56\) eV, a higher barrier, and a theoretical slope of 0.46 µm per mm. The LCLS measured about twice that, about 0.9 µm per mm, as had BNL and others. The LCLS laser spot was a 1.3 mm diameter disc, \(\sigma_x = 0.325\) mm, so the cathode alone gives about 0.15 µm (theory) to 0.3 µm (measured), out of a slice emittance of 0.9 µm at 135 MeV. (The slice emittance is that of a thin slice of the bunch, taken at one moment of its passage; the projected emittance, of the whole bunch at once, is larger, for reasons the section on emittance compensation explains.) The cathode is a third of the final emittance.

Polarised electrons from GaAs. Gallium arsenide is a direct-gap semiconductor. Near its band edge the valence band’s top states have total angular momentum \(j = 3/2\) (heavy holes with \(m_j = \pm 3/2\), light holes with \(m_j = \pm 1/2\)), and the conduction band’s bottom states have \(j = 1/2\). Circularly polarised light of photon energy just above the gap, about 1.5 eV, carries angular momentum \(+1\) along the beam and drives \(\Delta m_j = +1\). Two transitions are possible: \(m_j = -3/2 \to -1/2\) and \(m_j = -1/2 \to +1/2\). The squares of the Clebsch-Gordan coefficients make the first three times as likely as the second. The conduction electrons therefore have spin down and spin up in the ratio 3 to 1, a polarisation

\[ P = \frac{3 - 1}{3 + 1} = 50\ \text{per cent}. \]

To let these electrons out, the surface is coated with caesium and oxygen until the vacuum level lies below the bottom of the conduction band (“negative electron affinity”), so electrons that thermalise to the band edge can still escape. D. T. Pierce and F. Meier described photoemission of spin-polarised electrons from GaAs in 1976. SLAC built a GaAs gun for the parity-violation experiment of C. Y. Prescott and colleagues, published in 1978, the measurement of an asymmetry in deep-inelastic scattering of longitudinally polarised electrons, a test of the electroweak theory. Sinclair and Miller report that the source ran 24 hours a day for six weeks with 93 per cent availability, cathodes near 77 K to improve the polarisation. The 50 per cent limit was broken by straining the crystal so that the heavy- and light-hole levels split: T. Maruyama and colleagues reported strain-enhanced polarisation in 1991. Polarised electrons are a requirement of linear colliders (chapter 25).

Pre-requisite (continued): Relativistic motion in electric and magnetic fields

Pre-requisite. This section adds one result to what chapters 1 and 3 taught about motion in fields: why the space-charge force between particles moving together falls as \(1/\gamma^2\), which is the reason RF guns exist. It then puts numbers into the gyration and the \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\times\mathbf{B}\) drift for the magnetron and ECR sections. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, The Classical Theory of Fields, §21, §22 and §38; Tong, Electromagnetism, sections 2.1.3 and 3.1.1 (the fields of a line charge and a straight current); Brown, sections 2.4.1 and 2.4.2; and Wangler, section 9.5.

Reminder: F4, section 10 gave the magnetic force and the circular orbit at frequency \(qB/2\pi m\), and F4, section 12 the field \(\mu_0I/2\pi r\) of a straight current. Chapter 1 gave the Lorentz force law \(d\mathbf{p}/dt = q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})\) with \(\mathbf{p} = \gamma m\mathbf{v}\), and the circle of radius \(p_\perp/qB\) at angular frequency \(qB/\gamma m\) in a magnetic field. Chapter 3 added the helix and the drift in crossed fields, \(\mathbf{v}_d = \boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\times\mathbf{B}/B^2\), with the cycloid of an electron released at rest, which swings at most \(2m\mathcal{E}/(qB^2)\) along \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) before turning back.

Repulsion and attraction in a moving beam. Take a long round beam of charge \(\lambda\) per metre (C/m), all moving along \(z\) at speed \(v = \beta c\). Gauss’s law gives a radial electric field at radius \(r\) outside it of \(\mathcal{E}_r = \lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 r)\) (the next pre-requisite does the inside too). The same beam is a current \(I = \lambda v\), and Ampère’s law gives an azimuthal magnetic field \(B_\theta = \mu_0 I/(2\pi r) = \mu_0\lambda v/(2\pi r)\). Since \(\mu_0\varepsilon_0 = 1/c^2\), \(B_\theta = (v/c^2)\mathcal{E}_r\). A particle of the beam, moving with the same \(v\), feels a radial force

\[ F_r = q(\mathcal{E}_r - vB_\theta) = q\mathcal{E}_r\left(1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\right) = \frac{q\mathcal{E}_r}{\gamma^2}. \]

The electric push outward and the magnetic pull inward (parallel currents attract) nearly cancel at high speed. The same factor \(1 - \beta^2\) appears inside the beam, and between any two charges moving side by side, since the magnetic field of a moving charge is always \(\mathbf{v}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}/c^2\) (Landau and Lifshitz, §38, derive the field of a uniformly moving charge). The factor is \(1/\gamma^2\) for a given charge density in the laboratory.

Beam \(\gamma\) \(1/\gamma^2\)
Electrons leaving a cathode at 1 eV 1.000002 1.000
Electrons from a 100 kV DC gun 1.196 0.70
Electrons leaving an S-band RF gun at 6 MeV (S-band: microwaves of 2 to 4 GHz, here 2856 MHz) 12.7 0.0062
H⁻ ions in a 65 keV LEBT 1.00007 1.000

This is the whole case for the RF gun: get the electrons from 1 to 0.006 in the first few centimetres. For ions in a source, nothing helps; their beams remain at \(1/\gamma^2 \approx 1\) until well beyond the first accelerator.

Gyration, with the numbers a source needs. The angular frequency \(\omega_c = qB/\gamma m\) does not depend on the speed (for \(\gamma \approx 1\)), only on the field. For electrons, \(f_{ce} = \omega_c/2\pi = 28.0\) GHz per tesla (Brown, equation 2.33); for protons, 15.2 MHz per tesla. In 0.1 T an electron gyrates at 2.80 GHz. A 100 eV electron (\(v = 5.9\times10^6\) m/s) has a gyroradius \(r_g = m_ev/(eB) = 0.34\) mm; a 1 eV proton has 1.4 mm. These are the chapter’s numbers for the magnetron, and the 28 GHz per tesla is the ECR condition read backwards: 2.45 GHz needs 87.5 mT.

At work: the magnetron’s crossed fields. Take \(\mathcal{E} = 10^5\) V/m (100 V across 1 mm) and \(B = 0.1\) T. Both species drift at \(\mathcal{E}/B = 10^6\) m/s around the cathode. But an electron born at rest swings only \(2m_e\mathcal{E}/(eB^2) = 0.11\) mm towards the anode before the field turns it back: it is trapped, circling the cathode and ionising gas as it goes. A proton would need to swing \(2m_p\mathcal{E}/(eB^2) = 0.21\) m; in a 1 mm gap it simply crosses to the cathode as if the field were not there. That is the chapter’s “the field traps the electrons but hardly the ions”.

Pre-requisite (continued): Gauss’s law and potentials of simple shapes

Pre-requisite. This section applies Gauss’s law to three charge shapes the chapter meets: a thin sheet of charge just leaving a cathode (the surface-charge limit), a slab of electrons displaced from its ions (the plasma oscillation), and a uniform round beam inside a metal pipe (the potential in a low-energy beam line, with its \(1 + 2\ln(b/a)\)). The RF gun, plasma and LEBT sections use them. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, sections 2.1.3, 2.1.4 and 2.2.1, and Wangler, section 11.1 and problem 9.12.

Reminder: F4, section 4, chapter 1 and the later chapters gave Gauss’s law, the field \(\sigma/\varepsilon_0\) just outside a charged conductor, the field \(\lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 r)\) of a line charge, and the potential as the integral of the field.

A sheet leaving a cathode. A bunch of charge \(Q\) (C), just emitted from a disc of area \(A\) (m²), is a thin sheet of surface charge \(\sigma = Q/A\) in front of a metal surface. The metal holds an equal and opposite image charge, so between sheet and cathode the field of the bunch is \(\sigma/\varepsilon_0\) (as between the plates of a capacitor), and it points so as to pull the electrons back. The applied field \(\mathcal{E}\) pulls them away. Further electrons can leave only while \(\mathcal{E} > \sigma/\varepsilon_0\), so the most charge a field can draw is

\[ Q_{max} = \varepsilon_0 \mathcal{E} A. \]

At 57 MV/m over a disc 1.3 mm across (\(A = 1.33\times10^{-6}\) m²) this is 0.67 nC; at 60 MV/m over 2.0 mm, 1.67 nC. The sheet picture needs the bunch to stay thin while it is emitted, which the chapter checks.

A displaced slab. A plasma holds electrons and ions at the same density \(n\) (m⁻³). Shift all the electrons in a wide slab sideways by a small distance \(\xi\) (m). On one face a layer of thickness \(\xi\) is left with only ions, of surface charge \(+ne\xi\); on the other face a layer of electrons with no ions, \(-ne\xi\). These two sheets make a field between them of

\[ \mathcal{E} = \frac{ne\xi}{\varepsilon_0}, \]

pointing so as to pull the electrons back, with no field outside. For \(n = 10^{17}\) m⁻³ a shift of only 1 µm makes 1.8 kV/m. The restoring force \(-e\mathcal{E}\) is proportional to \(\xi\), so the electrons oscillate: the plasma frequency of the chapter, solved in the next pre-requisite.

A round beam in a pipe. Take a beam of uniform charge density, radius \(a\) (m), carrying charge \(\lambda\) per metre, centred in a grounded metal pipe of radius \(b\). By symmetry the field is radial. Gauss’s law on a cylinder of radius \(r\) and length \(\ell\) encloses \(\lambda\ell\) for \(r > a\), and only the fraction \(r^2/a^2\) of it for \(r < a\). The flux is \(\mathcal{E}_r\cdot2\pi r\ell\), so

\[ \mathcal{E}_r = \frac{\lambda r}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 a^2}\quad (r \le a), \qquad \mathcal{E}_r = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\quad (a \le r \le b). \]

Inside, the field grows linearly from zero on the axis; outside, it falls as \(1/r\). Integrate inward from the pipe (\(\phi = 0\)) to get the potential. Across the empty gap,

\[ \phi(a) = \int_a^b\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\,dr = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{b}{a}, \]

and inside the beam, from its edge to the axis,

\[ \phi(0) - \phi(a) = \int_0^a\frac{\lambda r}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 a^2}\,dr = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}. \]

Adding,

\[ \phi(0) = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(1 + 2\ln\frac{b}{a}\right). \]

A beam of current \(I\) moving at speed \(v\) carries \(\lambda = I/v\), which gives the chapter’s formula. The 1 is the drop inside the beam, which pushes it apart; the \(2\ln(b/a)\) is the drop across the empty space, which raises the whole beam’s potential but exerts no force inside it.

At work. For 60 mA of H⁻ at 65 keV, \(v = 3.53\times10^6\) m/s (\(\beta = 0.0118\)), so \(\lambda = 1.7\times10^{-8}\) C/m. With \(a = 5\) mm and \(b = 50\) mm, \(\lambda/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) = 153\) V and \(\phi(0) = 153\times(1 + 2\ln10) = 857\) V. The field at the beam edge is \(\lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 a) = 61\) kV/m. That field, acting on slow ions with nothing to cancel it, is what the LEBT fights.

Pre-requisite: Simple and damped harmonic oscillator

Pre-requisite. This section solves the equation \(x'' + k^2x = 0\) and reads off its wavelength and half period; shows how to linearise a nonlinear equation about its equilibrium, which turns almost any small motion into this one; and applies both to the plasma oscillation and to the envelope equation of emittance compensation. It ends with damping, which the driven oscillator of the ECR section builds on. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §21 and §25, and Tong, Classical Mechanics, section 2.5.1.

Reminder: F3, sections 9 and 10 solved the mass on a spring in time, with and without damping, and F5, section 4 defined its quality factor. Here the same equation appears with distance along a beam line as its variable, and as the tool for any small motion about an equilibrium.

The equation. Many things obey “the further you go, the harder you are pulled back, in proportion”. With \(x\) the displacement and the independent variable either time \(t\) or distance \(s\) along a beam line,

\[ x'' + k^2 x = 0, \]

where the prime means a derivative with respect to \(s\) and \(k\) (m⁻¹) is a constant. (With time as the variable, write \(\ddot x + \omega^2 x = 0\), \(\omega\) in rad/s.) Try \(x = \cos ks\): its second derivative is \(-k^2\cos ks\), so it works; so does \(\sin ks\), and so does any sum

\[ x(s) = C_1\cos ks + C_2\sin ks = A\cos(ks - \varphi_0), \]

with \(C_1 = x(0)\) and \(C_2 = x'(0)/k\) fixed by the starting position and slope. A second-order equation needs two starting values, and this solution has two constants, so it is the general one. The motion repeats every wavelength \(2\pi/k\). It changes sign after a half period, a distance \(\pi/k\): particles that start displaced one way are displaced the other way, by the same amount, half a period later, whatever their starting amplitude. In time, the period is \(2\pi/\omega\) and the frequency \(f = \omega/2\pi\).

The quantity \(x^2 + x'^2/k^2\) stays constant (differentiate it and use the equation), so in the plane of \(x\) and \(x'/k\) the motion is a circle. That is the simplest phase-space picture, and the one the next chapters build on.

Linearising. Most equations are not this simple. Suppose \(x'' = F(x)\), with \(F\) any smooth function, and suppose there is an equilibrium \(x_e\) where \(F(x_e) = 0\): a particle placed there with no slope stays there. Write \(x = x_e + \Delta x\) and expand \(F\) to first order, the first two terms of its Taylor series (F1, section 8):

\[ F(x_e + \Delta x) \approx F(x_e) + F'(x_e)\,\Delta x = F'(x_e)\,\Delta x. \]

So \(\Delta x'' = F'(x_e)\,\Delta x\). If \(F'(x_e) < 0\), this is the oscillator with \(k^2 = -F'(x_e)\): the equilibrium is stable and small departures oscillate. If \(F'(x_e) > 0\) they grow exponentially. The approximation holds while \(\Delta x\) is small enough that the next term of the expansion, \(\tfrac12F''(x_e)\Delta x^2\), is negligible.

At work: emittance compensation. The chapter’s slice envelope obeys \(\sigma_r'' = F(\sigma_r) = -k_f^2\sigma_r + K_s/\sigma_r\). The equilibrium is where \(F = 0\): \(\sigma_{eq}^2 = K_s/k_f^2\). The slope is \(F'(\sigma) = -k_f^2 - K_s/\sigma^2\), which at equilibrium is \(-k_f^2 - k_f^2 = -2k_f^2\). So \(\Delta\sigma'' + 2k_f^2\Delta\sigma = 0\), oscillation at wavenumber \(\sqrt2\,k_f\), independent of \(K_s\). With \(k_f = 2\) m⁻¹ the half period is \(\pi/(2\sqrt2) = 1.11\) m. Integrating the full nonlinear equation numerically from a 5 per cent offset gives successive zero crossings 1.10 and 1.12 m apart: the linear answer, with a small correction for the finite amplitude.

At work: the plasma oscillation. The previous pre-requisite found that electrons displaced by \(\xi\) in a plasma of density \(n\) feel a field \(ne\xi/\varepsilon_0\) pulling them back. Newton’s law for one electron is

\[ m_e\ddot\xi = -\frac{ne^2}{\varepsilon_0}\xi \quad\Rightarrow\quad \ddot\xi + \omega_p^2\xi = 0, \qquad \omega_p = \sqrt{\frac{ne^2}{\varepsilon_0 m_e}}. \]

The restoring force is linear in \(\xi\), so the oscillation is exactly harmonic, at a frequency that depends only on the density:

\(n\) (m⁻³) \(f_p = \omega_p/2\pi\) Period
\(10^{16}\) 0.90 GHz 1.11 ns
\(10^{17}\) 2.84 GHz 0.35 ns
\(10^{18}\) 8.98 GHz 0.11 ns

Brown’s handy form is \(f_p = 8980\sqrt{n}\) Hz with \(n\) in cm⁻³.

Damping. Add a drag proportional to velocity, as resistance does in a circuit and wall losses in a cavity:

\[ \ddot x + 2\Gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2 x = 0, \]

with \(\Gamma\) in s⁻¹. Try \(x = e^{\lambda t}\): then \(\lambda^2 + 2\Gamma\lambda + \omega_0^2 = 0\), so \(\lambda = -\Gamma \pm i\sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \Gamma^2}\). When the damping is light (\(\Gamma < \omega_0\)) the solution is

\[ x = A e^{-\Gamma t}\cos(\omega_d t - \varphi_0), \qquad \omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \Gamma^2}: \]

an oscillation whose amplitude decays by \(1/e\) in a time \(1/\Gamma\), and whose energy (proportional to amplitude squared) decays in \(1/(2\Gamma)\). The quality factor counts how many radians of oscillation the energy lasts: \(Q_0 = \omega_0/(2\Gamma)\) (written \(Q_0\) here because \(Q\) is a bunch charge in this chapter; for a cavity, \(Q_0\) is the course’s symbol). The driven oscillator, before the ECR section, uses it.

Pre-requisite: Resonant cavity modes

Pre-requisite. This section gives the two facts about cavities the RF gun section needs before chapter 13 treats cavities in full. First, the field of a standing wave on a cavity’s end wall: everywhere in step, rising from a zero crossing to a peak a quarter period later, so the moment the laser fires sets the field the electrons feel. Second, the modes of two coupled cells, the 0-mode and the π-mode, and how far apart in frequency they lie. Taught from Wangler, sections 1.12, 4.6 and 4.7.

A standing wave in a pillbox. The simplest accelerating cavity is a closed metal cylinder, a pillbox, of radius \(R_c\) (m). Like a string fixed at both ends (F5, section 10), it holds standing waves only at certain frequencies. Its lowest useful resonance, called TM₀₁₀, has an electric field along the axis that does not vary along the axis at all, falls to zero at the curved wall, and oscillates in time:

\[ \mathcal{E}_z(r, t) = \mathcal{E}_0\,J_0\!\left(\frac{2.405\,r}{R_c}\right)\sin\omega t. \]

\(J_0\) is the Bessel function of chapter 2, equal to 1 on the axis, and 2.405 is its first zero, which puts the zero of field on the wall, where a tangential electric field must vanish (Wangler, section 1.12). Putting the wave equation’s solution together with that condition fixes the frequency: \(\omega = 2.405\,c/R_c\). At 2856 MHz, \(R_c = 2.405c/(2\pi f) = 40.2\) mm.

Two features matter for a gun. The field has the same time dependence everywhere: it is a standing wave, not a travelling one. And the end walls are flat metal surfaces on which \(\mathcal{E}_z\) is largest, so a cathode set into the centre of an end wall sits at the cavity’s peak field. Here the course’s sine convention for RF phase is natural: write the field on the cathode as

\[ \mathcal{E}(\varphi) = \mathcal{E}_{peak}\sin\varphi, \qquad \varphi = \omega t, \]

so \(\varphi = 0\) is the zero crossing where the field is about to pull electrons off, and \(\varphi = 90°\) the peak. At 2856 MHz one period is 350 ps, and one degree of phase is 0.97 ps.

At work. The LCLS gun’s peak field on the cathode is 115 MV/m. Fired 30° after the zero crossing, the laser frees electrons into \(115\sin30° = 57.5\) MV/m: the chapter’s “about half”. Why not fire at the peak? The electrons start from rest and take a good fraction of a period to cross the half cell, so they slip in phase as they go, and the launch phase decides the fields they meet all the way out. The best launch phase is found by tracking; guns of this type run near 30° because that gives the least emittance and energy spread at the exit. A 10 ps laser pulse covers 10.3° of phase, so the head and tail of the bunch, emitted at about 25° and 35°, see 48.6 and 66.0 MV/m.

Two coupled cells. The S-band gun is two cells joined by an iris: a half cell holding the cathode and a full cell. Each cell alone would resonate at nearly the same frequency, \(\omega_0\). Joined, they behave like two identical pendulums linked by a weak spring. Two pendulums so linked have two natural ways to swing, normal modes: together, in phase, or against each other, in opposite phase (F5, section 7 works them out). Each has its own frequency, slightly different from \(\omega_0\).

Wangler (sections 4.6 and 4.7) models each cell as a resonant circuit, coupled to its neighbour through a mutual inductance (a current changing in one circuit induces a voltage in the other, F4, section 15) with coupling constant \(k_c\) (dimensionless, typically a fraction of a per cent to a few per cent). For two end cells with field amplitudes \(X_1\) and \(X_2\), a normal mode at frequency \(\Omega\) must satisfy

\[ X_1\left(1 - \frac{\omega_0^2}{\Omega^2}\right) + k_cX_2 = 0, \qquad X_2\left(1 - \frac{\omega_0^2}{\Omega^2}\right) + k_cX_1 = 0. \]

Adding and subtracting, the solutions are \(X_2 = X_1\) with \(1 - \omega_0^2/\Omega^2 = -k_c\), and \(X_2 = -X_1\) with \(1 - \omega_0^2/\Omega^2 = +k_c\):

\[ \Omega_0 = \frac{\omega_0}{\sqrt{1 + k_c}}\quad\text{(0-mode)}, \qquad \Omega_\pi = \frac{\omega_0}{\sqrt{1 - k_c}}\quad\text{(π-mode)}. \]

For small \(k_c\) they differ by \(\Omega_\pi - \Omega_0 \approx k_c\,\omega_0\).

Why the π-mode. In the π-mode the fields in neighbouring cells point opposite ways at every instant. An electron that crosses one cell in half an RF period arrives in the next just as its field has reversed, and is accelerated again. At \(\beta \approx 1\) and 2856 MHz, half a period of travel is \(\lambda/2 = 52.5\) mm, the length of a full cell. In the 0-mode the electron would be decelerated in alternate cells.

Why the separation matters. The RF source drives the π-mode, but a cavity responds to frequencies within about \(f/Q_0\) of each resonance, and an RF pulse has a spread of frequencies of its own. If the 0-mode is close, the drive excites some of it, and its field adds to one cell and subtracts from the other: the balance between cathode cell and full cell, which sets the beam’s energy and phase, is spoiled. The chapter’s earlier guns had 3.2 MHz between the modes, which means \(k_c = 3.2/2856 = 0.11\) per cent; the LCLS gun’s 15 MHz means \(k_c = 0.53\) per cent, a larger iris coupling. The driven-oscillator pre-requisite puts a number on how much of the unwanted mode a detuned drive excites.

RF guns and photoinjectors

Space charge does most harm where the beam is slow. Between two electrons moving together at speed \(\beta c\) the electric repulsion is partly cancelled by their magnetic attraction, and the net transverse force falls as \(1/\gamma^2\) (chapter 22). So the cure is to accelerate as fast as possible. A DC gun is limited by breakdown to fields of order 10 MV/m (Sinclair and Miller ran 8 MV/m at their cathode). An RF cavity holds far more: the field reverses every fraction of a nanosecond and is on only for pulses of microseconds, and the surfaces are clean copper (chapter 4, chapter 13).

The RF photoinjector puts a photocathode on the end wall of a resonant cavity, at the point of highest field. Fraser, Sheffield, Gray and Rodenz proposed it at Los Alamos in 1985: a photoemitter at 200 A/cm² on the end wall of the first cell of a 1300 MHz linac, a frequency-doubled mode-locked Nd:YAG laser at 532 nm (mode-locked: the laser emits a steady train of short pulses) chopping the electron bunches directly, cells shaped for least emittance growth rather than best shunt impedance (the accelerating voltage squared per watt lost in the walls, chapter 13), 2 to 3 MeV in the first two cavities and 10 MeV at the end. The Los Alamos group went on to build such guns for its free-electron laser work.

The surface-charge limit. In an RF gun the bunch is shorter than the gap, so Child-Langmuir does not apply directly. A bunch of charge \(Q\) just leaving a disc of area \(A\) makes a sheet of charge whose own field, \(Q/(\varepsilon_0 A)\), opposes the applied field \(\mathcal{E}\). Emission stops when they are equal:

\[ Q_{max} \approx \varepsilon_0 \mathcal{E} A. \]

The LCLS gun has a peak field of 115 MV/m on the cathode; with the laser fired 30° after the zero crossing, the field at emission is about half, 55 to 58 MV/m. Over the 1.3 mm spot that gives \(Q_{max} = 0.65\) nC; the 2.0 mm spot of the design allows 1.7 nC at 60 MV/m. The estimate assumes the whole bunch is emitted before its head has moved a distance comparable with the spot radius. The LCLS bunch lasted about 10 ps, and in 10 ps at 55 MV/m an electron starting from rest moves 0.47 mm, close to the 0.65 mm radius, so the sheet picture is only rough. The commissioning team did draw 1 nC from the 1.3 mm spot, but the bunch came out longer than designed (10 to 11 ps against 9 ps), which they put down to the small spot. The bigger spot costs thermal emittance in proportion to its radius. This trade, field against spot size against charge, is a central design choice of every photoinjector.

Emittance compensation. Space charge in a bunch depends on the local current, which varies along the bunch: high in the middle, low at the ends. Each thin slice is pushed outward at its own rate, so the slices’ ellipses in \((x, x')\) fan out, and the projected emittance grows even though each slice’s emittance is small. Bruce Carlsten at Los Alamos showed in 1989 that a solenoid placed just after the gun can undo most of this. A simple model shows why. Consider the rms size \(\sigma_r\) (m) of one slice in a uniform focusing channel of wavenumber \(k_f\) (m⁻¹; not the quadrupole strength \(k\) of the other chapters), with a space-charge term \(K_s\) (dimensionless, proportional to the slice current; not the gun perveance \(K\) above), and emittance negligible (the laminar regime, in which neighbouring trajectories do not cross, like the layers of a smooth flow). Primes are derivatives with respect to \(s\):

\[ \sigma_r'' + k_f^2\sigma_r - \frac{K_s}{\sigma_r} = 0. \]

The equilibrium is \(\sigma_{eq} = \sqrt{K_s}/k_f\); it depends on the slice current. Write \(\sigma_r = \sigma_{eq} + \Delta\sigma\) and linearise, using \(K_s/\sigma_r \approx K_s/\sigma_{eq} - (K_s/\sigma_{eq}^2)\,\Delta\sigma\):

\[ \Delta\sigma'' + k_f^2\Delta\sigma + \frac{K_s}{\sigma_{eq}^2}\Delta\sigma = \Delta\sigma'' + 2k_f^2\Delta\sigma = 0. \]

Every slice oscillates about its own equilibrium at the same wavenumber \(\sqrt{2}\,k_f\), whatever its current. Slices that start together at the cathode therefore return into line after each half period of this “plasma” oscillation, and the projected emittance shrinks again. The art is to arrange the solenoid and the following linac so that the realignment happens where the beam becomes relativistic and space charge stops mattering. L. Serafini and J. B. Rosenzweig gave the full theory in 1997 (the “invariant envelope”). The model ignores emittance pressure, energy spread within the bunch and nonlinear space charge; in a real gun these leave a residue, and codes that track particles with space charge are used for the design.

The S-band gun. A widely copied design is the 1.6-cell gun at 2856 MHz developed by BNL, SLAC and UCLA. The LCLS gun is a version of it: the full cell fed from two sides to remove dipole fields, shaped as a racetrack to cancel the quadrupole fields the double feed brings, and the separation between the wanted π-mode and the unwanted 0-mode raised from the 3.2 MHz of earlier guns to 15 MHz. Electrons leave it at about 6 MeV, where \(\gamma = 12.7\).

Numbers. The LCLS injector produced first electrons on 5 April 2007 and 250 MeV beam in the SLAC linac on 14 April. It met its design: 1 nC per bunch, a projected normalised emittance of 1.2 µm and a slice emittance of 0.9 µm at 135 MeV, an initial peak current of 95 A. That is a brightness of \(95/(1.2\times10^{-6})^2 = 6.6\times10^{13}\) A m⁻² rad⁻², ten times the thermionic limit computed above for 10 A/cm², with a pulse whose current density on the cathode, \(95\ \mathrm{A}/\pi(0.65\ \mathrm{mm})^2\), is about 7 kA/cm².

Pre-requisite: Waves in plasmas

Pre-requisite. This section derives how an electromagnetic wave travels through a plasma: the dispersion relation \(\omega^2 = \omega_p^2 + k^2c^2\), the cutoff below the plasma frequency, where the wave is reflected and decays within the plasma, and the critical density that a microwave of given frequency cannot exceed. The plasma section uses it for the density ceiling of microwave-driven sources, and the ECR section for its scaling with frequency. Taught from Tong, Electromagnetism, sections 8.6.1 to 8.6.3 (the Drude model, waves in conductors and plasma oscillations), and Brown, sections 2.3.3, 2.4.2 and 11.4.4.

The setting. A microwave enters a plasma of electron density \(n\) (m⁻³). Its electric field shakes the electrons; the moving electrons are a current; the current changes the wave. The ions, thousands of times heavier, barely move at microwave frequencies and are left out. Collisions are left out too, and there is no static magnetic field (a magnetised plasma supports more kinds of wave, including the one that resonates at the cyclotron frequency; this section gives the basic one).

The electrons’ response. Let the field at a point be \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}e^{-i\omega t}\), using the complex notation in which the physical field is the real part (F5, section 3). An electron obeys \(m_e\,d\mathbf{v}/dt = -e\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\). With \(\mathbf{v}\) also proportional to \(e^{-i\omega t}\), \(d/dt\) becomes \(-i\omega\):

\[ -i\omega m_e\mathbf{v} = -e\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mathbf{v} = \frac{e\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}{i\omega m_e}. \]

The current density is \(\mathbf{J} = -ne\mathbf{v} = i\dfrac{ne^2}{\omega m_e}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\). The factor \(i\) means the current lags the field by a quarter period: the electrons store and return energy but absorb none, like an inductance.

Maxwell’s equations. Faraday’s and Ampère-Maxwell’s laws (F4, section 16; the curl is F2, section 11) are

\[ \nabla\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = -\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}, \qquad \nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{J} + \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}{\partial t}. \]

Look for a transverse plane wave, all fields proportional to \(e^{i(kz - \omega t)}\), with \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) perpendicular to \(z\). Then \(\nabla\times\) becomes \(ik\hat{\mathbf z}\times\) and \(\partial/\partial t\) becomes \(-i\omega\). Faraday gives \(\mathbf{B} = (k/\omega)\hat{\mathbf z}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\). Put that into Ampère-Maxwell; since \(\hat{\mathbf z}\times(\hat{\mathbf z}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}) = -\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\) for a transverse field,

\[ -\frac{ik^2}{\omega}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} = i\mu_0\frac{ne^2}{\omega m_e}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}} - \frac{i\omega}{c^2}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}. \]

Multiply by \(i\omega c^2\) and use \(\mu_0 c^2 = 1/\varepsilon_0\):

\[ k^2c^2 = \omega^2 - \frac{ne^2}{\varepsilon_0 m_e} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \omega^2 = \omega_p^2 + k^2c^2, \]

with \(\omega_p\) the plasma frequency of the oscillator pre-requisite. In empty space (\(n = 0\)) this is \(\omega = kc\), light. In a plasma every wave has \(\omega > \omega_p\) if it is to travel at all.

Cutoff. If \(\omega < \omega_p\), then \(k^2 < 0\) and \(k = i\kappa\) is imaginary, with \(\kappa = \sqrt{\omega_p^2 - \omega^2}/c\). The wave \(e^{ikz} = e^{-\kappa z}\) does not travel; it decays into the plasma over a distance \(1/\kappa\), and the power is reflected. The electrons, able to follow the slow field, screen it out. So a plasma passes waves above its plasma frequency and reflects those below, a high-pass filter (Brown, section 11.4.4). The ionosphere reflects long radio waves for the same reason.

Figure 7.6. Frequency against wavenumber for a wave in a plasma, \(\omega^2 = \omega_p^2 + k^2c^2\) (red), in units of the plasma frequency. At high frequency it approaches light in vacuum (dashed). Below \(\omega_p\) no real \(k\) exists: the wave decays as \(e^{-\kappa z}\), with the decay constant \(\kappa\) drawn to the left (blue), largest at low frequency.

The critical density. Turned round: a wave of frequency \(\omega\) can enter a plasma only while \(\omega_p < \omega\), that is while

\[ n < n_c = \frac{\varepsilon_0 m_e\omega^2}{e^2}. \]

Microwave frequency \(n_c\) (m⁻³)
2.45 GHz \(7.4\times10^{16}\)
6.4 GHz \(5.1\times10^{17}\)
14.5 GHz \(2.6\times10^{18}\)
18 GHz \(4.0\times10^{18}\)
28 GHz \(9.7\times10^{18}\)

Brown’s tables give the same in cm⁻³ (\(7.6\times10^{10}\) cm⁻³ at 2.45 GHz with rounded constants). The critical density rises as \(f^2\), the reason high-charge-state sources moved to higher frequencies. Sources can run “overdense”, above \(n_c\), through mode conversion and the magnetised-plasma waves left out here, but the hot-electron density that strips ions stays near or below it.

Speeds. Recall the two speeds of a wave from F5, section 12: the crests move at the phase velocity \(\omega/k\), the energy and any signal at the group velocity \(d\omega/dk\). Above cutoff the phase velocity \(\omega/k\) exceeds \(c\) and the group velocity \(d\omega/dk = c^2k/\omega\) is below it; their product is \(c^2\). At \(n = n_c/2\) for a 2.45 GHz wave, \(v_{ph} = 1.41c\) and \(v_g = 0.71c\). Energy, carried at the group velocity, slows to a stop as the wave approaches the critical layer, where it turns back.

Exercise X1. A 2.45 GHz wave meets a plasma of \(10^{18}\) m⁻³. How far does it penetrate?

Answer

\(\omega_p/2\pi = 8.98\) GHz, well above 2.45 GHz. \(\kappa = \sqrt{\omega_p^2 - \omega^2}/c = 2\pi\sqrt{8.98^2 - 2.45^2}\times10^9/c = 181\) m⁻¹, so \(1/\kappa = 5.5\) mm. The microwave heats only a thin skin of such a plasma.

Exercise X2. Why can the ions be left out? Estimate their correction for protons.

Answer

The ions’ contribution has the same form with \(m_e\) replaced by the ion mass: \(\omega_{pi}^2 = \omega_p^2(m_e/M)\). The full relation is \(\omega^2 = \omega_p^2(1 + m_e/M) + k^2c^2\). For protons \(m_e/M = 5.4\times10^{-4}\), a correction of 0.05 per cent to \(\omega_p^2\).

Exercise X3. An ECR source doubles its frequency from 14 to 28 GHz. By how much can its plasma density rise, and by how much its resonant field?

Answer

\(n_c \propto f^2\), so four times. \(B_{ECR} \propto f\), so twice, from 0.50 to 1.00 T. Higher frequency buys density at the price of stronger magnets.

Pre-requisite: Sheaths and the Bohm criterion

Pre-requisite. This section explains where the ions entering a sheath get their speed. The plasma section that follows shows that they must reach the ion sound speed \(c_s = \sqrt{k_B T_e/M}\) (the Bohm criterion); here we find that a weak presheath gives it to them, across a potential drop of \(k_B T_e/2e\), and that the density at the sheath edge is therefore \(n e^{-1/2} = 0.61n\). It ends with the potential a floating wall takes up. Taught from Brown, sections 2.3, 7.1 and 8.4.1, with the Boltzmann factor of chapter 2.

The picture. A plasma of density \(n\) (m⁻³) has hot electrons, \(k_B T_e\) of a few eV, and cold ions, \(k_B T_i\) well below that. Near a wall or an extraction hole the potential falls in two stages. Next to the wall is the sheath, a few Debye lengths thick (the next section defines the Debye length), where the plasma is not neutral and the potential drops steeply. Before it lies the presheath, much thicker, of the order of the plasma size or the ions’ mean free path, where the plasma stays nearly neutral and the potential falls only gently. The two stages do different jobs.

Two rules for two species. Electrons are light and fast; in a potential \(\varphi\) (V, measured from the plasma’s bulk, negative towards the wall) they settle into the Boltzmann distribution, their density reduced wherever they would have to climb:

\[ n_e = n\exp\!\left(\frac{e\varphi}{k_B T_e}\right). \]

This is the Boltzmann factor \(e^{-E/k_BT}\) (F6, section 13) with \(E = -e\varphi\) the electron’s potential energy. Ions are heavy and, being cold, are not in such a balance: they fall towards the wall, and their energy is conserved,

\[ \tfrac12 M u^2 = \tfrac12 M u_{bulk}^2 - e\varphi, \]

with \(u\) (m/s) their speed and \(M\) (kg) their mass.

The presheath. The Bohm criterion says that ions must arrive at the sheath edge with speed at least \(c_s = \sqrt{k_B T_e/M}\). In the bulk they are nearly at rest. So something must accelerate them before the sheath: a small field across the presheath. Starting from rest, an ion reaches \(c_s\) after falling through a potential \(\Delta\varphi_{ps}\) with

\[ e\Delta\varphi_{ps} = \tfrac12 M c_s^2 = \tfrac12 k_B T_e \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta\varphi_{ps} = \frac{k_B T_e}{2e}. \]

The drop is half the electron temperature, in volts, whatever the ion.

The density at the sheath edge. In the presheath the plasma is still neutral, so the ion density equals the electron density, and the electrons follow Boltzmann. At the sheath edge, \(\varphi = -k_B T_e/2e\), so

\[ n_s = n\exp\!\left(-\tfrac12\right) = 0.607\,n. \]

The ions arriving at the sheath are therefore only 61 per cent as dense as in the bulk, moving at \(c_s\). Their flux, the most a wall or an extraction hole can take from the plasma, is

\[ J_{Bohm} = e n_s c_s = 0.61\,ne\sqrt{\frac{k_B T_e}{M}}. \]

Brown’s chapter 7 quotes the same relation in a slightly different form, \(0.4\,ne\sqrt{2k_BT_e/M_i}\), which is \(0.57\,ne\sqrt{k_BT_e/M_i}\); the small difference comes from how the presheath is modelled.

At work. For a hydrogen plasma at \(k_B T_e = 5\) eV and \(n = 10^{17}\) m⁻³: the presheath drops 2.5 V, protons arrive at \(c_s = 21.9\) km/s at a density of \(6.1\times10^{16}\) m⁻³, and the current density is 21 mA/cm². For H₂⁺ it is 15 mA/cm², and for argon ions 3.4 mA/cm²: heavier ions come more slowly.

The floating wall. An insulated wall, or a probe drawing no current, charges until it takes equal fluxes of ions and electrons. The ion flux is \(n_s c_s\). Electrons crossing a plane in a Maxwellian gas arrive at a rate \(\tfrac14 n\bar v\) per unit area, with \(\bar v = \sqrt{8k_B T_e/\pi m_e}\) the mean speed (F6, section 14 and chapter 3 derive this rate for gas molecules hitting a wall); and only the fraction \(e^{-e\Delta V/k_BT_e}\) climbs the sheath drop \(\Delta V\). At the sheath edge the electron density is also \(n_s\), so equal fluxes need

\[ n_s c_s = \tfrac14 n_s\bar v\,e^{-e\Delta V/k_B T_e} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta V = \frac{k_B T_e}{e}\ln\frac{\bar v}{4c_s} = \frac{k_B T_e}{2e}\ln\frac{M}{2\pi m_e}. \]

For protons \(\ln(M/2\pi m_e) = 5.68\), so the sheath holds \(2.84\,k_B T_e/e\), and with the presheath the wall floats \(3.34\,k_B T_e/e\) below the plasma: 16.7 V at 5 eV. For argon the total is \(5.18\,k_B T_e/e\). Brown’s “three or four times the electron temperature” is this. An extraction electrode is not floating: it is driven far more negative (for positive ions) and the sheath becomes the extraction gap, as the next sections show; but the ion supply at its edge is still the Bohm flux.

Answer

Plasmas for ion sources

Almost every ion source makes its ions in a plasma: a gas partly ionised by electrons, overall neutral. Irving Langmuir named it a plasma in 1928. Four ideas are needed.

Quasi-neutrality and the Debye length. Place a test charge in a plasma with electron temperature \(T_e\). The electrons, being light and mobile, follow a Boltzmann distribution in the potential \(\varphi\): \(n_e = n\exp(e\varphi/k_B T_e)\). The ions, heavy, stay near their mean density \(n\). Poisson’s equation reads

\[ \nabla^2\varphi = -\frac{e(n - n_e)}{\varepsilon_0} \approx \frac{n e^2}{\varepsilon_0 k_B T_e}\,\varphi \]

after expanding the exponential for \(e\varphi \ll k_B T_e\) (\(e^u \approx 1 + u\), F1, section 8). Call the constant on the right \(1/\lambda_D^2\), so that \(\nabla^2\varphi = \varphi/\lambda_D^2\).

Around a point charge \(q\) the potential depends only on the distance \(r\). For such a function the Laplacian is

\[ \nabla^2\varphi = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{d\varphi}{dr}\right) = \frac{1}{r}\frac{d^2(r\varphi)}{dr^2}. \]

The first form is the divergence of the gradient (F2, sections 10 and 12) worked out on a thin spherical shell: the flux of \(\nabla\varphi\) out through a sphere of radius \(r\) is \(4\pi r^2\,d\varphi/dr\), and its change across the shell, divided by the shell’s volume \(4\pi r^2\,dr\), is the divergence. Differentiating out shows that both forms equal \(\varphi'' + 2\varphi'/r\). So, with \(u = r\varphi\), the equation becomes \(u'' = u/\lambda_D^2\), whose solutions are \(e^{-r/\lambda_D}\) and \(e^{+r/\lambda_D}\). The growing one is ruled out, since the potential must vanish far away. Close to the charge, well inside \(\lambda_D\), the potential must be Coulomb’s, \(q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)\). Therefore

\[ \varphi(r) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\,e^{-r/\lambda_D}, \]

the Coulomb potential multiplied by \(e^{-r/\lambda_D}\), with

\[ \lambda_D = \sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_0 k_B T_e}{n e^2}}. \]

Beyond a few Debye lengths a charge is screened. A gas is a plasma when its size is many Debye lengths and there are many electrons inside a Debye sphere. The same screening in a classical plasma appears in Landau and Lifshitz, Statistical Physics Part 1, §78. For an ion-source plasma with \(n = 10^{17}\) m⁻³ and \(k_B T_e = 5\) eV, \(\lambda_D = 53\) µm and a Debye sphere holds \(6\times10^4\) electrons; at \(10^{15}\) m⁻³, 0.53 mm. Faircloth gives 0.1 to 1 mm as the range in ion sources.

The plasma frequency. Displace all the electrons in a slab by \(\xi\). The uncovered ion charge makes a field \(n e\xi/\varepsilon_0\) that pulls them back: \(m_e\ddot\xi = -(n e^2/\varepsilon_0)\xi\). They oscillate at

\[ \omega_p = \sqrt{\frac{n e^2}{\varepsilon_0 m_e}} \]

(Tonks and Langmuir, 1929). At \(10^{17}\) m⁻³, \(f_p = \omega_p/2\pi = 2.84\) GHz. An electromagnetic wave below \(\omega_p\) cannot enter the plasma. So a microwave-driven source has a ceiling on its density, the critical density at which \(\omega_p\) equals the drive frequency:

\[ n_c = \frac{\varepsilon_0 m_e\,\omega_{RF}^2}{e^2}, \]

\(7.4\times10^{16}\) m⁻³ at 2.45 GHz and \(9.7\times10^{18}\) m⁻³ at 28 GHz.

The sheath. Where a plasma meets a wall, the electrons, faster than the ions by \(\sqrt{M/m_e}\) at equal temperatures and by more when \(T_e > T_i\), would reach it first and charge it negative. The wall settles at a potential below the plasma that repels most electrons, and a thin positive layer, the sheath, a few Debye lengths thick, holds the potential drop. Langmuir described sheaths in 1923. All of an ion source’s extraction happens across such a layer.

The Bohm criterion and the Bohm current. How fast do ions arrive at the sheath? Let singly charged ions enter it at speed \(u_0\) where the density is \(n_s\), and fall without collisions through a potential \(\varphi(x) < 0\) measured from the sheath edge. Energy conservation and the continuity of ion flux, \(n_i u = n_s u_0\), give the ion density; the electrons remain Boltzmann:

\[ n_i = n_s\left(1 + \frac{2e|\varphi|}{M u_0^2}\right)^{-1/2}, \qquad n_e = n_s \exp\!\left(-\frac{e|\varphi|}{k_B T_e}\right). \]

Both densities fall as \(|\varphi|\) grows: the ions because they speed up and thin out, the electrons because they are repelled. For the sheath to hold a net positive charge, \(n_i > n_e\) just inside it. Expand both for small \(|\varphi|\), using \((1 + u)^{-1/2} \approx 1 - u/2\) and \(e^{-u} \approx 1 - u\) (F1, section 8):

\[ n_i \approx n_s\left(1 - \frac{e|\varphi|}{M u_0^2}\right), \qquad n_e \approx n_s\left(1 - \frac{e|\varphi|}{k_B T_e}\right). \]

So the ions must thin out more slowly than the electrons:

\[ \frac{1}{M u_0^2} \le \frac{1}{k_B T_e} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u_0 \ge c_s = \sqrt{\frac{k_B T_e}{M}}. \]

Figure 7.7. The densities just inside a sheath, as the potential falls: electrons (blue) by the Boltzmann factor, ions by energy conservation and constant flux. Ions that enter at the ion sound speed \(c_s\) (red) thin out more slowly than the electrons, so the layer is positive, as a sheath must be. Ions entering at \(0.7\,c_s\) (dashed) thin out faster at first, the layer would be negative, and no steady sheath can form.

This is the Bohm criterion (David Bohm, 1949): ions must enter the sheath at least at the ion sound speed. They are accelerated to it in a weak “presheath” across which the potential falls by \(k_B T_e/2e\), so the density at the sheath edge is \(n_s = n\,e^{-1/2} = 0.61\,n\). The ion current density a plasma can give to any wall or extraction hole is therefore

\[ J_{Bohm} = 0.61\, n e \sqrt{\frac{k_B T_e}{M}}. \]

It depends on the electron temperature, not the ion temperature, and it is the plasma’s fixed supply: no extraction voltage can draw more ions from a given plasma. For protons from a plasma at \(k_B T_e = 5\) eV, \(c_s = 21.9\) km/s and \(n = 10^{17}\) m⁻³ gives 21 mA/cm². To reach 1 A/cm² needs \(4.7\times10^{18}\) m⁻³.

Across the sheath, ions flow space-charge limited, so its thickness \(s_{sh}\) follows from equating the Bohm current to the Child-Langmuir current at the wall potential \(V_w\): \(s_{sh} = (\sqrt{2}/3)\,\lambda_{D,s}\,(2eV_w/k_B T_e)^{3/4}\), where \(\lambda_{D,s}\) is the Debye length at the sheath-edge density \(n_s = 0.61\,n\), larger than the bulk value by \(1/\sqrt{0.61} = 1.28\). At \(10^{17}\) m⁻³ and 5 eV, a 60 V sheath (the cathode sheath of a Penning H⁻ source) is 0.35 mm thick. A 20 kV extraction voltage would need a “sheath” of 27 mm. That is the extraction gap.

Extraction: the meniscus and the gap

An ion source has a plasma chamber with a hole in its wall, the plasma electrode, and a few millimetres beyond it an extraction electrode at tens of kilovolts. The plasma bulges into the hole until its edge, the meniscus, sits where the plasma’s Bohm supply equals what the gap can carry:

\[ 0.61\, n e \sqrt{\frac{k_B T_e}{M}} = \frac{4\varepsilon_0}{9}\sqrt{\frac{2q}{M}}\,\frac{V^{3/2}}{d_{\mathrm{eff}}^2}. \]

Here \(d_{\mathrm{eff}}\) is the effective gap from the meniscus to the extraction electrode. The meniscus is a free boundary, and its shape is the beam’s first lens. If the plasma is dense compared with the gap’s capacity, the meniscus bulges outward, convex, and the beam diverges and strikes the extraction electrode. If the plasma is thin, the meniscus retreats into the chamber, concave, and focuses the beam; too thin and it over-focuses to a crossover and diverges again. Between them lies a matched condition, a slightly concave meniscus, at which the beam leaves nearly parallel and the emittance is least. Because the right-hand side scales as \(V^{3/2}\), matching is a condition on the perveance: for a fixed geometry and plasma, there is one best voltage. The practical consequence is in “At the bench”.

The extraction gap is limited by breakdown (chapter 4). At fixed field \(\mathcal{E} = V/d\), the Child-Langmuir current density is

\[ J_{CL} = \chi\, \mathcal{E}^{3/2} d^{-1/2}, \]

so a smaller gap at the same field gives more current, until the gap is so small that the aperture radius is comparable to it and aberrations grow. Real extraction systems use three or more electrodes: an extraction electrode, a suppressor biased a few kilovolts negative to keep downstream electrons out (for positive ions), and a ground electrode, sometimes followed by a further acceleration.

Numbers. The ISIS Penning H⁻ source in its 2013 operating state extracted 35 mA at 17 kV through a 0.6 × 10 mm slit: 0.58 A/cm². The planar Child-Langmuir gap for H⁻ at that current density and voltage is 4.5 mm, a mean field of 3.7 MV/m. The BNL magnetron gives 90 to 100 mA at 35 kV through a 2.8 mm hole: 1.6 A/cm², a planar gap of 4.7 mm and 7.5 MV/m. At fields of this size, with caesium on the electrodes, flashovers are part of normal running (see “At the bench”).

The thermal emittance of an ion beam follows the electron formula with the ion’s mass, \(\varepsilon_n = \sigma_x\sqrt{k_B T_i/Mc^2}\). For H⁻ at \(k_B T_i = 1\) eV it is 0.033 µm per mm, so a 2.8 mm aperture (\(\sigma_x = 0.7\) mm) would give 0.023 µm. The BNL magnetron’s measured 4-rms normalised emittance is 1.6 π mm mrad, an rms value of 0.4 µm, seventeen times larger. Real ion beams are dominated not by temperature but by the meniscus’s aberrations, plasma fluctuations, magnetic fields at the extraction, and space charge in the first centimetres.

Positive-ion sources

Low-voltage arc. A hot filament emits electrons, which an anode some tens of volts positive accelerates through low-pressure gas. Above the ionisation energy (F7, section 7), 13.6 eV for atomic hydrogen and about 15.4 eV for the molecule, they ionise. The filament, not the discharge, supplies the electrons, so the arc runs at low voltage. In hydrogen much of the ion current is molecular, H₂⁺ and H₃⁺; protons need a denser plasma that dissociates the molecules.

Capillary arc. Draw the arc along a narrow channel: the current density and plasma density rise, the proton fraction rises with them, and the narrow channel limits how much gas escapes. This is the principle of the cyclotron capillary sources of the late 1930s.

Penning (PIG) source. A cylindrical anode between two cathodes, in an axial magnetic field. Electrons from the cathodes spiral along the field and are reflected by the opposite cathode, oscillating many times before they reach the anode; each electron’s path, and so its chance of ionising, is multiplied. F. M. Penning built a pressure gauge on this geometry at Philips in Eindhoven in 1937; the “Penning ionisation gauge” gave the source its name, PIG. The cathodes can be cold, heated by the discharge. Penning sources give multiply charged heavy ions at modest currents and have served as cyclotron sources; at the LBL 88-Inch Cyclotron the first ECR source replaced a PIG in 1984. Variants of the geometry became the calutron source of wartime isotope separation and, after the war, the Nielsen and Bernas sources; the Bernas source, built in 1958 with a reflecting “anticathode” opposite its filament, is now the commonest source of ion implanters.

Magnetron. The magnetic field is now perpendicular to the electric field between a central cathode and a surrounding anode, about 1 mm apart, so electrons drift around the cathode in closed loops (\(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\times\mathbf{B}\) drift; Landau and Lifshitz, The Classical Theory of Fields, §22). In 0.1 T an electron gyrates at 2.8 GHz, and a 100 eV electron’s radius is 0.34 mm, smaller than the gap; a 1 eV proton’s is 1.4 mm. The field traps the electrons but hardly the ions, which is what an ion source wants.

Duoplasmatron. Manfred von Ardenne built it in 1956 as a powerful source of gas ions. A hot cathode drives an arc to an intermediate electrode, which constricts it; a magnetic field between the intermediate electrode and the anode squeezes the plasma further into a small, very dense region at the anode aperture. The two stages (“duo”) give a high current density and a high proton fraction. Duoplasmatrons have long served as proton sources for accelerators, and in the 1960s they were found to give negative and multiply charged ions as well.

RF sources. An antenna, inside the plasma or wrapped around a ceramic chamber, drives a current at 2 to 13.56 MHz; the changing magnetic field induces an electric field in the gas (F4, section 14) that heats the electrons. There is no filament to burn out. Multicusp magnets, rows of permanent magnets of alternating polarity around the chamber’s walls, make a field that is strong at the walls and weak inside, and keep the plasma off them.

Pre-requisite: Driven oscillator, resonance and quality factor

Pre-requisite. This section drives the oscillator of the earlier pre-requisite with a periodic force. It shows that a force gains energy for the oscillator only through its part in step with the velocity, that this happens every cycle only when the drive frequency equals the natural one (resonance), and how the quality factor sets the width of the resonance. The ECR section applies it to an electron gyrating in a magnetic field and pushed by a rotating microwave field. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §22 and §26, and Tong, Classical Mechanics, section 2.5.2.

Reminder: F3, section 10 and F5, section 5 drove a mass on a spring and found the resonance curve. This section recalls the result and applies it to the sources.

Work done by a periodic force. The power a force \(F\) delivers to a body moving at velocity \(v\) is \(Fv\). Over one cycle of a periodic force acting on an oscillating body, the average of \(Fv\) is what remains. If \(F\) and \(v\) oscillate at the same frequency, the average is \(\tfrac12 F_0v_0\cos\delta\), with \(\delta\) the phase difference between them: positive when they are in step, zero when they are a quarter period apart, negative when opposed. If they oscillate at different frequencies, the phase difference drifts, and over many cycles the average is zero. So a force feeds an oscillator steadily only if it has the oscillator’s frequency and is in step with its velocity. A child on a swing is pushed once a swing, as it moves away.

The driven, damped oscillator. Take the damped oscillator of the earlier pre-requisite and add a force \(F_0\cos\omega t\):

\[ \ddot x + 2\Gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2 x = \frac{F_0}{m}\cos\omega t. \]

After the start-up has decayed (in a time of order \(1/\Gamma\)), the body moves at the drive frequency: \(x = A\cos(\omega t - \delta)\). Putting this in (most easily as the real part of \(Ae^{-i(\omega t - \delta)}\), so each \(d/dt\) is \(-i\omega\)) gives

\[ A = \frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + 4\Gamma^2\omega^2}}, \qquad \tan\delta = \frac{2\Gamma\omega}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2}. \]

Far below resonance the body follows the force (\(\delta \approx 0\)); far above, it moves against it (\(\delta \approx \pi\)); at \(\omega = \omega_0\), \(\delta = \pi/2\): the displacement lags the force by a quarter period, so the velocity is exactly in step with the force, and the power absorbed is greatest. At resonance \(A = F_0/(2m\Gamma\omega_0)\), limited only by the damping.

Width and quality factor. Near resonance write \(\omega = \omega_0 + \Delta\omega\) with \(\Delta\omega \ll \omega_0\). Then \(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 \approx -2\omega_0\Delta\omega\), and the amplitude relative to its peak is

\[ \frac{A}{A_{peak}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (\Delta\omega/\Gamma)^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (2Q_0\Delta\omega/\omega_0)^2}}, \]

with \(Q_0 = \omega_0/2\Gamma\). The absorbed power, proportional to \(A^2\), falls to half at \(\Delta\omega = \pm\Gamma\), so the full width at half power is \(2\Gamma = \omega_0/Q_0\). A high \(Q_0\) means a narrow, tall resonance and a slow decay once the drive stops.

At work: a copper gun cell. With \(Q_0 = 1.4\times10^4\) at 2856 MHz, the half-power width is \(f_0/Q_0 = 0.20\) MHz, and a field left alone decays in amplitude by \(1/e\) in \(1/\Gamma = 2Q_0/\omega_0 = 1.56\) µs. Driven exactly at the π-mode, how strongly is the unwanted 0-mode excited? By the formula, a resonance detuned by 3.2 MHz responds with \(1/\sqrt{1 + (2\times14000\times3.2/2856)^2} = 3.2\) per cent of the amplitude it would have on resonance; detuned by 15 MHz, 0.7 per cent. (A pulsed drive with sharp edges contains a spread of frequencies, so the real numbers depend on the pulse; the trend does not.) That is the gain from raising the mode separation in the LCLS gun.

Exactly on resonance, with no damping. With \(\Gamma = 0\) and \(\omega = \omega_0\) the steady state does not exist: the amplitude grows without limit. Starting from rest, the solution is

\[ x = \frac{F_0}{2m\omega_0}\,t\sin\omega_0 t, \]

an oscillation whose amplitude grows linearly in time, and whose energy grows as \(t^2\) (Landau and Lifshitz, §22). Off resonance by \(\Delta\omega\), the force and the motion drift out of step after a time of order \(1/\Delta\omega\), and the amplitude beats up and down instead of growing.

At work: cyclotron resonance. An electron in a magnetic field \(B\) gyrates at \(\omega_{ce} = eB/m_e\): two perpendicular oscillations, a quarter period apart, so a circle. Add a microwave electric field of amplitude \(\mathcal{E}\) (V/m) that rotates in the plane at \(\omega\). If it rotates in the same sense as the electron and \(\omega = \omega_{ce}\), then in a frame turning with the electron the field stands still, and it pushes along the electron’s velocity on every part of every turn. The speed grows linearly, \(dv_\perp/dt = e\mathcal{E}/m_e\), and the energy as \(t^2\):

\[ W(t) = \frac{(e\mathcal{E}t)^2}{2m_e}. \]

With \(\mathcal{E} = 5\) kV/m, after 10 ns the electron has 220 eV, and a numerical integration of the full equations of motion in 87.5 mT at 2.45 GHz gives the same 220 eV. Reverse the sense of rotation of the field, and the same integration gives \(10^{-5}\) eV: the counter-rotating field is never in step and does nothing. Detune by 1 per cent instead, and the push drifts out of step after \(1/\Delta\omega = 6.5\) ns; the speed can never exceed \(2e\mathcal{E}/(m_e\Delta\omega)\), an energy of 370 eV here, before the electron starts giving energy back. In an ECR source the field varies in space, so an electron is in resonance only while it crosses the thin shell where \(B = B_{ECR}\), gaining a kick each time it passes; many passes on a magnetically confined orbit build keV energies.

Exercise F1. A resonator has \(f_0 = 1300\) MHz and \(Q_0 = 2\times10^4\). What is its half-power bandwidth, and how long does its stored energy take to fall by \(1/e\)?

Answer

\(f_0/Q_0 = 65\) kHz. The energy decays as \(e^{-2\Gamma t}\), so its time constant is \(1/(2\Gamma) = Q_0/\omega_0 = 2\times10^4/(2\pi\times1.3\times10^9) = 2.4\) µs.

Exercise F2. In the cyclotron-resonance example, how long does it take to reach 10 keV, if the electron stayed in resonance throughout? How many turns is that at 2.45 GHz?

Non-relativistically \(v = \sqrt{2W/m_e} = 5.93\times10^7\) m/s at 10 keV (the exact relativistic value, \(5.85\times10^7\) m/s, is close), and \(t = m_ev/(e\mathcal{E}) = 67\) ns, about 165 turns. Exercise F3. Show that at \(\omega = \omega_0\) the velocity of the driven, damped oscillator is in phase with the force.

At resonance \(\delta = \pi/2\), so \(x = A\cos(\omega_0 t - \pi/2) = A\sin\omega_0 t\) and \(\dot x = A\omega_0\cos\omega_0 t\), which has the same phase as the force \(F_0\cos\omega_0 t\). The average power \(\langle F\dot x\rangle = \tfrac12F_0A\omega_0\) is the largest it can be for that amplitude.

Pre-requisite: Adiabatic invariants

Pre-requisite. This section shows that when an oscillation’s conditions change slowly, its energy changes but the ratio of energy to frequency does not: an adiabatic invariant. For a charged particle gyrating in a slowly varying magnetic field it gives the invariant magnetic moment \(\mu_m = p_\perp^2/(2mB)\), and from that the magnetic mirror, the mirror ratio, the loss cone and the fraction of particles it lets out. The ECR section uses all of them. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §49, and The Classical Theory of Fields, §21; Tong, Classical Mechanics, section 10.6; Wangler, section 5.12; and Brown, sections 2.4.3 and 11.4.3.

Slow change. Take an oscillator whose frequency \(\omega(t)\) is changed slowly: a pendulum whose string is drawn up, or a particle gyrating in a magnetic field that strengthens. “Slowly” means that \(\omega\) changes by only a small fraction during one period. The energy is not conserved, because the system is being worked on. But something else is.

The oscillator’s invariant. Per unit mass, the energy of the oscillator \(\ddot x + \omega^2x = 0\) is \(E = \tfrac12(\dot x^2 + \omega^2x^2)\). Differentiate, using the equation of motion:

\[ \frac{dE}{dt} = \dot x\ddot x + \omega^2x\dot x + \omega\dot\omega x^2 = \omega\dot\omega x^2. \]

Over one period \(x^2\) oscillates, but \(\omega\) and \(\dot\omega\) hardly change, so average over the period. For a harmonic oscillation half the energy is potential on average: \(\langle\tfrac12\omega^2x^2\rangle = \tfrac12E\), so \(\langle x^2\rangle = E/\omega^2\). Then

\[ \frac{dE}{dt} = \omega\dot\omega\frac{E}{\omega^2} = \frac{\dot\omega}{\omega}E \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{E}{\omega}\right) = 0. \]

\(E/\omega\) is constant: double the frequency slowly and the energy doubles. (In general, Landau and Lifshitz, §49, show that the action \(\oint p\,dq\) over one period is the invariant; for the oscillator it is \(2\pi E/\omega\).) A check: integrating \(\ddot x + \omega^2x = 0\) numerically while \(\omega\) rises from 1 to 2 rad/s over 200 s, about 50 oscillations, the energy doubles and \(E/\omega\) stays at 0.5000, changing by five parts in \(10^5\).

Gyration as an oscillator. A particle gyrating in a field \(B\) at \(\omega_c = qB/m\) (non-relativistic) is a two-dimensional oscillation, and its transverse kinetic energy \(W_\perp = p_\perp^2/2m\) plays the role of \(E\). If the field the particle sees changes slowly, either because the field itself changes or because the particle drifts along a field line into a stronger region, then \(W_\perp/\omega_c\) is invariant. Since \(\omega_c \propto B\), the invariant is

\[ \mu_m = \frac{p_\perp^2}{2mB} = \frac{W_\perp}{B}, \]

in J/T. It is the magnetic moment of the current loop that the gyrating charge makes. Equivalently, the magnetic flux through the orbit, \(\pi r_g^2B = \pi p_\perp^2/(q^2B)\), is constant: the orbit shrinks as the field grows (Landau and Lifshitz, Fields, §21). “Slowly” here means that the field changes little over one gyration: a 10 keV electron in 0.5 T has a gyroradius of 0.68 mm and turns in 73 ps, while the field of an ECR source changes over centimetres.

The magnetic mirror. Now let a particle move along a field line into a region where \(B\) rises. A static magnetic field does no work, so the total kinetic energy, and \(p^2 = p_\perp^2 + p_\parallel^2\), stay fixed. But \(p_\perp^2 = 2m\mu_mB\) must grow with \(B\). So \(p_\parallel^2 = p^2 - 2m\mu_mB\) shrinks, and where it reaches zero the particle stops moving along the line and is turned back. A rising field reflects particles: it is a magnetic mirror. The push that turns them is \(-\mu_m\,\partial B/\partial s\) along the line.

Let the particle cross the weakest point of the field, \(B_{min}\), at pitch angle \(\alpha\) to the field line, so \(p_\perp = p\sin\alpha\) there. It turns back where \(p_\perp = p\), that is where

\[ B = B_{refl} = \frac{B_{min}}{\sin^2\alpha}. \]

If the strongest field along its path, \(B_{max}\), is below \(B_{refl}\), it escapes.

The loss cone. With mirror ratio \(R_m = B_{max}/B_{min}\), a particle is held only if \(\sin^2\alpha > 1/R_m\). Particles with

\[ \alpha < \alpha_{lc} = \arcsin\frac{1}{\sqrt{R_m}} \]

leave through the mirror. In velocity space they fill a cone of half-angle \(\alpha_{lc}\) about the field direction, and another about the opposite direction (Brown, equation 11.4).

The fraction lost. If the directions are spread evenly over a sphere (an isotropic distribution), the fraction in a cone of half-angle \(\alpha\) is the area the cone cuts from a sphere of unit radius, its solid angle, over the whole sphere’s \(4\pi\). On the unit sphere the band between polar angles \(\theta\) and \(\theta + d\theta\) has area \(2\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta\) (its circumference \(2\pi\sin\theta\) times its width \(d\theta\), F2, section 13), so the cone holds \(\int_0^\alpha 2\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta = 2\pi(1 - \cos\alpha)\), and the fraction is \(2\pi(1 - \cos\alpha)/4\pi = \tfrac12(1 - \cos\alpha)\). Two cones, one at each end, hold

\[ f_{lost} = 1 - \cos\alpha_{lc}. \]

Exercise J3. At the centre of a source (\(B = 0.5\) T) an electron has 1 keV of motion across the field and 3 keV along it. Does it pass a mirror of 1.5 T? What are its transverse energy and gyroradius where \(B = 1.5\) T?

Pre-requisite: Two-dimensional magnetic potentials and multipole expansion

Pre-requisite. This section shows that a magnetic field which does not vary along one direction, in a region with no currents, is an analytic function of \(x + iy\), and so can be written as a sum of multipoles: dipole, quadrupole, sextupole (also called hexapole) and higher. For the sextupole the field’s magnitude grows as \(r^2\) towards the poles. Combined with a solenoid’s mirror field, this makes the minimum-B field of an ECR source, and the same sextupole sorts atoms by spin in a polarised source. Taught from Lee, Accelerator Physics, chapter 2, section I.2 (the multipole expansion, his equation 2.19), Brown, section 11.4.3, and the complex functions above.

The field as an analytic function. Let the field \((B_x, B_y)\) (T) lie in the \(x\), \(y\) plane and not depend on the third coordinate, as in the middle of a long magnet. Where there is no current, Maxwell’s equations (\(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0\) from F4, section 13, and Ampère’s law with no current, \(\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = 0\), from F4, section 12) require

\[ \frac{\partial B_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial B_y}{\partial y} = 0 \quad(\text{no magnetic charge}), \qquad \frac{\partial B_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial B_x}{\partial y} = 0 \quad(\text{no current}). \]

Form the complex combination \(F = B_y + iB_x\), as a function of \(w = x + iy\), and compare with the Cauchy-Riemann equations for \(u = B_y\), \(v = B_x\): they ask \(\partial u/\partial x = \partial v/\partial y\), which is the second equation, and \(\partial u/\partial y = -\partial v/\partial x\), which is the first. So \(B_y + iB_x\) is an analytic function of \(x + iy\) wherever there are no currents. An analytic function can be expanded in powers about the axis:

\[ B_y + iB_x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} C_n\,(x + iy)^n, \]

with complex constants \(C_n\) fixed by the magnet. Lee writes the same expansion with normalised coefficients \(b_n + ia_n\); the real parts are the “normal” multipoles, the imaginary parts the “skew” ones, rotated.

The multipoles. Each term is a magnet in its own right:

  • \(n = 0\), dipole: \(B_y + iB_x = C_0\), a uniform field.
  • \(n = 1\), quadrupole: \(B_y + iB_x = C_1(x + iy)\); for real \(C_1\), \(B_y = C_1x\) and \(B_x = C_1y\). The field grows linearly from the axis.
  • \(n = 2\), sextupole: \(B_y + iB_x = C_2(x + iy)^2\); for real \(C_2\), \(B_y = C_2(x^2 - y^2)\) and \(B_x = 2C_2xy\).

The magnitude of each term is \(|C_n|\,|w|^n = |C_n|r^n\), the same at every angle around the axis. A \(2(n+1)\)-pole magnet has \(2(n+1)\) poles alternating north and south, and a field magnitude growing as \(r^n\), zero on the axis for \(n \ge 1\). If the field at the pole radius \(r_p\) is \(B_p\), then \(|C_n| = B_p/r_p^n\):

\[ |B_{\text{sextupole}}| = B_p\left(\frac{r}{r_p}\right)^2. \]

At half the pole radius a sextupole gives a quarter of its pole field: 0.3 T for a 1.2 T magnet. The sextupole is weak near the axis and strong at the wall, which is what both of its uses in this chapter want.

Minimum-B. A plasma held by a magnetic field is stable only if the field strength rises in every direction away from the plasma; otherwise the plasma slips out where the field is weakest (Brown, section 11.4.3, calls the configuration stable against magnetohydrodynamic instabilities). Two solenoids, one at each end of the chamber, make a field along the axis that is strongest at the ends, \(B_{max}\), and weakest at the centre, \(B_{min}\): a magnetic bottle in the axial direction, with the mirror ratio of the adiabatic invariants. Across the axis, though, the solenoid field does not rise much. A sextupole around the chamber adds a transverse field that is zero on the axis and grows as \(r^2\). Near the centre the two fields are roughly at right angles, so the magnitudes add as

\[ |B| \approx \sqrt{B_z^2 + B_p^2(r/r_p)^4}, \]

and \(|B|\) now rises along the axis because of the solenoids and outward because of the sextupole: a minimum in every direction. The surface where \(|B| = B_{ECR}\) is then closed around the centre, the “egg shell” of the ECR section.

At work. Take an illustrative 28 GHz source (\(B_{ECR} = 1.00\) T) with 0.5 T at the centre and a sextupole giving 2.2 T at a chamber wall of 75 mm radius. In the mid-plane the resonance surface lies where \(B_p^2(r/r_p)^4 = B_{ECR}^2 - B_z^2\), at \(r = r_p\,[\sqrt{1.00^2 - 0.5^2}/2.2]^{1/2} = 47\) mm, well inside the wall, as Brown says a high-performance source requires.

And the polarised source. The same \(r^2\) law, acting on an atom’s magnetic moment rather than on a plasma, gives the harmonic restoring force of the spin pre-requisite. A quadrupole would not do: its \(|B| \propto r\) gives a force of constant size, pointing to the axis, not one growing with offset, so atoms would not be focused to a point.

Exercise M1. Show that the magnitude of a quadrupole field is the same at every angle, and find \(|B|\) at 10 mm from the axis of a quadrupole with 0.8 T at its 40 mm pole radius.

Answer

\(B_y + iB_x = C_1(x + iy)\), so \(|B| = |C_1|\,|x + iy| = |C_1|r\), independent of angle. With \(|C_1| = 0.8/0.04 = 20\) T/m, \(|B| = 0.2\) T at 10 mm.

Exercise M2. For a 2.45 GHz source with 60 mT at the centre and a sextupole of 0.3 T at 40 mm, where does the resonance surface cross the mid-plane?

Answer

\(B_{ECR} = 87.5\) mT. \(r = 40\ \mathrm{mm}\times[\sqrt{0.0875^2 - 0.060^2}/0.3]^{1/2} = 40\times(0.0637/0.3)^{1/2} = 18\) mm.

Exercise M3. Write \(B_x\) and \(B_y\) for an octupole (\(n = 3\)) with real \(C_3\), and say how its field grows.

Answer

\((x + iy)^3 = x^3 - 3xy^2 + i(3x^2y - y^3)\), so \(B_y = C_3(x^3 - 3xy^2)\) and \(B_x = C_3(3x^2y - y^3)\). The magnitude is \(C_3r^3\): flatter still near the axis, steeper at the wall. The sextupole is the usual choice for ECR sources (Brown, section 11.4.3): it leaves the centre to the solenoids while still rising steeply towards the wall.

ECR sources

An electron in a magnetic field \(B\) gyrates at

\[ \omega_{ce} = \frac{eB}{\gamma m_e} \]

(Landau and Lifshitz, The Classical Theory of Fields, §21). A microwave field whose rotating component turns with the electron at the same frequency stays in phase with it and does work on it every turn, the way a pushed swing gains energy. So the condition for electron cyclotron resonance is \(\omega_{RF} = \omega_{ce}\), and for slow electrons (\(\gamma \approx 1\))

\[ B_{ECR} = \frac{2\pi f_{RF}\,m_e}{e}. \]

At the common magnetron frequency of 2.45 GHz (the frequency of microwave ovens, made cheaply by the magnetron valve, a microwave tube and a cousin in geometry of the magnetron ion source) this is 87.5 mT. At 6.4 GHz, 0.229 T; at 14.5 GHz, 0.518 T; at 28 GHz, 1.000 T. Because \(\omega_{ce}\) falls as \(1/\gamma\), the resonant field at a fixed frequency is \(\gamma B_{ECR}\): higher by 2 per cent for a 10 keV electron and by 20 per cent for a 100 keV electron, which spreads the resonance in a hot plasma.

An ECR ion source fills a chamber with a “minimum-B” field: two solenoids make a magnetic bottle along the axis, and a hexapole adds a radial field that grows towards the walls. The field is weakest at the centre and rises in every direction, so the surface where \(B = B_{ECR}\) is closed, like an egg shell around the centre. Electrons crossing it gain energy; the bottle traps them because the magnetic moment \(\mu_m = p_\perp^2/(2m_e B)\) (subscripted to keep it apart from the phase advance per cell \(\mu\)) is an adiabatic invariant, so an electron whose velocity makes too large an angle with the field is reflected where the field rises. With mirror ratio \(R_m = B_{max}/B_{min}\), electrons whose pitch angle lies within \(\arcsin(1/\sqrt{R_m})\) of the field escape; for \(R_m = 4\) that is 30°, and 13 per cent of an isotropic distribution. The hot, confined electrons, at keV energies, strip the ions one electron at a time while the ions, cold and held by the electrons’ space charge, wait. The charge state reached depends on the product of electron density and ion confinement time.

Two scalings follow from the physics derived above. The density cannot much exceed \(n_c \propto f_{RF}^2\), so higher frequency gives a denser plasma and more ion current. And the field must rise to several times \(B_{ECR}\) in all directions to hold the electrons, which at 28 GHz means superconducting coils. Richard Geller (1927 to 2007), at the French atomic energy commission (CEA), led the group that developed ECR ion sources for accelerators in the 1970s and 1980s.

Numbers. The ECR source of the 88-Inch Cyclotron at Berkeley, designed by Claude Lyneis and Yves Jongen, delivered its first beam through the cyclotron in January 1984; it heats with 6.4 GHz at 200 to 600 W and extracts at 10 kV. Its successor VENUS is designed for 28 GHz, with axial fields of 4 T at injection and 3 T at extraction and 2.2 T radially at the chamber wall: 4.0, 3.0 and 2.2 times \(B_{ECR}\) (Brown’s book gives the nominal radial design field as 2 T; in commissioning the sextupole reached 2.4 T). It was commissioned at 18 GHz in 2003, with 28 GHz to follow. It has given 0.25 mA of Bi²⁹⁺.

EBIS: highly charged ions by brute force

An electron beam ion source sends a dense electron beam, compressed by a strong solenoid, along the axis of a series of drift tubes. The beam’s negative space charge traps positive ions radially; voltages on the end tubes trap them axially. Inside the trap the ions are struck again and again, and each charge state \(q\) lasts a time

\[ \tau_q \approx \frac{e}{j_e\,\sigma_q} \]

before the next ionisation, where \(j_e\) is the electron current density (A/m²) and \(\sigma_q\) the cross-section (m²) for removing one more electron. Higher charge states need higher electron energy (above the ionisation energy of the innermost electron to be removed) and a larger product \(j_e\tau\). Then the end barrier is lowered and the ions leave in a pulse. The number of ions is limited by the number of electrons in the trap, since the ions’ charge neutralises the well. The EBIS was invented by Evgeny Donets at Dubna. Its laboratory cousin, the electron beam ion trap of M. Levine and R. Marrs at Livermore (late 1980s), has produced bare uranium, U⁹²⁺. Brookhaven’s EBIS is the pre-injector of RHIC; it gives every species from deuterons to uranium and can switch between them on a timescale of one second, and it was built to take over from the two Tandem Van de Graaffs that had been the workhorse injectors for forty years (chapter 6).

Negative-ion sources

An H⁻ ion is a proton with two electrons, the second bound by only 0.75 eV (the electron affinity of hydrogen). It is easily destroyed in a plasma by mutual neutralisation with a positive ion, by electron detachment and by associative detachment with atoms. Two ways make it in quantity.

Surface production. A hydrogen atom approaching a metal surface sees its affinity level shift downward as it nears; if the surface’s work function is low, an electron tunnels across onto the atom, and the atom leaves as H⁻. Caesium has the lowest work function of any element, 2.14 eV, and a partial layer of caesium on molybdenum does better still: 1.5 eV at about 0.6 of a monolayer, rising again towards 2 eV at a full monolayer. In the early 1960s Victor Krohn had noticed that bombarding metal with caesium ions multiplied the yield of sputtered negative ions. In the early 1970s at the Budker Institute of Nuclear Physics in Novosibirsk, Gennadii Dimov, Yuri Belchenko and Vadim Dudnikov added caesium vapour to a magnetron discharge. The H⁻ current rose dramatically and the co-extracted electron current fell; they extracted 880 mA in 1 ms pulses at a discharge current of 450 A. Dudnikov’s Penning-geometry version gave 150 mA of H⁻ with only 250 mA of electrons. These were orders of magnitude more than anything before. Such sources are called surface-plasma sources.

In the magnetron the cathode facing the extraction hole has an indent, a dimple for a round hole or a groove for a slit, that focuses the H⁻ ions produced on it towards the aperture. In the Penning source the cathodes are two parallel molybdenum jaws in a field normal to them, enclosing a discharge of about 10 × 5 × 5 mm; ribs on the aperture plate block the direct line of sight, so only H⁻ ions that have exchanged charge with slow atoms on the way out are extracted. That gives a beam with a smaller energy spread than the magnetron’s. The Penning is the brightest H⁻ source, above 1 A/cm² at extraction.

Volume production. In 1979 Marthe Bacal and G. W. Hamilton reported H⁻ and D⁻ produced in the body of a hydrogen plasma. The process has two steps: fast electrons excite H₂ molecules to high vibrational levels, then slow electrons, about 1 eV, attach to the excited molecules, which split into H⁻ and H. The two steps need different electrons. A magnetic filter field of about 10 mT across the chamber divides it into a hot region where the molecules are excited and a cold region near the aperture where H⁻ is formed and extracted. Volume sources avoid caesium; the most intense modern sources combine both processes, a volume plasma with a caesiated surface near the aperture.

Co-extracted electrons. A negative-ion source extracts electrons too, sometimes ten times the ion current. They must be bent out of the beam by a weak magnetic field and dumped at low energy: the SNS source dumps them at 6.2 keV before accelerating the H⁻ to 65 keV, J-PARC at 10 keV before 50 keV. A tandem accelerator (chapter 6) and a ring filled by charge exchange (chapter 21) depend on these sources.

Polarised ion sources

Polarised protons and deuterons are made from polarised atoms. In an atomic-beam source, hydrogen atoms from a dissociator pass through a sextupole magnet, which focuses atoms with electron spin in one direction and defocuses the others (a Stern-Gerlach separation); RF transitions then move the electron polarisation onto the nucleus through the hyperfine coupling, and the atoms are ionised. In an optically pumped source, a vapour of alkali atoms is polarised with circularly polarised laser light, and protons capture polarised electrons from it, after which the electron polarisation is transferred to the nucleus. The currents are far smaller than from unpolarised sources, and every collision with a wall or a stray field can depolarise the atoms.

The low-energy beam transport

Between the source and the first accelerating structure, usually an RFQ (chapter 14), lies the low-energy beam transport (LEBT), a metre or two of solenoids or electrostatic lenses at 30 to 70 keV. Here the beam is slow and its space charge strong. The potential at the centre of a uniform round beam of current \(I\), radius \(a\) and speed \(v\) in a pipe of radius \(b\), relative to the pipe, is

\[ \Delta\varphi = \frac{I}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 v}\left(1 + 2\ln\frac{b}{a}\right). \]

For a 60 mA H⁻ beam at 65 keV (\(v = 3.5\times10^6\) m/s, \(\beta = 0.012\)) of radius 5 mm in a 50 mm pipe this is 0.86 kV, and 0.15 kV between the beam’s axis and its edge. The radial field of that potential pushes the beam apart; the forces are those of chapter 22 at \(\gamma \approx 1\).

Residual gas helps. The beam ionises it. For a negative beam the positive ions so made are trapped in the beam’s potential well and the electrons expelled; for a positive beam it is the other way round. The trapped charge neutralises a large part of the beam’s space charge. The time to build up is about one ionisation time,

\[ \tau_N = \frac{1}{n_g\sigma_{ion} v}, \]

with \(n_g\) the gas density and \(\sigma_{ion}\) the ionisation cross-section. Cross-sections for making secondaries by collisions of beam ions with residual gas are typically of order \(10^{-20}\) m² (Brown, §6.3.1); with that value, at \(10^{-3}\) Pa (gas density \(n_g = p/k_BT = 2.5\times10^{17}\) m⁻³ at room temperature, F6, section 10) and the speed above, \(\tau_N \approx 115\) µs. That is comparable with source pulses of 250 µs to 1 ms, so the head of each pulse is less neutralised than the rest and transmits differently. Hence the gas pressure in the LEBT is itself a tuning knob, monitored at Linac4. Electrostatic lenses sweep out the neutralising ions, so electrostatic LEBTs carry the full space charge; magnetic ones keep the neutralisation.

How it was researched and built

Cathodes and guns: 1880 to 1960

Cathodes were developed for valves, radio, television and microwave tubes, not for accelerators: Langmuir’s thoriated tungsten (1923), oxide cathodes after Wehnelt (1904), dispenser cathodes after Philips (around 1950; Levi, 1953 and 1955). Accelerator builders took them from that industry.

Pierce’s paper of 1940 turned gun design from trial into calculation. Before it, gun electrodes were shaped largely by trial; after it, they could be derived from the flow. The method needed no computer for the planar case; round and converging beams needed numerical solutions. By 1981 Sinclair and Miller designed their 200 kV GaAs gun with W. B. Herrmannsfeldt’s SLAC Electron Trajectory Program (SLAC report 226), which solves Poisson’s equation with space charge together with the trajectories, iterating until the two agree; it gave them an emittance of \(1.0\times10^{-3}\ m_e c\) cm (they write \(m_0\) for the electron’s rest mass), about 10 µm normalised.

Ion sources: 1930 to 1960

The early ion sources were built and rebuilt by the accelerator groups themselves. The Penning geometry was tried as an ion source by Maxwell (1931), magnetron sources followed (Lamar and Luhr at MIT, 1933; Van Voorhis at Princeton, 1934), and cyclotron groups built arc and capillary sources (Livingston, Holloway and Baker, 1939). Each improvement in a source showed up directly as more current at the target. Within about a decade of the first magnetron and Penning sources it was possible to make multi-milliampere beams of positive ions of almost every element, whether solid, liquid or gas. The duoplasmatron (von Ardenne, 1956) was developed further by Demirkhanov, Fröhlich and Kistemaker from 1959 to 1965.

Caesium and the H⁻ source: the early 1970s to today

The Novosibirsk discovery travelled quickly once published (Belchenko, Dimov and Dudnikov, Nuclear Fusion, 1974). The developments that followed show how a source matures.

  • At Brookhaven, Krsto Prelec’s group improved the magnetron with the cathode indent and built a 2 A version for neutral-beam injectors for fusion.
  • In the late 1970s Chuck Schmidt at Fermilab built the accelerator version of the magnetron, mounted face down in a 90° bending magnet inside a refrigerated “cold box” that freezes out stray caesium, followed by a 750 kV Cockcroft-Walton column (chapter 5; since 2012 the source feeds an RFQ at 35 keV instead). It has run for over thirty years: in 2018 it gave over 80 mA at 0.3 per cent duty and 15 Hz, with a discharge of 15 A at 180 V and a lifetime of about nine months. DESY copied it for HERA, and Jim Alessi at Brookhaven refined it with a lower discharge current, higher extraction voltage and permanent magnets: 90 to 100 mA at 35 kV, discharge 8 to 18 A at 140 to 160 V, 0.5 mg of caesium per hour, a lifetime of 270 days.
  • At Los Alamos, Paul Allison and colleagues developed the Penning design. In the early 1980s Peter Gear and Roger Bennett at the Rutherford Appleton Laboratory combined it with parts of the Fermilab design (the 90° magnet, the cold box, rear loading) for the ISIS spallation source. In the 1990s Vernon Smith, Allison and Joe Sherman at Los Alamos built a scaled-up Penning giving 120 mA in 500 µs pulses at 60 Hz.
  • Around 1980 Ehlers and Leung at Berkeley built the surface-converter source, a caesiated curved surface in a multicusp plasma, biased negative so that the H⁻ made on it is driven and focused towards the exit. With a large molybdenum converter it gave more than 1 A of H⁻ continuously.
  • RF-driven sources came last. The SNS source at Oak Ridge has a 2.5-turn antenna inside the plasma, 50 to 60 kW pulsed at 2 MHz, caesium from caesium chromate cartridges, and over 60 mA at 6 per cent duty with a lifetime of 14 weeks. J-PARC’s gives 45 mA in user operation, 66 mA in 1 ms pulses at 25 Hz on its test stand. CERN’s Linac4 source is a copy of DESY’s caesium-free external-antenna source. It was meant to run without caesium; the required parameters could only be met by adding it. It needs two days to stabilise after installation, has run seven weeks without interruption, and delivers 45 mA at 0.8 Hz.

Lifetimes look very different until the duty factor is taken into account. Faircloth’s comparison: the DESY magnetron lasted 900 days at 0.047 per cent plasma duty, the Fermilab magnetron 200 days at 0.12, the BNL magnetron 270 days at 0.525, the ISIS Penning 25 days at 4 per cent. In days of plasma actually burning, all four lie between 0.24 and 1.42. The limit is the same in each: caesium ions accelerated by the cathode sheath sputter the molybdenum.

ECR and EBIS: the 1960s to today

ECR sources grew out of electron cyclotron heating of plasmas for controlled fusion, which Geller worked on in the 1960s. His group at the CEA developed them as accelerator sources through the 1970s and 1980s: first ECR-heated sources of multiply charged ions in 1972 (Wiesemann’s group in Germany reported one the same year), and in 1974 SUPERMAFIOS, a plasma-research mirror with a hexapole added, the first source with the minimum-B field and closed resonance surface of every modern ECR source. The history since is a climb in frequency and field, each “generation” bringing a higher critical density: 6.4 GHz for the first Berkeley source (1984), and 28 GHz with superconducting magnets for VENUS, which its laboratory calls a third-generation source.

Photoinjectors: 1976 to today

The photoinjector grew from two lines of work. One was polarised sources for SLAC’s physics programme: after Pierce and Meier (1976) came the parity-violation source (1978) and Sinclair and Miller’s high-current GaAs test gun (1981), which drew 60 A from a 6 mm diameter spot at 57 kV (they quote 180 A/cm²; 60 A over a uniform 6 mm disc is 210 A/cm²), with 80 kV/cm at the cathode and no sign of damage. They found that room-temperature cathodes were limited by slow loss of caesium, easily restored, while cold cathodes were spoiled by gas freezing onto them. The other was the free-electron laser: Fraser and colleagues’ proposal (1985), Carlsten’s compensation (1989), Serafini and Rosenzweig’s theory (1997), and the BNL/SLAC/UCLA gun. The LCLS injector, built on that gun, was commissioned in five months in 2007; the gun ran nearly continuously at 30 Hz for more than \(3\times10^8\) pulses between 5 April and 24 August.

The LCLS commissioning shows how a cathode behaves in practice. The copper’s quantum efficiency at the start was \(0.4\times10^{-5}\), fifteen times below the specification of \(6\times10^{-5}\), and it limited the charge to 300 pC for the first half of commissioning. Images of the cathode in white light and in its own photoemission matched: the emission was patchy. The group then scanned a small, intense laser spot (0.67 mm diameter, 10 s dwell, 100 µm steps) over a 2 × 2 mm area with the gun’s RF on, and the quantum efficiency rose from \(2.3\times10^{-5}\) to \(4.1\times10^{-5}\). The procedure is called laser cleaning.

The sources used for this chapter do not give the costs or staff numbers of these projects.

How a source is designed today

The physics is the same; the method is computation. For a gun or an extraction system, a code solves Poisson’s equation with the beam’s space charge and traces rays until the two agree; for a plasma source, the meniscus is found self-consistently from the Bohm condition at the plasma boundary. For photoinjectors, codes that track macro-particles with space charge through the gun, solenoid and first linac sections set the laser spot, the pulse length, the solenoid field and the phase. Common choices today:

  • Electron beams for klystrons and DC injectors: dispenser cathodes in Pierce-type guns, with grids or modulating anodes for pulsing.
  • Bright electron beams for FELs: copper or caesium telluride cathodes in normal-conducting RF guns, at over 100 MV/m in S-band (115 MV/m at the LCLS), with an emittance-compensating solenoid.
  • Polarised electrons: strained GaAs in DC guns at very high vacuum.
  • Protons: duoplasmatrons, 2.45 GHz ECR sources (more than 100 mA of H⁺ or D⁺ in DC operation) or RF sources.
  • H⁻: caesiated Penning, magnetron or RF sources; filament or RF volume sources where lower current suffices.
  • Highly charged heavy ions: ECR sources at up to 28 GHz, or EBIS for pulsed beams.

At the bench

Bringing up a thermionic gun

The heater is raised slowly, over minutes, because a cold cathode has low resistance and a sudden voltage overheats it. The filament current and voltage are read on meters at the heater supply, which floats at the cathode’s high voltage and is read through an isolation link. With the anode voltage fixed, the experimenter raises the heater and watches the beam current on a Faraday cup (an insulated metal cup that catches the beam, whose charge flows to a meter) or a current transformer (a ring of magnetic material around the beam, in which the beam’s own magnetic field induces a signal; chapter 8). At first the current rises steeply with temperature: the temperature-limited regime, 1 per cent per kelvin for tungsten. Then it bends over and stops rising: the space-charge-limited regime, where the current depends on \(V^{3/2}\) and no longer on temperature. A gun is run just past this knee, so that small drifts of temperature do not change the current, but no hotter, because evaporation of the cathode rises with temperature too and sets its life. The knee moves to higher temperature as the cathode ages and its work function rises. A cathode that needs more heater power each month to stay space-charge limited is near its end.

Vacuum is part of the gun. Oxide and dispenser cathodes are poisoned by water, oxygen and carbon compounds, and a burst of gas shows as a fall of emission that recovers slowly. A tungsten filament switched on at atmosphere burns through in seconds (chapter 3). Lanthanum hexaboride is more tolerant and lasts ten to fifteen times longer than tungsten, but coats the Wehnelt with evaporated material over time.

Running a photocathode gun

The quantum efficiency is measured by plotting the bunch charge against laser energy at low charge, where emission is linear; the slope is \(\eta_Q\). At higher laser energy the line bends over: the surface-charge limit. A camera at a “virtual cathode”, an optical plane equivalent to the cathode, shows the laser spot’s shape and position; the laser is locked to the RF phase to a fraction of a picosecond (under 0.2 ps rms at the LCLS). The gun’s RF phase is scanned while the charge is watched: no charge until the field at the cathode turns accelerating, a rise, a plateau. At the LCLS the working phase is 30° past the zero crossing. The cathode is imaged by its own photoemission with the solenoid, at about 10 pC to avoid space-charge blurring; dark spots are dead areas. GaAs cathodes need ultra-high vacuum, 2 to 3 × 10⁻¹¹ Torr in Sinclair and Miller’s guns, and are revived by fresh caesium. Their quantum efficiency decays with charge drawn, partly through ions from the residual gas accelerated back onto the cathode.

Lighting an ion source

The source sits on a high-voltage platform or deck at the extraction voltage, so all its supplies (arc, filament, magnet, gas valve, caesium oven heater) float with it and are controlled through insulating links, today usually optical fibres.

Gas. Hydrogen is admitted through a needle valve or, in pulsed sources, a piezoelectric valve that opens for each pulse. The ISIS Penning used 20 mL per minute in 2013, the BNL magnetron 3 mL per minute. Pressure in the discharge is below about 1 mbar (100 Pa), far higher than in the extraction region; the aperture is the conductance that keeps them apart, and the source’s gas load sets the pumping needed in the LEBT.

Arc. The arc supply is current-regulated. The discharge strikes at a few hundred volts and then runs at lower voltage: the ISIS Penning at 55 A and 60 to 80 V, the BNL magnetron at 8 to 18 A and 140 to 160 V, the Fermilab magnetron at 15 A and 180 V. A caesiated Penning source has two modes. Cold, it runs in a high-impedance mode at several hundred volts and low current, giving little beam. Once the electrodes are hot enough, a few hundred degrees Celsius, to hold the right fraction of a caesium monolayer, it jumps to the low-impedance mode at 60 to 70 V and high current. If conditions drift, it can slip back: the arc current falls, the electrodes cool, and the discharge goes out, and restarting takes high voltage and patience. Thermionic emission from caesium on molybdenum rises by thirteen orders of magnitude between 300 K and 600 K; that is why these sources will not run cold.

Colour. Through a viewport a hydrogen plasma glows pink-red, mostly from the Balmer-alpha line at 656 nm (and blue-green H-beta at 486 nm). A change of colour means a change of gas or an impurity. Caesium’s strongest lines are in the near infrared and are not seen by eye.

Caesium. Elemental caesium is kept in an oven, typically between 70 and 190 °C, whose temperature sets the vapour flow. It melts at about 28 °C and reacts violently with water and air, so ovens are filled and changed under inert gas. Consumption: 3 mg per hour at ISIS (2013), 0.5 mg per hour at BNL; Penning and magnetron sources can use more than 10 g over their life, while the SNS source, conditioned carefully once, uses only a few milligrams. Caesium that escapes promotes breakdown on high-voltage surfaces, hence the cold box at −40 to 0 °C after early sources, and LEBTs of 1 to 2 m before an RFQ. Tuning a surface-plasma source is slow: change one parameter at a time, in small steps, with hours or days between, until the electrode temperature and caesium coverage settle.

Scanning the extraction voltage

With the arc steady, the experimenter steps the extraction voltage while watching the current on a Faraday cup a short distance downstream. The cup has a suppressor ring biased a few hundred volts negative (for positive ions) to keep secondary electrons in, or else the reading is wrong by the secondary yield. For negative ions the co-extracted electrons must be removed before the cup, or the cup reads them; current transformers can read high or low depending on where stray electron currents flow (chapter 8).

The current first rises as \(V^{3/2}\), the gap limiting it. Then it levels off when the plasma’s Bohm supply becomes the limit. Along the way the transmission through the first aperture downstream, and the beam’s size on a screen, pass through a best point: the matched meniscus. Below it the beam over-focuses and the current lost on the extraction electrode rises; above it the meniscus bulges, the beam diverges and strikes the electrode. A standard plot is current and emittance against extraction voltage at fixed arc, or against arc current at fixed voltage, both showing a minimum emittance at the matched perveance.

Measuring emittance

Slit and screen, slit and collector. A narrow slit in a plate passes a thin ribbon of the beam, selecting one value of \(x\). A screen or a wire array a known distance \(L\) downstream shows how that ribbon has spread: its profile is the distribution of \(x'\) at that \(x\). Stepping the slit across the beam fills in the whole \((x, x')\) map, from which the code computes \(\varepsilon\) by the moments above. The slit must be narrow compared with the beam, thick enough to stop it, and cooled at high power.

The Allison scanner. P. W. Allison, J. D. Sherman and D. B. Holtkamp at Los Alamos described in 1983 an electric-sweep scanner for intense, low-energy H⁻ beams: a 10 cm analyser with a front slit, a pair of deflecting plates driven by a ±500 V ramp and a rear slit. Ions that pass the rear slit at a given moment entered at an angle proportional to the instantaneous plate voltage, so one ramp sweeps \(x'\) while a stepping motor moves the head across \(x\). It was built for ±0.5 mrad angular resolution. Scanners of this kind are widely used at ion-source test stands.

The pepper-pot. A plate with a grid of small holes in front of a scintillator or a phosphor screen measures both planes in one shot: each hole’s spot gives the angular spread at that position. It suits pulsed beams and fast changes, at the cost of resolution.

Readings are processed with care. A threshold that cuts off faint halo can change an emittance markedly, so the fraction of beam included must be quoted; Faircloth and Lawrie recommend the 4-rms value, which for almost all real beams is close to the emittance holding 90 per cent of the beam (Wangler, §9.4; for an exact two-dimensional Gaussian it is 86 per cent). Typical results: the ISIS source 0.35 µm rms normalised at 35 mA (2013) and 1.0 π mm mrad 4-rms at 55 mA (2018); J-PARC 0.9 π mm mrad and Linac4 1.2 π mm mrad (4-rms). For electron guns at relativistic energy, emittance is measured instead by varying a quadrupole and fitting the beam size on a screen (chapter 8, chapter 16).

Sparks and other failures

Sparking in the extraction gap. The gap runs at several MV/m, the source fills it with gas and caesium, and the beam’s edge strikes the electrodes. Sparks show as spikes on the extraction supply’s current meter, a missing pulse, a step in the beam current, and a burst on the vacuum gauge. A new or vented source is conditioned by raising the voltage slowly, letting each spark clean a surface. The extraction supply must survive repeated flashovers; caesium sources make them part of normal running.

Flakes. In magnetron and Penning sources, molybdenum sputtered from the cathode by caesium ions redeposits in the discharge chamber and flakes off. A flake bouncing in the discharge makes it look unstable; a flake partly covering the slit causes a step down in beam current, sometimes recovered by tuning; a flake across anode and cathode shorts the discharge, all beam is lost and the source cools. ISIS’s aperture plate shows visible erosion of the ribs after 26 days.

Ancillary failures. Most source stops are not the source: power supplies, temperature controllers, cooling, gas and caesium delivery, vacuum pumps, timing.

Typical timescales. Caesium conditioning and stabilisation: hours to two days (Linac4). Lifetime between changes: 25 days to nine months for H⁻ sources, depending on duty. A GaAs cathode in Sinclair and Miller’s guns: one to several days when cooled to 77 K, before heat cleaning and reactivation; at room temperature, after a few re-caesiations, several weeks with no measured loss of quantum efficiency.

Where it stops

For electrons the limit is set at the cathode and in the first few millimetres. Brightness is bounded by \(J/T_{\mathrm{eff}}\): for a photocathode \(\mathcal{B}_{max} = 12\pi J mc^2/(\hbar\omega - \phi_{\mathrm{eff}})\). The current density is bounded by the surface-charge limit \(\varepsilon_0 \mathcal{E}\) per unit area, and \(\mathcal{E}\) by breakdown and dark current in the gun, about 100 to 120 MV/m at S-band. The LCLS numbers show the result: of a 0.9 µm slice emittance, about a third is the cathode’s own, the rest space charge and RF effects that compensation does not fully remove. Lower emittance needs colder emission (cathodes run near threshold, or semiconductor cathodes cooled to cryogenic temperatures), higher fields, or smaller charge per bunch. Beyond the gun the beam belongs to the linac (chapter 14) and, for an FEL, to the compression and undulator (chapter 26).

For ions the limit is the extraction gap. At a breakdown field \(\mathcal{E}\), the Child-Langmuir current density is \(\chi \mathcal{E}^{3/2}d^{-1/2}\): for H⁻ at 7.5 MV/m across 4.7 mm, 1.6 A/cm², the BNL magnetron’s value. More current needs larger apertures, which bring larger emittance, or more apertures, which is what the large multi-aperture sources for fusion do. And after extraction the beam is slow, at \(\beta \approx 0.01\), its space charge only partly neutralised; it must be focused and bunched quickly, which is the job of the RFQ (chapter 14). For heavy ions the limit is the charge state: an ECR source or EBIS gives tens of charges on uranium, not ninety-two, and the rest are stripped off in foils at higher energy (chapter 6, chapter 21). How space charge limits the beam all the way round a ring is chapter 22. And none of these numbers, current, emittance, energy spread, is known until it is measured, on a beam that cannot itself be seen (chapter 8).

Exercises

1. An ECR source is to run at 14.5 GHz. What field does the resonance need, and what is the highest plasma density at which the microwaves still enter the plasma?

Answer

\(B_{ECR} = 2\pi f m_e/e = 2\pi\times14.5\times10^9\times9.109\times10^{-31}/1.602\times10^{-19} = 0.518\) T. The critical density is \(n_c = \varepsilon_0 m_e (2\pi f)^2/e^2 = 2.6\times10^{18}\) m⁻³. Both scale with frequency, \(B_{ECR}\) linearly and \(n_c\) as its square.

2. A dispenser cathode at 1160 K (Fraser’s “typical thermionic emitter”) emits uniformly from a disc of 5 mm radius at 10 A/cm². Find the beam current and its thermal normalised rms emittance.

Answer

\(I = 10\ \mathrm{A/cm^2}\times\pi(0.5\ \mathrm{cm})^2 = 7.9\) A. For a uniform disc \(\sigma_x = r_c/2 = 2.5\) mm. \(k_B T = 0.100\) eV, so \(\sqrt{k_BT/m_ec^2} = \sqrt{0.100/510\,999} = 4.42\times10^{-4}\) and \(\varepsilon_n = 2.5\ \mathrm{mm}\times4.42\times10^{-4} = 1.1\) µm. No later optics can make it smaller.

3. A tungsten cathode (\(\phi_w \approx 4.5\) eV, \(A_G = 0.6\times10^6\) A m⁻² K⁻²) runs at 2500 K, temperature limited. By what factor does the current fall if the cathode cools by 100 K, and what rise in temperature doubles it?

Answer

\(J(2500\ \mathrm{K}) = 0.32\) A/cm² and \(J(2400\ \mathrm{K}) = 0.12\) A/cm², a factor of 0.39. Solving \(J(T) = 2J(2500\ \mathrm{K})\) gives \(T = 2578\) K, a rise of 78 K. The logarithmic derivative is \(2/T + \phi_w/(k_BT^2) = 0.0092\) per kelvin, so about 1 per cent per kelvin, which is why guns are run space-charge limited rather than temperature limited.

4. Sinclair and Miller’s SLAC GaAs gun was designed for 15 A at 200 kV from a 2 cm diameter cathode; their test gun was designed for 50 A at 50 kV from a 1 cm cathode. Find both perveances, and the planar Child-Langmuir gap for each.

Answer

\(K = I/V^{3/2}\): \(15/(2\times10^5)^{3/2} = 1.68\times10^{-7}\) A/V\(^{3/2}\) = 0.17 µperv; \(50/(5\times10^4)^{3/2} = 4.47\times10^{-6}\) = 4.5 µperv. Planar gap \(d = \sqrt{\chi A V^{3/2}/I}\) with \(\chi = 2.334\times10^{-6}\) A/V\(^{3/2}\): for the full gun, \(A = \pi(1\ \mathrm{cm})^2\), \(d = 66\) mm; for the test gun, \(A = \pi(0.5\ \mathrm{cm})^2\), \(d = 6.4\) mm. The high-perveance gun is a short, wide diode; the low-perveance one can have a long gap and a low field at the cathode.

5. The BNL magnetron delivers 100 mA of H⁻ through a 2.8 mm diameter aperture at 35 kV extraction. Find the current density, the planar Child-Langmuir gap, and the mean field across it.

Answer

\(J = 0.1/\pi(1.4\times10^{-3})^2 = 1.62\times10^4\) A/m² = 1.6 A/cm². For H⁻, \(\chi = (4\varepsilon_0/9)\sqrt{2e/M} = 5.44\times10^{-8}\) A/V\(^{3/2}\) (with \(M \approx m_p + 2m_e\)). \(d = \sqrt{\chi V^{3/2}/J} = 4.7\) mm, and \(V/d = 7.5\) MV/m. A real extraction geometry differs from a planar diode (the aperture is not small compared with the gap, and the meniscus is curved), but the estimate shows why the gap sits at a field where caesium-laden surfaces spark.

6. The LCLS copper cathode (\(\phi_w = 4.31\) eV) is lit at 253 nm, and the field at the moment of emission is 57.5 MV/m. Find the photon energy, the Schottky lowering and the excess energy, and from them the thermal emittance per mm of rms spot size. Repeat with Dowell and Schmerge’s quantum-efficiency-consistent \(\phi_{\mathrm{eff}} = 4.56\) eV. What does each give for the 1.3 mm diameter uniform spot, and how does that compare with the measured slice emittance of 0.9 µm?

Answer

\(\hbar\omega = hc/\lambda = 4.90\) eV. \(\Delta\phi_w = \sqrt{e\mathcal{E}/4\pi\varepsilon_0}\) (in volts) \(= 0.288\) eV, so \(\phi_{\mathrm{eff}} = 4.02\) eV and the excess is 0.88 eV. \(\varepsilon_n/\sigma_x = \sqrt{0.88/(3\times510\,999)} = 0.76\) µm per mm. With \(\phi_{\mathrm{eff}} = 4.56\) eV the excess is 0.34 eV and the slope 0.47 µm per mm (Dowell and Schmerge quote 0.46 from their full model). The spot has \(\sigma_x = 0.65/2 = 0.325\) mm, giving 0.25 µm and 0.15 µm. The measured slope was about twice the second figure, about 0.9 µm per mm, or 0.29 µm: about a third of the 0.9 µm slice emittance.

7. For the LCLS gun, estimate the largest charge that can be emitted from a uniform spot of 1.3 mm diameter when the field at emission is 55 MV/m, and from the 2.0 mm design spot at 60 MV/m.

Answer

\(Q_{max} = \varepsilon_0 \mathcal{E} A\). For 1.3 mm: \(8.854\times10^{-12}\times55\times10^6\times\pi(0.65\times10^{-3})^2 = 0.65\) nC. For 2.0 mm at 60 MV/m: 1.67 nC. So by this estimate 1 nC needs the larger spot, and the smaller spot, whose thermal emittance is 0.65 times as large, must carry less charge. The estimate assumes a bunch short compared with the time its head takes to move a spot radius away; the LCLS’s 10 ps bunch is not, and it did draw 1 nC from the 1.3 mm spot, at the cost of a bunch longer than designed. In practice the bunch is run well below \(Q_{max}\), because near it the emission is distorted.

8. The LCLS beam has a normalised emittance of 1.2 µm. Find its geometric emittance at the 6 MeV gun exit, at 135 MeV, and at 14 GeV, assuming the normalised emittance is kept.

Answer

\(\varepsilon = \varepsilon_n/(\beta\gamma)\) with \(\gamma = 1 + W/m_ec^2\). At 6 MeV, \(\beta\gamma = 12.70\) and \(\varepsilon = 94\) nm. At 135 MeV, \(\beta\gamma = 265.2\) and \(\varepsilon = 4.5\) nm. At 14 GeV, \(\beta\gamma = 27\,398\) and \(\varepsilon = 0.044\) nm, or 44 pm. The physical angles shrink as the beam gains longitudinal momentum; this is adiabatic damping, and it is why the normalised emittance is the number that a source can be held to.

9. Derive the angle of the Pierce electrode for a sheet beam. Then say what changes for a round beam.

Answer

Inside the beam, \(\phi = V(z/d)^{4/3}\). Outside, Laplace’s equation holds, with \(\phi\) and \(\partial\phi/\partial y = 0\) given on the beam edge \(y = 0\). The function \(\phi = V\,\mathrm{Re}[(w/d)^{4/3}]\), \(w = z + iy\), is harmonic, equals \(V(z/d)^{4/3}\) on the real axis, and its \(y\)-derivative there is the real part of \(i\) times a real number, zero. In polar form \(\phi = V(r/d)^{4/3}\cos(4\theta/3)\). The cathode-potential surface \(\phi = 0\) is at \(4\theta/3 = \pi/2\), so \(\theta = 3\pi/8 = 67.5^\circ\) from the beam edge. For a round beam the inside solution is the same along the axis, but the outer Laplace problem has cylindrical symmetry and no simple closed form; the electrode is found numerically and is curved, starting at about the same angle near the cathode edge.

10. Show that circularly polarised light just above the band gap of unstrained GaAs gives electrons at most 50 per cent polarised, and say why strain helps.

Answer

The valence-band top has \(j = 3/2\); the conduction-band bottom has \(j = 1/2\). Light of helicity \(+1\) drives \(\Delta m_j = +1\), so the allowed transitions are \(-3/2 \to -1/2\) and \(-1/2 \to +1/2\). Their relative strengths, from the squared Clebsch-Gordan coefficients for coupling a \(j = 3/2\) state with a photon to \(j = 1/2\), are 3 and 1. The conduction electrons are spin down and spin up in the ratio 3 to 1, \(P = (3 - 1)/(3 + 1) = 0.5\). In unstrained GaAs the heavy-hole (\(m_j = \pm3/2\)) and light-hole (\(m_j = \pm1/2\)) levels have the same energy at the band edge, so both transitions are excited together. Strain splits them; tuning the light to excite only the heavy-hole transition gives, in principle, 100 per cent. This is the idea behind the strained cathodes Maruyama and colleagues reported in 1991.
