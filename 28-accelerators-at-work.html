28 Accelerators at work

1895 to today: the X-ray tube, the medical linac, proton and carbon-ion therapy, the isotope cyclotron, the implanter, the industrial electron machine, the spallation source and the AMS tandem; the wall is the step from a physics machine run by its builders to one that runs every day for people who are not physicists, with a dose or a count known to a few per cent.

In one page

The wall. Every machine in this course began as a physics instrument, tended by the people who built it. Medicine, industry and the other sciences need something else: a beam that is there every morning, whose dose or count is known to a few per cent, in a box that fits a hospital basement or a factory floor. Getting there took decades for each kind of machine. The first medical linac treated a patient in 1953; protons were proposed for therapy in 1946 and reached a hospital only in 1989 and 1990.

The idea. Nothing new in the acceleration. The work lies in what the beam does when it stops in matter, and in making the machine dull and dependable. More than 30,000 accelerators run today, and only about 1 per cent of them are research machines above 1 GeV; most treat patients or implant ions in silicon.

The equations to remember.

\[ \begin{aligned} \lambda_{min} &= \frac{hc}{eV} && \text{shortest X-ray wavelength from a tube at voltage } V \\ I(z) &= I_0\, e^{-\mu_a z} && \text{photons in matter, } \mu_a \text{ in m}^{-1} \\ \mathcal{R}(W) &= \int_0^{W}\frac{dW'}{S(W')} \approx a_{BK} W^{p_{BK}}, \quad p_{BK} \approx 1.76 && \text{range of a proton, the Bragg peak at its end} \\ \mathcal{A}(t) &= Y_{sat}\, I \left(1 - e^{-\lambda_d t}\right) && \text{activity made by a beam current } I \text{ in time } t \\ \dot m &= \frac{\eta_u P}{D} && \text{mass treated per second by beam power } P \text{ at dose } D \\ t_{age} &= -8033\ \text{years} \times \ln F && \text{radiocarbon age from the measured fraction } F \end{aligned} \]

Numbers worth knowing. A 6 MV photon beam reaches its maximum dose 1.5 cm under the skin and keeps 67 per cent of it at 10 cm. A 230 MeV proton stops in 32.9 cm of water, its range spread by about 1 per cent; its rigidity is 2.32 T m. Carbon at 430 MeV per nucleon reaches 30.9 cm, but its rigidity is 6.62 T m, and Heidelberg’s carbon gantry weighs 670 tonnes. Fluorine-18 has a half-life of 109.7 minutes: two hours on the road cost more than half of it. Technetium-99m is milked from molybdenum-99, whose 66 hour half-life sets the weekly rhythm of nuclear medicine. A 50 µA carbon beam from a modern sample brings about 370 atoms of carbon-14 a second to the detector, out of \(3\times10^{14}\) of carbon-12. A 1 GeV proton on lead knocks out about 20 neutrons, 50 MeV of beam a neutron; the Spallation Neutron Source’s design 1.4 MW of 1 GeV protons is \(8.7\times10^{15}\) protons a second.

The wall

Tubes that would not repeat themselves

Wilhelm Röntgen found X-rays on 8 November 1895. On 29 January 1896 Emile Grubbé in Chicago gave an X-ray treatment to a patient with breast cancer (AAPM Virtual Museum). The tubes of the time were gas discharge tubes (chapter 2): the current came from ions striking a cold cathode, the gas pressure changed as the tube ran, and with it the voltage, the hardness of the rays (their power to penetrate, which rises with the voltage, because a faster electron can make a more energetic photon) and the dose, the energy the rays leave in each kilogram of tissue. A physician could not give the same dose twice. William Coolidge at General Electric removed the gas in 1913: a heated tungsten filament supplied the electrons in a high vacuum, so the current was set by the filament temperature and the energy by the voltage, independently. Dose became something to prescribe.

Rays that burned the skin

The next wall was depth. X-rays from a few hundred kilovolts give their largest dose at the skin, and a deep tumour could not be given a curative dose without burning the surface. Orthovoltage tubes, the name for X-ray tubes of about 150 to 300 kV, came in during the 1920s; in 1933 the first 1000 kV machines were installed at the Swedish Hospital in Seattle and at Caltech (AAPM Virtual Museum). Robert Van de Graaff and John Trump put a 1 MV electrostatic generator into the Collis P. Huntington Memorial Hospital in Boston in 1937, and from 1946 High Voltage Engineering built 2 to 2.5 MV therapy machines, about 40 of them before production stopped in 1959 (chapter 6). Kerst’s betatron (chapter 11) gave tens of MeV and treated patients through the 1950s, but its output was about 1 Gy per minute (Livingston and Blewett give up to 100 roentgen a minute at 1 m for 20 to 22 MeV machines, section 7-12). The gray, 1 Gy = 1 J/kg, is the unit of absorbed dose, energy left per kilogram (chapter 8, section 12; section 9 below); the roentgen is the older unit of exposure, the charge the rays set free in a kilogram of air, and one roentgen leaves about 0.01 Gy in soft tissue, so 100 roentgen a minute is about 1 Gy a minute. The betatron was also large and hard to rotate around a patient (Podgorsak, section 5.4). From 1951 cobalt-60 units gave 1.25 MeV gamma rays from a sealed source 1 to 2 cm across, and they dominated radiotherapy into the 1980s.

The first medical linacs

The travelling-wave linac grew from wartime microwave work (chapter 14). On 19 August 1953 an 8 MeV machine built by Metropolitan-Vickers treated its first patient at Hammersmith Hospital in London. The same year Mullard installed a 4 MeV isocentric linac (one mounted so that the beam, as the machine turns round the patient, always passes through one fixed point, the isocentre, placed in the tumour) at Newcastle General Hospital. In January 1956 a 6 MeV linac at Stanford, developed with the Varian brothers, Edward Ginzton and William Hansen, treated its first patient under Henry Kaplan: a seven-month-old child with retinoblastoma, a cancer of the eye (AAPM Virtual Museum). Linacs began replacing cobalt units in the 1980s. Today the medical linac is the most numerous kind of accelerator in the world.

Forty years from proposal to hospital

In 1946 Robert R. Wilson pointed out in Radiology that a proton beam deposits most of its energy just before it stops, so that its depth of maximum dose can be placed on a tumour by choosing the energy. The first patients were treated with physics machines: at Berkeley in 1954, at Uppsala in 1957, and at the Harvard Cyclotron Laboratory with Massachusetts General Hospital from 1961 until 2002, 9,115 patients in all (Harvard Gazette, 2002; Wikipedia gives 9,116). The machines belonged to physics laboratories and were built for physics. The first proton centres inside hospitals were Clatterbridge in 1989, a low-energy cyclotron for eye tumours, and Loma Linda University Medical Center in California in 1990. Which was first depends on the definition: Chao and Tigner’s Handbook (section 1.6.14) calls Loma Linda’s synchrotron, built by Fermilab, the first hospital-based proton therapy accelerator.

Isotopes, and counting atoms

John Lawrence treated patients with cyclotron-made phosphorus-32 from 1936 or 1937 (accounts differ) (chapter 10). After the war, reactors made most radioisotopes more cheaply, and the cyclotron’s medical role shrank until short-lived positron emitters, which a reactor makes poorly, brought it back (a positron emitter is a nucleus that decays by giving out a positron, the electron’s antiparticle of F7, section 14; such a nucleus has too many protons for its neutrons, and a reactor’s neutrons, absorbed by nuclei, make the opposite kind, while a proton beam adds the protons; the pre-requisite before section 6 explains the decays). In archaeology the wall was the size of the sample: counting the decays of carbon-14 needs at least 10 g of carbon. (Carbon-14 is made in the air by cosmic rays and taken up by every living thing; after death it decays with a half-life of about 5,700 years, so the fraction left dates the sample, section 11. It decays so slowly that a gram of modern carbon gives only about 14 decays a minute.) In 1977 Richard Muller proposed counting the atoms themselves with an accelerator, and two groups did it with tandems the same year (chapter 6).

The physics

Each machine here was explained in its own chapter. What is new is the beam’s fate: where its energy goes in tissue, in a target or in a sample.

1. A census

Wikipedia, citing the IAEA, gives more than 30,000 accelerators in operation, and splits them: about 44 per cent for radiotherapy, 41 per cent for ion implantation, 9 per cent for industrial processing and research, 4 per cent for biomedical and other low-energy research, and about 1 per cent research machines above 1 GeV. On 30,000 that is about 13,200 therapy machines, 12,300 implanters, 2,700 industrial machines and 300 large research machines. The IAEA’s Directory of Radiotherapy Centres now lists more than 20,000 units in more than 8,500 centres; that count includes equipment other than accelerators, and a study drawing on it around 2020 found about 13,900 external-beam machines (machines that aim a beam into the patient from outside, as against radioactive sources placed inside the body), 12.7 per cent of them cobalt units. The IAEA counts over 1,500 cyclotron facilities making radionuclides, about 1,300 of them in its database. Counting machines built rather than running, Chao and Tigner’s Handbook (section 1.6.10, citing Reviews of Accelerator Science and Technology vol. 10) gives more than 46,000 accelerators built from 1968 to 2017: about 31,000 for industry, 14,000 for medical treatment and only 1,200 for research. About 14,000 implanters were sold from 1968 to 2018, about as many as the medical accelerators installed worldwide.

Pre-requisite (continued): Photons and the photoelectric effect

Pre-requisite. Chapter 1 gave the photon’s energy \(E = h\nu = hc/\lambda\) and its momentum \(E/c\), and the rule that one electron can give at most all its kinetic energy to one photon, which is the Duane-Hunt limit of section 2. This section adds two things that sections 2 and 3 use. The first is the characteristic X-ray lines that sit on top of a tube’s continuous spectrum. The second is Compton scattering, the event by which a megavoltage photon hands its energy to an electron in tissue, and the largest energy that electron can get. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, volume 3, §74 (X-ray terms); Landau and Lifshitz, volume 2, §13 (elastic collisions of particles, of which Compton scattering is the case of a massless projectile); and Livingston and Blewett, §14-4 (X-ray attenuation).

Characteristic lines: the idea. The electron beam in an X-ray tube does two things to the atoms of its target. An electron deflected by a nucleus radiates the continuous bremsstrahlung of section 2. An electron that strikes an atomic electron can knock it out of an inner shell, and the atom is left with a hole deep inside it. An electron from a higher shell then drops into the hole, and the energy difference leaves as one photon. The shells have fixed energies, so these photons have fixed energies too. They form sharp lines, and the lines are characteristic of the element of the target.

Why the energy is so large. An inner electron sits close to the nucleus, inside nearly all the other electrons, so it feels almost the bare nuclear charge \(Ze\). Its energy is then close to that of the single electron of a hydrogen-like ion of charge \(Ze\):

\[ E_n \approx -\frac{Z^2\,\mathrm{Ry}}{n^2}, \]

with \(n\) the shell number (\(n = 1\) for the shell called K, 2 for L, 3 for M) and \(\mathrm{Ry} = 13.606\) eV the Rydberg energy, the binding energy of hydrogen. This is Bohr’s formula for hydrogen, \(E_n = -\mathrm{Ry}/n^2\) (F7, section 7), with the nuclear charge \(e\) replaced by \(Ze\): the attraction is \(Z\) times stronger, which pulls the orbit \(Z\) times closer, and the energy, which goes as charge over radius, rises by \(Z \times Z\). The scale is \(Z^2\) times hydrogen’s: tens of keV for a heavy atom, against a few eV for the outer electrons that make visible light. Landau and Lifshitz (vol. 3, §74) refine this by replacing \(Z\) with \(Z - \delta\), where \(\delta\) is a small number that accounts for screening by the other inner electrons.

Moseley’s rule. The brightest line, K\(\alpha\), comes from an L electron (\(n = 2\)) filling a hole in the K shell (\(n = 1\)). The L electron sees the nucleus screened by the one K electron that remains, so take \(\delta \approx 1\):

\[ E_{K\alpha} \approx (Z - 1)^2\,\mathrm{Ry}\left(\frac{1}{1^2} - \frac{1}{2^2}\right) = \frac{3}{4}(Z - 1)^2\,\mathrm{Ry}. \]

This is Moseley’s rule of 1913, the measurement that sorted the elements by \(Z\) (F7, section 5).

Target \(Z\) Estimate \(\tfrac34(Z - 1)^2\,\mathrm{Ry}\) Measured K\(\alpha_1\)
Copper 29 8.00 keV 8.05 keV
Molybdenum 42 17.15 keV 17.48 keV
Tungsten 74 54.4 keV 59.3 keV

The estimate is good to 1 or 2 per cent for the lighter elements and 8 per cent low for tungsten. There the inner electrons move fast enough for relativistic corrections to matter; Landau and Lifshitz call the levels they split relativistic doublets.

At work. A line appears only if the beam electrons can make the hole: their energy \(eV\) must exceed the binding energy of the K shell, 69.5 keV in tungsten (the hydrogen-like estimate with \(Z - 1\) gives 72.5 keV). So a tungsten tube run at 60 kV shows no K lines at all. At 100 or 120 kV its spectrum carries lines at 58.0 and 59.3 keV (K\(\alpha_2\) and K\(\alpha_1\)) and 67.2 keV (K\(\beta\)) on top of the continuum. Mammography tubes use molybdenum targets for their 17.5 keV line, an energy at which soft tissues differ in absorption. Livingston and Blewett (§14-4) point out that the lines are far too soft to matter when shielding a megavoltage machine.

Compton scattering: the set-up. At megavoltage energies a photon in water mostly scatters off an electron (section 3). The electron’s binding, tens of eV, is nothing beside an MeV, so treat the electron as free and at rest. Before: a photon of energy \(E_\gamma\) and momentum \(E_\gamma/c\), and an electron of rest energy \(m_ec^2\) at rest. After: a photon of energy \(E'_\gamma\) travelling at angle \(\theta\) to the first, and the electron moving off with kinetic energy \(W_e = E_\gamma - E'_\gamma\).

Solving it with four-momenta. Write the photon’s four-momenta \(K\) before and \(K'\) after, and the electron’s \(P\) before and \(P'\) after. Chapter 1’s notation: a four-momentum is \((E/c, \mathbf{p})\), and its invariant square is \(m^2c^2\), which is zero for a photon. Here it helps to name one more operation. The dot product of two four-vectors \(A = (A_0, \mathbf{a})\) and \(B = (B_0, \mathbf{b})\) is

\[ A\cdot B = A_0B_0 - \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}, \]

the ordinary dot product of F2, section 2 for the three space parts, with a minus sign. With \(B = A\) it is \(A_0^2 - |\mathbf{a}|^2\), chapter 1’s invariant square, written \(A^2\); and like that square it is the same in every frame (expand \((A + B)^2 = A^2 + 2A\cdot B + B^2\): the two squares on the right are invariant, and so is the left, so \(A\cdot B\) must be). Squaring a sum works as in ordinary algebra, \((A + B - C)^2 = A^2 + B^2 + C^2 + 2A\cdot B - 2A\cdot C - 2B\cdot C\), because the dot product multiplies out term by term.

Conservation is \(K + P = K' + P'\). The unknown \(P'\) is removed by putting it alone on one side and squaring. The squares are \(P'^2 = P^2 = m_e^2c^2\) and \(K^2 = K'^2 = 0\):

\[ P' = K + P - K' \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m_e^2c^2 = 0 + m_e^2c^2 + 0 + 2K\cdot P - 2K'\cdot P - 2K\cdot K' . \]

Each dot product is easiest in the laboratory, where the electron starts at rest, \(P = (m_ec, \mathbf{0})\): \(K\cdot P = (E_\gamma/c)(m_ec)\) and \(K'\cdot P = (E'_\gamma/c)(m_ec)\). For the two photons, the momenta have sizes \(E_\gamma/c\) and \(E'_\gamma/c\) with the angle \(\theta\) between them, so \(K\cdot K' = (E_\gamma/c)(E'_\gamma/c) - (E_\gamma/c)(E'_\gamma/c)\cos\theta = (E_\gamma E'_\gamma/c^2)(1 - \cos\theta)\). Cancelling \(m_e^2c^2\) from both sides and dividing by 2:

\[ m_e(E_\gamma - E'_\gamma) = \frac{E_\gamma E'_\gamma}{c^2}(1 - \cos\theta) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E'_\gamma = \frac{E_\gamma}{1 + x_\gamma(1 - \cos\theta)}, \qquad x_\gamma = \frac{E_\gamma}{m_ec^2} . \]

The second form follows by solving the first for \(E'_\gamma\), multiplied through by \(c^2\) and with the \(E'_\gamma\) terms gathered on one side,

\[ m_ec^2E_\gamma = E'_\gamma\left[m_ec^2 + E_\gamma(1 - \cos\theta)\right], \]

then dividing top and bottom by \(m_ec^2\). In wavelengths (\(E = hc/\lambda\), so divide the first form by \(m_eE_\gamma E'_\gamma/hc\)) this is Compton’s result of 1923, \(\lambda' - \lambda = (h/m_ec)(1 - \cos\theta)\), with \(h/m_ec = 2.426\) pm. The same steps with a massive projectile gave chapter 8 the largest energy a fast ion can give an electron; Landau and Lifshitz (vol. 2, §13) do the general case.

The largest electron energy. The electron takes the most when the photon comes straight back, \(\theta = 180°\), so that \(1 - \cos\theta = 2\):

\[ W_{e,max} = E_\gamma - \frac{E_\gamma}{1 + 2x_\gamma} = E_\gamma\,\frac{2x_\gamma}{1 + 2x_\gamma} . \]

It always falls short of \(E_\gamma\). A free electron cannot swallow a photon whole, because energy and momentum cannot both be conserved; that is why the photoelectric effect needs an atom to take up the momentum. \(W_{e,max}\) is called the Compton edge: a detector in which photons scatter records a continuum of electron energies that ends sharply there.

At work. Section 3 takes its numbers from here. A 2 MeV photon, a fair average for a 6 MV beam, sets an electron moving with up to 1.77 MeV, and an electron of that energy goes about a centimetre in water before it stops: that sets the depth over which the dose builds up. The photon that comes back has less than \(m_ec^2/2 = 0.256\) MeV however energetic it was, because for large \(x_\gamma\) the formula tends to \(E_\gamma/(2x_\gamma) = m_ec^2/2\). So radiation scattered back from a patient or a wall is soft and easy to shield, while forward scatter keeps most of the energy and the direction (Livingston and Blewett, §14-4).

Exercise P1. Estimate the K\(\alpha\) energy of rhodium (\(Z = 45\)), another mammography target, and say whether a tube at 28 kV excites it. Its K-shell binding energy is 23.2 keV.

Answer

\(\tfrac34 \times 44^2 \times 13.606\) eV \(= 19.8\) keV; the measured K\(\alpha_1\) is 20.2 keV. The electrons bring 28 keV, more than the 23.2 keV needed to empty the K shell, so the line appears. (The hydrogen-like estimate of the binding, \(44^2 \times 13.606\) eV \(= 26.3\) keV, would also say yes.)

Exercise P2. A PET scanner (positron emission tomography: it images where a positron-emitting drug has gathered in the body, section 7) detects 511 keV photons. Where is their Compton edge, and what is the least energy a 511 keV photon can keep after one scattering?

Answer

\(x_\gamma = 1\), so \(W_{e,max} = 511 \times 2/3 = 340.7\) keV, and the photon keeps \(511/3 = 170.3\) keV after scattering straight back. A scanner rejects photons well below 511 keV in its detectors, because a photon that has scattered in the patient no longer points back along its true line.

Exercise P3. Show that a photon scattered through \(90°\) keeps less than \(m_ec^2\) however energetic it was, and find the electron’s kinetic energy when a 6 MeV photon scatters through \(90°\).

Answer

At \(90°\), \(E'_\gamma = E_\gamma/(1 + x_\gamma) = m_ec^2\,x_\gamma/(1 + x_\gamma) < m_ec^2\). For 6 MeV, \(x_\gamma = 11.74\) and \(E'_\gamma = 0.471\) MeV, so the electron gets \(6 - 0.471 = 5.53\) MeV.

2. X-rays from an electron beam

The picture. An electron that passes close to a nucleus is deflected, and an accelerated charge radiates (the Larmor formula; Landau and Lifshitz vol. 2, §67; Tong, Electromagnetism, section 7.2.2). The radiation is bremsstrahlung, “braking radiation”; its quantum theory is in Landau and Lifshitz vol. 4, §92 and §93, and Tong treats it classically in section 7.4.4.

The upper limit. One electron of kinetic energy \(W = eV\), accelerated through a voltage \(V\) (V), can give at most all of it to one photon. So the spectrum ends sharply at \(h\nu_{max} = eV\), the Duane-Hunt limit (F7, section 5), or in wavelength

\[ \lambda_{min} = \frac{hc}{eV}, \]

with \(h\) Planck’s constant (J s) and \(c\) the speed of light (m/s). At 100 kV, \(\lambda_{min} = 0.0124\) nm; at 120 kV, 0.0103 nm. The spectrum below the limit is continuous, with the characteristic lines of the target metal on top.

Efficiency and heat. The fraction of the beam energy radiated, the radiative yield, grows with the atomic number \(Z\) of the target and with the electron energy. Podgorsak gives it as of order 1 per cent at about 100 keV and 10 to 20 per cent in the megavoltage range (section 1.3.2). So a diagnostic tube is a heater that leaks X-rays: at 120 kV and 200 mA it takes \(P = VI = 120\ \text{kV} \times 0.2\ \text{A} = 24\) kW, of which about 240 W leaves as X-rays and the rest heats the small spot the beam strikes (the focal spot, kept small, about a millimetre, because the X-rays seem to come from it and a small source casts sharp shadows). That is why the anode is tungsten, with its high atomic number and melting point, and why in large tubes it rotates, sweeping the beam over a larger area. At low energy the emission peaks near 90° to the electron path; at megavoltage it points forward, so a medical linac fires its electrons straight into a transmission target, a target thin enough that the photons come out of its far side, and uses the forward cone (Podgorsak, section 1.3.2).

Pre-requisite (continued): Cross-section, rate and luminosity basics

Pre-requisite. Chapter 3 defined a cross-section as an effective target area, and gave the probability \(n\sigma\,dx\) of an interaction in a thin layer, the attenuation \(e^{-n\sigma L}\), the mean free path, the thin-target rate and the barn. Section 3 uses these for photons in tissue and needs three more things: how the attenuation coefficient follows from a cross-section per electron, why tables give it per unit mass (\(\mu_a/\rho\), cm\(^2\)/g) and not per unit length, and the half-value and tenth-value thicknesses in which shielding is reckoned. Sections 6 and 8 use the thin-target rate and the barn as chapter 3 gave them. Taught from Livingston and Blewett, §14-4 (X-ray attenuation: cross-sections, the linear and mass absorption coefficients, the half-value thickness).

From a cross-section to an attenuation coefficient. Chapter 3’s rule is that a particle crossing a layer \(dz\) that holds \(n\) targets per unit volume, each of cross-section \(\sigma\), interacts with probability \(n\sigma\,dz\). So the linear attenuation coefficient of section 3 is \(\mu_a = n\sigma\). The only question is what to count as a target.

For Compton scattering the target is an electron. Each electron in the material scatters as if it were free, with a cross-section \(\sigma_e\) that depends only on the photon’s energy. A material of density \(\rho\) made of atoms of atomic number \(Z\) and molar mass \(A\) (g/mol) holds \(\rho/A\) moles per unit volume, each of \(N_A\) atoms (Avogadro’s number, F7, section 1), so \(N_A\rho/A\) atoms per unit volume and \(Z\) times as many electrons, so

\[ \mu_a = \rho\,N_A\,\frac{Z}{A}\,\sigma_e, \qquad \frac{\mu_a}{\rho} = N_A\,\frac{Z}{A}\,\sigma_e . \]

Work in centimetres and grams, as the tables do: \(\rho\) in g/cm\(^3\), \(N_A = 6.022\times10^{23}\) mol\(^{-1}\), \(Z/A\) in mol/g, \(\sigma_e\) in cm\(^2\) (1 b \(= 10^{-24}\) cm\(^2\)), and then \(\mu_a\) is in cm\(^{-1}\) and \(\mu_a/\rho\) in cm\(^2\)/g. For a compound, \(Z/A\) means electrons per gram summed over its atoms, divided by \(N_A\); for water, H\(_2\)O, it is \(10/18.015 = 0.5551\).

Why per unit mass. The density has dropped out of \(\mu_a/\rho\), which depends only on \(Z/A\) and \(\sigma_e\). And \(Z/A\) hardly varies: 0.4995 for carbon, 0.4992 for air, 0.5551 for water (its hydrogen, with \(Z/A = 0.992\), raises it), 0.3958 for lead. So where Compton scattering dominates, a gram of almost any material stops photons about as well as a gram of any other. A table of \(\mu_a/\rho\) for water serves for soft tissue, and what decides how many photons cross a patient is the mass per unit area crossed, \(\rho z\) in g/cm\(^2\). Written with the mass coefficient, the attenuation law is

\[ I = I_0 \exp\left[-\frac{\mu_a}{\rho}\,(\rho z)\right]. \]

A check with the electron’s cross-section. The cross-section of a free electron for a photon is the Klein-Nishina formula (Landau and Lifshitz, vol. 4, §86), quoted here. With \(x_\gamma = E_\gamma/(m_ec^2)\) as in the photon section above and \(r_e = e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 m_ec^2) = 2.818\times10^{-13}\) cm the classical electron radius (the distance at which the Coulomb energy of two electrons equals the electron’s rest energy; it sets the size of every cross-section between a photon and a free electron),

\[ \sigma_e = 2\pi r_e^2\left\{\frac{1 + x_\gamma}{x_\gamma^2}\left[\frac{2(1 + x_\gamma)}{1 + 2x_\gamma} - \frac{\ln(1 + 2x_\gamma)}{x_\gamma}\right] + \frac{\ln(1 + 2x_\gamma)}{2x_\gamma} - \frac{1 + 3x_\gamma}{(1 + 2x_\gamma)^2}\right\} . \]

At low energy it tends to the classical Thomson value \((8\pi/3)r_e^2 = 0.665\) b (the cross-section of an electron shaken by a light wave and re-radiating it, by the Larmor formula); at MeV energies it falls roughly as \(1/E_\gamma\). In water:

At 6 MeV it falls 12 per cent short: a photon above \(2m_ec^2 = 1.022\) MeV can turn into an electron-positron pair in the field of a nucleus, with a probability that rises with energy, and that adds to the attenuation (Livingston and Blewett, §14-4).

Half-value and tenth-value thickness. Setting \(e^{-\mu_a z} = \tfrac12\) gives the half-value thickness \(\ln 2/\mu_a = 0.693/\mu_a\); setting it to \(\tfrac1{10}\) gives the tenth-value thickness \(\ln 10/\mu_a = 2.303/\mu_a\). Shielding is counted in these: \(n\) tenth-value layers cut a narrow beam by \(10^n\). The mean free path \(1/\mu_a\) is the distance over which the beam falls by \(e\).

At work. For lead at 2 MeV, NIST gives \(\mu_a/\rho = 0.0461\) cm\(^2\)/g; with \(\rho = 11.35\) g/cm\(^3\), \(\mu_a = 0.523\) cm\(^{-1}\), a half-value thickness of 1.33 cm and a tenth-value thickness of 4.40 cm. The Compton part alone, with lead’s lower \(Z/A\), would be only 0.0349 cm\(^2\)/g; the rest is the photoelectric effect and pair production, which both grow steeply with \(Z\). Gram for gram, lead at 2 MeV is no better than water (0.0461 against 0.0494 cm\(^2\)/g). Its worth as a shield is its density, which packs the same grams into about an eleventh of the thickness.

3. Photons in tissue: attenuation and build-up

Attenuation. A photon beam is not slowed; it is thinned. Each photon travels some distance and then interacts once, mostly by Compton scattering at megavoltage energies (Klein-Nishina; Landau and Lifshitz vol. 4, §86). If the probability of interacting in \(dz\) is \(\mu_a\,dz\), the number \(I\) of photons not yet interacted obeys \(dI = -\mu_a I\,dz\): the loss in a thin layer is proportional to how many arrive. That is the equation of exponential decay, with depth in the place of time (F1, section 10), so

\[ I(z) = I_0 e^{-\mu_a z}. \]

Here \(\mu_a\) (m\(^{-1}\)) is the linear attenuation coefficient; I write it with a subscript because \(\mu\) in this course is a phase advance. NIST tabulates the mass coefficient \(\mu_a/\rho\) for water: 0.0632 cm\(^2\)/g at 1.25 MeV (cobalt-60), 0.0494 cm\(^2\)/g at 2 MeV, 0.0277 cm\(^2\)/g at 6 MeV. At 2 MeV the mean free path is 20 cm and half the photons have interacted after 14 cm. A photon beam therefore has no range; it falls off slowly and leaves through the far side of the patient.

Build-up. Photons do not deposit dose themselves. They set electrons moving (in a Compton event, up to 1.77 MeV for a 2 MeV photon), and the electrons deposit it over a range of millimetres to centimetres. At the skin few electrons have yet been set moving, so the dose is low. It rises with depth until as many electrons stop in a layer as start in it, a condition called charged-particle equilibrium, reached at a depth \(z_{max}\) about equal to the range of the secondary electrons. Beyond it the dose falls with the photon fluence, the number of photons crossing each square centimetre (Podgorsak, section 6.5). This skin sparing is what the megavoltage machines bought: \(z_{max}\) is 0.5 cm for cobalt-60, 1.5 cm for 6 MV, 3.5 cm for 18 MV (Podgorsak, table 6.1). A photon beam is named by the voltage of the electrons that made it, in MV: a 6 MV beam comes from electrons of about 6 MeV, and holds bremsstrahlung photons of every energy up to 6 MeV, most of them far below it; that is why 2 MeV serves as its typical photon.

Depth dose. Beyond \(z_{max}\) three effects shape the curve: attenuation, the inverse square of the distance from the source, and scattered photons that add dose. The inverse square is geometry: the photons leave a small source and spread over a sphere whose area grows as the square of the distance, so the number per square centimetre falls as \(1/(\text{distance})^2\). The source sits a distance \(f\) from the skin (100 cm for a linac), so a point at depth \(z\) is \(f + z\) from it. The first two effects alone give, for a 6 MV beam treated as 2 MeV photons,

\[ \frac{D(10\ \text{cm})}{D(z_{max})} \approx e^{-\mu_a (10 - 1.5)}\left(\frac{f + 1.5}{f + 10}\right)^2 = 0.657 \times 0.851 = 0.56 . \]

Here \(\mu_a = 0.0494\ \text{cm}^2/\text{g} \times 1\ \text{g/cm}^3 = 0.0494\) cm\(^{-1}\) for water, and the beam crosses \(10 - 1.5 = 8.5\) cm beyond \(z_{max}\). The measured value for a 10 × 10 cm\(^2\) field (the beam’s cross-section where it enters the patient) is 67 per cent (Podgorsak, table 6.4); the eleven points are scatter, which grows with field size. For cobalt-60 the same estimate (1.25 MeV photons, \(z_{max} = 0.5\) cm, \(f = 100\) cm) gives 46 per cent against a measured 59. The lesson stands: photons put more dose in front of a deep tumour than in it, and the only way to concentrate dose is to come from many directions and shape each field. Figure 28.2 in the next section sets this curve beside a proton’s.

4. Protons and ions in tissue: the Bragg peak

The picture. A proton is slowed continuously, by many small collisions with electrons, and it slows faster the slower it is (chapter 8, section 2). Bohr’s impulse estimate (chapter 1) shows why: an electron at impact parameter \(b\) (the distance by which the proton’s straight path misses it) feels the proton’s Coulomb pull for a time of about \(2b/v\), and receives momentum \(\Delta p = 2ze^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 b v)\), with \(ze\) the projectile’s charge and \(v\) its speed; so it takes energy \(\Delta p^2/2m_e \propto 1/v^2\). A slow particle spends longer near each electron and gives it a bigger push. The loss per unit path, the stopping power \(S\), is given by the Bethe formula of chapter 8, which in water (\(Z/A = 0.5551\), mean excitation energy 75 eV), integrated upward from the tabulated range at 10 MeV, reproduces the ranges of the NIST PSTAR table to about 0.1 per cent. It is quoted and explained there and used here.

The range. A particle of kinetic energy \(W\) (MeV) that loses energy continuously stops after

\[ \mathcal{R}(W) = \int_0^{W} \frac{dW'}{S(W')}, \]

with \(S\) in MeV cm\(^2\)/g and \(\mathcal{R}\) in g/cm\(^2\) (for water, numerically cm). The reason is short: \(S = -dW/dx\), so a step \(dx\) costs energy \(dW = S\,dx\), and turned round, each small energy \(dW'\) is spent over a path \(dW'/S(W')\); adding the paths from the full energy down to zero gives the integral. The units are those of the tables: the stopping power per unit length (MeV/cm) divided by the density (g/cm\(^3\)), the mass stopping power, so that path is counted in grams per square centimetre crossed, as for photons in the pre-requisite above; for water, of density 1 g/cm\(^3\), the two counts are the same number. I write \(\mathcal{R}\) because \(R\) is a ring’s radius. Integrating the Bethe formula upward from PSTAR’s 0.1230 g/cm\(^2\) at 10 MeV gives

Proton energy Stopping power at entry Range in water
70 MeV 9.56 MeV cm\(^2\)/g 4.08 cm
150 MeV 5.45 MeV cm\(^2\)/g 15.77 cm
230 MeV 4.11 MeV cm\(^2\)/g 32.94 cm
250 MeV 3.91 MeV cm\(^2\)/g 37.93 cm

PSTAR gives 31.74 cm at 225 MeV and 37.94 cm at 250 MeV, so 32.9 cm at 230 MeV agrees. That is why therapy machines are built for 230 to 250 MeV: 30 cm of water covers any tumour in an adult. The stopping power at 1 MeV is about 65 times the entry value at 230 MeV; that steep rise at the end of the track is the Bragg peak (figure 28.2).

Between 50 and 250 MeV the ranges follow the Bragg-Kleeman rule

\[ \mathcal{R} \approx a_{BK} W^{p_{BK}}, \qquad a_{BK} = 0.00226\ \text{cm}, \quad p_{BK} = 1.765 \quad (W \text{ in MeV}), \]

a fit to the integrated values that is good to about 2 per cent (1.1 per cent at 230 MeV, 1.7 per cent at 250 MeV). The exponent carries a subscript because \(p\) is momentum. Near 230 MeV, \(d\mathcal{R}/dW = 1/S\) (the integrand, by the fundamental theorem of F1, section 7) means \(dW = S\,d\mathcal{R} = 4.11\ \text{MeV/cm} \times 0.1\ \text{cm} = 0.41\) MeV per millimetre: the energy must be known to a fraction of an MeV to place the peak to a millimetre.

Straggling. The collisions are random, so protons of one energy stop at slightly different depths. The spread is measured by the variance \(\sigma^2\), the mean squared departure from the average, and its square root, the standard deviation \(\sigma\) (F6, section 3). The energy a proton loses in a layer is the sum of very many small, independent kicks, so it scatters about its mean in a Gaussian (F6, sections 5 and 7), and variances of independent pieces add. Bohr’s estimate of the variance of the energy lost per unit path, from the same impulse picture, is

\[ \frac{d\sigma_W^2}{dx} = 4\pi r_e^2 (m_e c^2)^2 N_A \frac{Z}{A}\,\frac{1 - \beta^2/2}{1 - \beta^2} \]

per g/cm\(^2\), in MeV\(^2\) when \(r_e\), the classical electron radius, is in cm and \(m_e c^2\) in MeV; \(N_A\) is Avogadro’s number (mol\(^{-1}\)), \(Z/A\) is in mol/g, \(\beta\) is the proton’s speed over \(c\), and the last factor is the relativistic correction (for an ion of charge number \(z\) multiply by \(z^2\)). A spread in energy loss becomes a spread in range through \(d\mathcal{R} = dW/S\): a layer \(dx\) crossed at energy \(W'\) adds a variance \((d\sigma_W^2/dx)\,dx\) in energy, which is a variance \((d\sigma_W^2/dx)\,dx/S(W')^2\) in range (a quantity divided by \(S\) has its standard deviation divided by \(S\), and so its variance by \(S^2\); chapter 1), and \(dx = dW'/S(W')\). The layers are independent, so their variances add, and the sum over layers becomes an integral over the energy:

\[ \sigma_{\mathcal{R}}^2 = \int_0^{W} \frac{d\sigma_W^2/dx}{S(W')^3}\,dW' . \]

Numerically this gives 0.34 cm at 230 MeV, 1.0 per cent of the range, and 1.1 per cent at 70 MeV. The peak of a single energy is therefore a few millimetres wide, sharper at low energy.

From a peak to a plateau. A tumour is centimetres deep, so a treatment superposes (adds together) Bragg peaks of several energies, each weighted, to make a flat high-dose region, the spread-out Bragg peak. Each peak brings its entrance plateau with it, so the dose before the tumour is higher than for one peak; beyond the distal edge (the deep side, the side away from the source) there is almost none. The deepest peak needs the largest weight, since every shallower depth already gets the plateaus of the peaks behind it (figure 28.3).

Eighteen proton energies from 116 to 150 MeV (ranges 10.0 to 15.8 cm), each a Bragg curve as in figure 28.2, weighted (thin lines) so that their sum (thick) is flat to half a per cent over a tumour from 10 to 15.3 cm deep. Changing the energy. A fixed-energy cyclotron (chapter 12), the usual choice, lowers its energy with a degrader, a block of material in the beam whose thickness takes off the unwanted energy by the same stopping power, and throws away much of the beam, which comes out of the block with its energies and angles spread by straggling and scattering; a synchrotron (chapter 15) extracts at the energy wanted, cycle by cycle (chapter 21).

Pre-requisite (continued): Four-momentum, energy-momentum relation, invariant mass

Pre-requisite. Chapter 1 built the four-momentum, the relation \(E^2 = (pc)^2 + (Mc^2)^2\), the kinetic energy \(W = (\gamma - 1)Mc^2\) and the invariant mass; sections 5 and 11 use them as they stand, and the next section’s rule that equal kinetic energy per nucleon means nearly equal speed follows from \(\gamma = 1 + W/(Mc^2)\), since the rest energy per nucleon, \(Mc^2/A\), is nearly the same for every nucleus (938.3 MeV for the proton, 931.2 MeV for carbon-12). Section 10 needs one more result: a particle at rest that decays into two gives each product a fixed momentum, set by the three masses alone. Section 7’s positron annihilation is the same result with two photons. Taught from Landau and Lifshitz, volume 2, §11 (decay of particles).

The set-up. A particle of mass \(M\), at rest, breaks into two particles of masses \(m_1\) and \(m_2\). Before, the momentum is zero and the energy is \(Mc^2\). After, particle 1 has energy \(E_1\) and momentum \(\mathbf{p}_1\), particle 2 has \(E_2\) and \(\mathbf{p}_2\). Two laws fix everything.

Momentum. \(\mathbf{p}_1 + \mathbf{p}_2 = 0\): the two fly apart back to back, with momenta of the same size \(p\).

Energy. \(E_1 + E_2 = Mc^2\).

Solving. Write chapter 1’s relation for each product and subtract. Since both have the same \(p\), the momentum terms cancel:

\[ E_1^2 - E_2^2 = (m_1^2 - m_2^2)c^4 . \]

The left side is \((E_1 - E_2)(E_1 + E_2) = (E_1 - E_2)Mc^2\), so \(E_1 - E_2 = (m_1^2 - m_2^2)c^2/M\). Adding this to \(E_1 + E_2 = Mc^2\) and halving,

\[ E_1 = \frac{M^2 + m_1^2 - m_2^2}{2M}\,c^2, \qquad E_2 = \frac{M^2 + m_2^2 - m_1^2}{2M}\,c^2 . \]

The momentum follows from \(p^2c^2 = E_1^2 - m_1^2c^4\). Put \(E_1\) in: the numerator becomes \((M^2 + m_1^2 - m_2^2)^2 - (2Mm_1)^2\), a difference of two squares, and each factor, \((M \pm m_1)^2 - m_2^2\), is again a difference of two squares. Collecting the four factors,

\[ pc = \frac{\sqrt{\left[M^2 - (m_1 + m_2)^2\right]\left[M^2 - (m_1 - m_2)^2\right]}}{2M}\,c^2 . \]

Three things follow. The decay is possible only if \(M > m_1 + m_2\), as Landau and Lifshitz point out: a body can fall apart only into pieces whose masses add to less than its own. Every decay gives the same energies, so the products come out in sharp lines, as chapter 1 found for alpha decay. And when the second product is massless or nearly so (a photon or a neutrino), \(m_2 = 0\) and the formula simplifies to

\[ pc = \frac{M^2 - m_1^2}{2M}\,c^2 . \]

A decay into three bodies has no such rule: the three can share the energy in many ways, and the products come out with a continuous spectrum. Beta decay and the muon’s own decay, in the sections that follow, are of this kind.

At work: surface muons. A positive pion (\(m_\pi c^2 = 139.570\) MeV) at rest decays into a muon (\(m_\mu c^2 = 105.658\) MeV) and a neutrino, whose mass is negligible here. Then \(p_\mu c = (139.570^2 - 105.658^2)/(2\times139.570) = 29.79\) MeV; the muon’s total energy is \(E_\mu = 109.78\) MeV, its kinetic energy \(W_\mu = 4.12\) MeV, its \(\gamma = 1.039\) and \(\beta = 0.271\). The neutrino carries the other 29.79 MeV, and the energies add up: \(109.78 + 29.79 = 139.57\) MeV. Every muon from a pion stopped in the target has this momentum, so a beam line tuned to 29.8 MeV/\(c\) collects them. Their low kinetic energy is what lets them stop in a thin sample.

At work: annihilation. An electron and a positron at rest have total momentum zero and total energy \(2m_ec^2\). Treat the pair as one body of mass \(M = 2m_e\) decaying into two photons: each photon gets \(pc = E = Mc^2/2 = m_ec^2 = 511\) keV, and the two go in opposite directions. That is the pair of 511 keV photons a PET scanner looks for.

\(p_\mu c = (493.677^2 - 105.658^2)/(2\times493.677) = 235.5\) MeV. Then \(E_\mu = \sqrt{235.5^2 + 105.658^2} = 258.1\) MeV and \(W_\mu = 152.5\) MeV. Heavier parents give faster muons; the momentum alone tells which parent decayed.

5. Carbon ions and the size of the gantry

Scaling the range. The Bethe stopping power depends on the projectile only through its charge number \(z\) and its speed \(\beta\): \(S = z^2 f(\beta)\), with \(f\) the same function for every ion (the electrons of the water feel only the passing charge and how fast it passes, as in Bohr’s impulse picture). Since \(W = (\gamma - 1)Mc^2\), a change of energy is \(dW = M c^2\, d\gamma\) for a projectile of mass \(M\), and since \(\gamma\) is fixed by \(\beta\), the range down from a given speed is

\[ \mathcal{R}(\beta) = \int \frac{M c^2\,d\gamma}{z^2 f(\beta)} = \frac{M}{z^2}\, g(\beta), \]

with \(g(\beta) = \int c^2\,d\gamma/f(\beta)\) the same function for every ion, since \(M\) and \(z\) are constants that come outside the integral. So a carbon nucleus (\(M c^2 = 11{,}175\) MeV, \(z = 6\)) goes \((M/z^2)/(m_p/1^2) = M/(36 m_p) = 0.33\) times as far as a proton of the same speed. Equal speed means equal \(\gamma = 1 + W/(Mc^2)\). Carbon at 430 MeV per nucleon has \(\gamma = 1 + 430/931.2 = 1.462\), where 931.2 MeV is its rest energy per nucleon, \(11{,}175/12\); a proton with the same \(\gamma\) has \(W = 0.462 \times 938.27 = 433\) MeV, and the range of a 433 MeV proton, from the Bethe integral, is 93.4 cm. Carbon’s is \(0.331 \times 93.4 = 30.9\) cm. The approximation ignores the electrons a slow ion picks up and the fragmentation of carbon nuclei on the way, which leaves a tail of dose beyond the peak.

A real check. The Heidelberg Ion-Beam Therapy Centre gives protons from 48 to 221 MeV and carbon from 88 to 430 MeV per nucleon. With the scaling, those ranges are 2.07 and 30.8 cm for protons, 2.06 and 30.9 cm for carbon: the two ion ranges were chosen to cover the same depths (figure 28.4).

Rigidity. The machine pays for carbon in magnets. The magnetic rigidity \(B\rho = p/q\) (T m; chapter 1, section 4.1) fixes the field \(B\) times the bending radius \(\rho\) any magnet must provide. With the momentum from \(E^2 = (pc)^2 + (Mc^2)^2\) and \(E = W + Mc^2\), where \(W\) is now the whole ion’s kinetic energy (12 times the energy per nucleon for carbon) and \(q = ze\) its charge:

\[ B\rho = \frac{\sqrt{(W + Mc^2)^2 - (Mc^2)^2}}{q c}. \]

For 230 MeV protons \(B\rho = 2.32\) T m; for 430 MeV per nucleon carbon, 6.62 T m, 2.85 times more. With iron magnets at 1.8 T the bending radii are 1.29 m and 3.68 m. A gantry, the rotating beam line that brings the beam to the patient from any angle, must bend the beam by 90° or more at a radius of metres and turn the whole assembly about the patient to a fraction of a millimetre. Heidelberg’s carbon gantry is 25 m long, 13 m in diameter and weighs 670 tonnes. Superconducting magnets (chapter 17) are the way to shrink it.

Pre-requisite (continued): Nuclear reactions and Q value

Pre-requisite. Chapter 1 taught reaction notation, the decay law with its decay constant, half-life and activity, and Q values from mass excesses; chapter 6 added the threshold of a reaction whose Q is negative, and chapter 8 the slowing and capture of neutrons and the build-up of activity under a beam. Sections 6 to 8 and 12 need four things more. The first is the two kinds of beta decay, and why a positron emitter needs 1.022 MeV more than a bare mass difference. The second is what becomes of the positron: two photons of 511 keV, back to back. The third is gamma emission from an excited nucleus, and the long-lived excited states written with an m, such as technetium-99m. The fourth is the arithmetic of a neutron chain that dies out, which gives a subcritical reactor its multiplication. Taught from Penrose, The Road to Reality, §25.3 (the weak interaction and the neutrino); Landau and Lifshitz, volume 2, §11 (decay of particles); and Chao and Tigner’s Handbook, sections 1.6.14 (medical applications) and 1.6.17 (neutron sources, for the neutrons a spallation proton gives). None of these books works the subcritical chain, so its arithmetic is done here from the definition of \(k_{eff}\).

Names. Nuclei with the same atomic number \(Z\) and different mass numbers \(A\) are isotopes of one element: oxygen-16 and oxygen-18. Nuclei with the same \(A\) and different \(Z\) are isobars: oxygen-18 and fluorine-18, or carbon-14 and nitrogen-14, the pair section 11 must tell apart. Beta decay changes \(Z\) by one and leaves \(A\) alone, so it moves a nucleus to a neighbouring isobar.

Beta decay. A nucleus with too many neutrons for its protons can turn a neutron into a proton, an electron and an antineutrino; this is beta-minus decay, \(Z \to Z + 1\). A nucleus with too many protons can turn a proton into a neutron, a positron and a neutrino, which is beta-plus decay, or swallow one of its own inner atomic electrons, which is electron capture; both take \(Z \to Z - 1\). A free proton is lighter than a neutron and cannot do this, but inside a nucleus the binding energies can pay for it. All three are the work of the weak interaction (Penrose, §25.3). In beta decay three bodies share the energy, so the electron or positron comes out with any energy from zero up to the Q value, a continuous spectrum, unlike the sharp lines of a two-body decay.

Q values from atomic masses. Tables give the masses of neutral atoms, nucleus plus \(Z\) electrons, as mass excesses \(\Delta\) (chapter 1). Counting the electrons carefully:

  • Beta-minus. The parent atom has \(Z\) electrons. The daughter nucleus has charge \(Z + 1\), and it keeps the \(Z\) electrons plus the one emitted: together, a neutral daughter atom. So \(Q_{\beta^-} = \Delta_{parent} - \Delta_{daughter}\).
  • Beta-plus. The daughter nucleus has charge \(Z - 1\), but the parent’s \(Z\) electrons are still there, so one electron is left over, and a positron of the same mass is made. So \(Q_{\beta^+} = \Delta_{parent} - \Delta_{daughter} - 2m_ec^2\), with \(2m_ec^2 = 1.022\) MeV.
  • Electron capture. The captured electron was part of the parent atom. So \(Q_{EC} = \Delta_{parent} - \Delta_{daughter}\), less the small binding energy of the captured electron.

The electrons’ own binding energies, eV to keV, are ignored. The consequence: an atom heavier than its lower neighbour by less than 1.022 MeV can decay only by electron capture; above that, both routes are open and compete.

At work: fluorine-18. With \(\Delta(^{18}\mathrm{F}) = 873.1\) keV and \(\Delta(^{18}\mathrm{O}) = -782.8\) keV, \(Q_{EC} = 1655.9\) keV and \(Q_{\beta^+} = 1655.9 - 1022.0 = 633.9\) keV. Both routes are open, and positron emission wins 97 per cent of the time (section 7). The positron takes on average about 250 keV of the 634 keV available, and travels on average a fraction of a millimetre in tissue before it annihilates; that distance blurs a PET image. The same mass excesses give the reaction that makes the fluorine, \(^{18}\)O(p,n)\(^{18}\)F, with \(\Delta(^1\mathrm{H}) = 7289.0\) keV and \(\Delta(\mathrm{n}) = 8071.3\) keV:

\[ Q = \Delta(^{18}\mathrm{O}) + \Delta(^{1}\mathrm{H}) - \Delta(\mathrm{n}) - \Delta(^{18}\mathrm{F}) = -2438.2\ \text{keV} . \]

The reaction runs electron capture backwards and turns a hydrogen atom into a neutron, which costs \(8071.3 - 7289.0 = 782.3\) keV; indeed \(-1655.9 - 782.3 = -2438.2\) keV. By chapter 6’s threshold rule, the proton needs at least \(2.438 \times (1 + 1.008/17.999) = 2.574\) MeV. Cyclotrons use about 18 MeV, where the cross-section is large.

What becomes of the positron. A positron slows in matter by the same collisions as an electron, and once slow it meets an electron and the pair annihilates. The pair is then nearly at rest: total momentum close to zero, total energy \(2m_ec^2 = 1.022\) MeV. One photon cannot carry this away, because a photon of energy \(E\) always has momentum \(E/c\), never zero. Two photons can. By the two-body decay of the previous continued section, they go in opposite directions with 511 keV each. The pair’s small leftover momentum, a few keV/\(c\), tilts them from exactly back to back by a few milliradians. A PET scanner records the two photons in coincidence and knows the decay lay on the line between its two detectors.

Gamma emission and metastable states. A decay or reaction often leaves the daughter nucleus excited. It falls to lower states by emitting gamma photons of fixed energies, as an atom does with light, but with energies of keV to MeV; section 8’s lithium-7 gives up 0.48 MeV this way. Most excited states live far less than a nanosecond. A few can reach the states below only by a large change of angular momentum, which makes the transition very slow: they live minutes, hours or years. Such a long-lived excited state is an isomer, marked with an m. Technetium-99m lies 142.7 keV above the ground state of technetium-99, has a half-life of 6.007 hours, and gives the 140.5 keV gamma of section 7. It has the same \(Z\) and \(A\) as technetium-99, so it is chemically the same element, which is why it can be drawn off and made into drugs. Its ground state, technetium-99, lives 211,000 years and so is almost inert in a patient; but it dilutes the chemistry, which is why section 7 counts its presence against the proton route.

A chain that dies out. A neutron absorbed by uranium-235 or plutonium-239 can split the nucleus. Each fission releases about 200 MeV and on average \(\nu\) new neutrons, about 2.4 for uranium-235 split by slow neutrons and nearer 3 for plutonium split by fast ones (values quoted). Some neutrons escape the assembly, some are captured without fission, and the rest cause new fissions. Define \(k_{eff}\), the effective multiplication factor, as the mean number of neutrons in the next generation for each neutron in this one.

Feed the assembly with \(S_0\) neutrons a second from outside. In the steady state the total rate \(n\) at which neutrons are born satisfies

\[ n = S_0 + k_{eff}\,n \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n = \frac{S_0}{1 - k_{eff}}, \]

the sum of the geometric series \(S_0(1 + k_{eff} + k_{eff}^2 + \dots)\) of section 12, and finite only if \(k_{eff} < 1\). The multiplication \(M_n = 1/(1 - k_{eff})\) is 10 at \(k_{eff} = 0.9\), 20 at 0.95, 100 at 0.99. At \(k_{eff} = 1\) the chain sustains itself with no source; that is a critical reactor. With \(k_{eff} > 1\) it grows. In a subcritical assembly, when the source stops, each generation is \(k_{eff}\) times the one before, and the chain dies away. (Some fission neutrons come out seconds late, from the decay of fission products; they slow the die-away, but do not change the steady-state sum.)

At work: the power. Each fission makes \(\nu\) neutrons, so \(k_{eff}/\nu\) is the chance that a given neutron causes a fission, and each source neutron leads to \((k_{eff}/\nu)M_n\) fissions. With \(k_{eff} = 0.95\) and \(\nu = 2.9\), that is 6.6 fissions, 1310 MeV, for every source neutron. A spallation neutron costs about 50 MeV of beam (section 10), so the core returns about 26 times the beam power as heat. As a rough check on MYRRHA (section 12): 600 MeV, 4 mA protons, taking about 12 neutrons per proton at that energy, give \(3.0\times10^{17}\) neutrons a second and about 63 MW of fission power, within its planned 50 to 100 MW.

Exercise N1. Carbon-11 (\(\Delta = 10650.3\) keV) decays to boron-11 (\(\Delta = 8667.7\) keV). Find \(Q_{EC}\) and the largest positron energy.

Answer

\(Q_{EC} = 10650.3 - 8667.7 = 1982.6\) keV, and \(Q_{\beta^+} = 1982.6 - 1022.0 = 960.6\) keV, the largest kinetic energy a positron can take (when the neutrino takes almost none).

Exercise N2. A subcritical core runs at \(k_{eff} = 0.95\). A change in temperature raises it to 0.96 with the beam unchanged. By how much does the power change, and what does \(dM_n/dk_{eff}\) say about running closer to 1?

Answer

\(M_n\) goes from 20 to 25, so the power rises by 25 per cent for a rise of 0.01 in \(k_{eff}\). Since \(dM_n/dk_{eff} = 1/(1 - k_{eff})^2\), which is 400 at 0.95 and 10,000 at 0.99, the nearer to critical, the more sensitive the power is to small changes in the core. That is the price of the higher gain, and the reason \(k_{eff}\) is kept near 0.95.

Exercise N3. Why can an electron-positron pair at rest not annihilate into a single photon, and why can a positron in flight not do so either?

Answer

A single photon has invariant mass zero: \(E^2 - (pc)^2 = 0\). A pair at rest has \(E = 2m_ec^2\) and \(p = 0\), so its invariant mass is \(2m_e\); in flight the invariant mass is larger still, and it is the same in every frame. Since conservation of energy and momentum conserves the invariant mass, the pair can never become one photon. Inside matter a nucleus close by can take up the momentum, and one-photon annihilation does happen there, but rarely.

6. Making radioactive nuclei with a beam

The rate. A beam of current \(I\) (A) carries \(I/(ze)\) particles per second, since each carries charge \(ze\) and a current is charge per second. In a thin target with \(n_t\) target nuclei per unit area (m\(^{-2}\)), each projectile meets a reaction of cross-section \(\sigma\) (m\(^2\)) with probability \(n_t\sigma\), the fraction of the target’s face covered by the nuclei’s effective areas (chapter 1, section 6.1); so reactions occur at the rate

\[ R_p = \frac{I}{ze}\, n_t\, \sigma . \]

In a thick target the projectile slows as it goes, \(\sigma\) changes with energy, and the rate per incident particle is \(\int n\,\sigma(W)\,dx = \int (n\,\sigma(W)/S_l(W))\,dW\), with \(n\) the number density of target nuclei and \(S_l = -dW/dx\) the linear stopping power (MeV per metre; the change of variable \(dx = dW/S_l\) is the one of the range integral in section 4): the beam spends longer at energies where it slows slowly.

Growth and decay. The product nucleus decays with decay constant \(\lambda_d = \ln 2/t_{1/2}\) (s\(^{-1}\)). The number \(N\) present obeys

\[ \frac{dN}{dt} = R_p - \lambda_d N, \qquad N(0) = 0, \]

since nuclei are made at the rate \(R_p\) and lost at the rate \(\lambda_d N\) (the decay law of F7, section 13). Its solution is \(N(t) = (R_p/\lambda_d)(1 - e^{-\lambda_d t})\). To see it, let \(u = R_p/\lambda_d - N\), the distance still to go to the level where loss balances production. Then \(du/dt = -dN/dt = -\lambda_d u\), plain exponential decay (F1, section 10), so \(u = u(0)e^{-\lambda_d t}\) with \(u(0) = R_p/\lambda_d\); and \(N = R_p/\lambda_d - u\) is the result. The activity, decays per second (Bq), is

\[ \mathcal{A}(t) = \lambda_d N = R_p\left(1 - e^{-\lambda_d t}\right) = Y_{sat}\, I \left(1 - e^{-\lambda_d t}\right). \]

The saturation yield \(Y_{sat}\) (Bq per ampere) is the activity at which decay balances production. After one half-life the activity is half of saturation, after two three-quarters (\(e^{-\lambda_d t} = 2^{-t/t_{1/2}}\) is \(\tfrac12\), then \(\tfrac14\)). Irradiating much longer than a half-life or two buys little: the beam then mostly replaces what decays. Figure 28.5 (left) shows a real run.

7. Two isotopes: fluorine-18 and technetium-99m

Fluorine-18. Most PET scans use fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG; in the United States 96 per cent of PET procedures in 2011 and about 74 per cent in 2023, by IMV’s surveys), a glucose labelled with fluorine-18. The nucleus is made by protons on water enriched in oxygen-18, by the reaction \(^{18}\)O(p,n)\(^{18}\)F, with protons of typically about 18 MeV. Its half-life is 109.7 minutes and it decays 97 per cent of the time by emitting a positron, which annihilates into two 511 keV photons that the scanner records in coincidence: a ring of detectors round the patient, and only pairs of hits on opposite sides within a few nanoseconds of each other are counted, so each pair marks a line through the point of decay.

A real machine. IBA quotes for its 18 MeV Cyclone KIUBE (100 µA, upgradable to 300 µA on target) 35 Ci, 1,295 GBq, of fluorine-18 from a 2 hour run, enough for up to 350 doses (the curie, 1 Ci \(= 3.7\times10^{10}\) Bq, is the older unit of activity, F7, section 13). Two hours is \(1 - 2^{-120/109.7} = 0.53\) of saturation, so \(Y_{sat} I\) must be \(35/0.53 = 66\) Ci. At 300 µA that is a saturation yield of 0.22 Ci, 8.1 GBq, per microampere; at 150 µA it would have to be twice that. The yield per microampere is fixed by the cross-section of the reaction and the stopping power, so a site’s output is set by the current its target can take.

The logistics. After 1 hour \(2^{-60/109.7} = 68\) per cent remains, after 2 hours 47, after 3 hours 32 (figure 28.5, left). A patient receives 200 to 400 MBq. The 35 Ci of the run, if delivered two hours later without losses, would be about 2,000 doses of 300 MBq; synthesis yields and a schedule spread over the day bring it far lower. The half-life draws the map: FDG can be flown for 1 to 3 hours from a cyclotron, and shorter-lived positron emitters (carbon-11 about 20 minutes, nitrogen-13 about 10, oxygen-15 about 2) can only be used beside the cyclotron that makes them.

Technetium-99m. About 20 million diagnostic procedures a year use technetium-99m, which emits a 140.5 keV gamma ray and has a half-life of 6.007 hours. It comes from the beta decay of molybdenum-99 (half-life 65.93 hours), shipped to hospitals in a shielded generator from which the technetium is chemically extracted as needed: salt water washed through a column that holds the molybdenum carries the technetium out and leaves the molybdenum behind. The washing is called elution, or milking the generator. The daughter grows in by the equations of section 6 with a decaying source. With \(N_1\) the parent and \(N_2\) the daughter, decay constants \(\lambda_1\), \(\lambda_2\), and \(b\) the fraction of parent decays that feed the daughter (the rest go straight to technetium-99’s ground state), the parent decays as \(N_1(0)e^{-\lambda_1 t}\) and so feeds the daughter at the rate \(b\lambda_1N_1(0)e^{-\lambda_1 t}\):

\[ \frac{dN_2}{dt} = b\lambda_1 N_1(0) e^{-\lambda_1 t} - \lambda_2 N_2, \qquad \mathcal{A}_2(t) = b\,\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_2 - \lambda_1}\,\mathcal{A}_1(0)\left(e^{-\lambda_1 t} - e^{-\lambda_2 t}\right). \]

The right-hand form, for a generator just eluted (\(N_2(0) = 0\)), can be checked by putting it back into the equation. Try \(N_2 = C(e^{-\lambda_1 t} - e^{-\lambda_2 t})\), which starts at zero. Then \(dN_2/dt + \lambda_2N_2 = C(\lambda_2 - \lambda_1)e^{-\lambda_1 t}\), the \(e^{-\lambda_2 t}\) terms cancelling, and this must equal \(b\lambda_1N_1(0)e^{-\lambda_1 t}\), so \(C = b\lambda_1N_1(0)/(\lambda_2 - \lambda_1)\). Multiplying by \(\lambda_2\) to turn \(N_2\) into the activity \(\mathcal{A}_2 = \lambda_2 N_2\), and writing \(\mathcal{A}_1(0) = \lambda_1N_1(0)\), gives the formula. Setting \(d\mathcal{A}_2/dt = 0\) gives the time of greatest activity (F1, section 5; worked in exercise 7), \(t_{max} = \ln(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)/(\lambda_2 - \lambda_1) = 22.8\) hours: a generator milked once a day is near its best each morning. Later the daughter’s activity follows the parent’s, in the ratio \(b\lambda_2/(\lambda_2 - \lambda_1) = 1.10\,b\) (transient equilibrium). After a week 17 per cent of the molybdenum is left, so generators are replaced weekly.

Accelerator routes. Most molybdenum-99 comes from the fission of uranium-235 in a few research reactors. When two old reactors that between them supplied about two-thirds of the world’s molybdenum-99, Canada’s NRU and the Dutch HFR, were shut down repeatedly in 2009 and 2010, technetium ran short worldwide. The accelerator routes are protons on enriched molybdenum-100, making technetium-99m directly by \(^{100}\)Mo(p,2n), and photons from an electron linac on molybdenum-100, making molybdenum-99 by (\(\gamma\),n). Both need costly enriched targets, and the proton route also makes the long-lived ground state technetium-99.

8. Neutron capture therapy

Boron-10 captures a slow neutron with a cross-section of about 3,840 barns (\(3.84\times10^{-25}\) m\(^2\)), a thousand times that of the nitrogen, hydrogen and oxygen of tissue. The compound nucleus, boron-11 formed for an instant by the boron and the neutron merged, breaks into an alpha particle and lithium-7: the reaction \(^{10}\)B(n,\(\alpha\))\(^7\)Li. The masses release 2.79 MeV, but in about 94 per cent of captures the lithium is left excited and emits a 0.48 MeV gamma ray, so the two particles share 2.31 MeV. Momentum conservation from rest divides the energy inversely as the masses (the two fly apart with equal and opposite momenta \(p\), and a kinetic energy \(p^2/2m\) is larger for the lighter one; F3, section 6): the alpha gets \(2.31 \times 7/11 = 1.47\) MeV and the lithium \(2.31 \times 4/11 = 0.84\) MeV. Both stop within 5 to 9 µm, about one cell. So if a drug puts boron into tumour cells and not their neighbours, a flood of slow neutrons kills the cells that took up boron. The neutrons used to come from reactors. Accelerators now make them by protons of 1.9 to 3.0 MeV on lithium-7, or protons of 5 to 30 MeV on beryllium-9, with moderators to slow them. A cyclotron-based system went into clinical use at Kyoto in 2012, and in March 2020, after a phase II trial (the second stage of testing a treatment on patients, which measures whether it works), Japan approved Sumitomo’s cyclotron system as a medical device; the Kansai BNCT Medical Center began treating with it in 2020.

Pre-requisite (continued): Resonant cavity modes

Pre-requisite. Chapter 7 and chapter 8 taught cavity modes through the pillbox: its accelerating mode, the stored energy \(U\), the quality factor \(Q_0\) and the power lost in the walls. Chapter 9 showed that a shorted length of coaxial line resonates. Section 9’s Rhodotron is built on a different cavity, a length of coaxial line closed at both ends. This section works out its fundamental mode: the radial electric field in the median plane, why the field must reverse while the electron is inside the inner conductor, the condition for re-entering the cavity in phase, and the power the walls take. Taught from Wangler, RF Linear Accelerators, section 1.11 (the coaxial resonator), and Chao and Tigner’s Handbook, section 1.6.20 (the Rhodotron).

The cavity. Take a coaxial line, an inner conductor of radius \(a\) inside an outer one of radius \(b\), both along \(z\), and close it with conducting plates at \(z = 0\) and \(z = l\). Between the conductors of a coaxial line a wave runs along \(z\) at the speed of light with the same field pattern as a static charge and current: the electric field is radial and falls as \(1/r\), by Gauss’s law (chapter 4), and the magnetic field circles the inner conductor and falls as \(1/r\), by Ampère’s law. Two such waves running opposite ways make a standing wave (F5, section 10), as on a string fixed at both ends. The end plates are conductors, so the field along them, here the radial field, must vanish at \(z = 0\) and \(z = l\). The voltage between the conductors therefore goes as \(\sin(\pi z/l)\) in the lowest mode, which fits half a wavelength into the length:

\[ l = \frac{\lambda}{2}, \qquad f = \frac{c}{2l} . \]

This is the half-wave coaxial resonator. With \(V_0\) (V) the peak voltage between the conductors at the middle, \(\omega = 2\pi f\) and \(r\) the distance from the axis, the fields are

\[ \begin{aligned} E_r &= \frac{V_0}{r\ln(b/a)}\,\sin\frac{\pi z}{l}\,\cos\omega t, \\ B_\theta &= -\frac{V_0}{c\,r\ln(b/a)}\,\cos\frac{\pi z}{l}\,\sin\omega t . \end{aligned} \]

The first is the coaxial field of chapter 4, scaled so that \(\int_a^b E_r\,dr = V_0\) in the middle: \(\int_a^b dr/r = \ln(b/a)\), which the denominator cancels. The second follows from Faraday’s law, \(\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t\) (F4, section 14; F2, section 11). The field has only a radial part that changes along \(z\), so the curl has only a \(\theta\) component, \(\partial E_r/\partial z\), and \(\partial B_\theta/\partial t = -\partial E_r/\partial z\). Differentiating \(E_r\) in \(z\) brings down \(\pi/l\) and turns the sine into a cosine; integrating in time turns \(\cos\omega t\) into \(\sin\omega t/\omega\); and \(\pi/(l\omega) = 1/c\), because \(\omega = 2\pi f = \pi c/l\).

The median plane. At \(z = l/2\) the sine is 1 and the cosine 0. The electric field is at its largest and purely radial, and the magnetic field is zero. So a beam that travels in the median plane is pushed only along its path, inwards or outwards, and no magnetic force from the cavity bends it. The current on the conductors, which makes \(B_\theta\), is largest at the end plates and zero at the middle. Chao and Tigner’s handbook describes exactly this: the beam crosses a half-wave coaxial cavity in its median plane (figure 28.6).

At work. At the 107.5 MHz of IBA (Ion Beam Applications, the Belgian firm that builds Rhodotrons and cyclotrons; see “How it was researched and built”), \(\lambda = 2.789\) m and \(l = 1.394\) m. (The real cavity’s central conductor ends in cones, which change the length somewhat; the simple formula gives the scale.)

Crossing along a diameter. An electron enters at the outer wall, at \(x = -b\), moving along \(x\) at nearly \(c\) (true after the first crossing). It crosses the gap between the conductors, passes through holes in the inner conductor, where there is no field, crosses the gap on the far side and leaves at \(x = +b\). Its charge is \(-e\). On the way in, it is pushed inwards, and so accelerated, while \(E_r > 0\) (field pointing outwards); on the way out it is accelerated while \(E_r < 0\). So the field must change sign while the electron is inside the inner conductor. That is the point of the design: the same field accelerates the electron twice.

Put \(t = 0\) at entry and let the field in the median plane be \(E_r = V_0\cos(\omega t + \varphi)/(r\ln(b/a))\), with \(\varphi\) the phase at entry (a cosine phase defined for this calculation only, not the sine-convention synchronous phase of chapter 12). On the way in, the electron is at radius \(r\) at time \((b - r)/c\); on the way out, at time \((b + r)/c\). The energy gained is the work done on the two halves:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta W &= \frac{eV_0}{\ln(b/a)}\int_a^b \frac{dr}{r}\left[\cos\left(\frac{\omega(b - r)}{c} + \varphi\right) - \cos\left(\frac{\omega(b + r)}{c} + \varphi\right)\right] \\ &= \frac{2eV_0}{\ln(b/a)}\,\sin\left(\frac{\omega b}{c} + \varphi\right)\int_a^b \frac{\sin(kr)}{r}\,dr, \qquad k = \frac{\omega}{c}, \end{aligned} \]

using \(\cos A - \cos B = 2\sin\tfrac{A + B}{2}\sin\tfrac{B - A}{2}\) (from the addition formulas of F1, section 3); here \(\tfrac{A + B}{2} = \omega b/c + \varphi\) and \(\tfrac{B - A}{2} = \omega r/c = kr\), and the first factor does not depend on \(r\), so it comes out of the integral. The best phase makes the first sine 1. Then \(\omega b/c + \varphi = \pi/2\), which says the field passes through zero just as the electron reaches the centre. The integral has no formula in elementary functions, but substituting \(s = kr\) turns it into \(\int_{ka}^{kb}(\sin s/s)\,ds\), a difference of the tabulated sine integral \(\mathrm{Si}(u) = \int_0^u (\sin s/s)\,ds\) (computed numerically, F1, section 11), so

\[ \Delta W_{max} = 2eV_0\,T_c, \qquad T_c = \frac{\mathrm{Si}(kb) - \mathrm{Si}(ka)}{\ln(b/a)} . \]

If the field could flip instantly at the centre and stay at its peak otherwise, the electron would gain \(2eV_0\). The transit factor \(T_c\), less than 1, is the price of a field that changes while the electron crosses.

At work. Take an illustrative cavity with \(a = 0.25\) m and \(b = 1.0\) m at 107.5 MHz (the handbook does not give the real dimensions). Then \(k = 2.253\) m\(^{-1}\), \(\mathrm{Si}(2.253) = 1.707\), \(\mathrm{Si}(0.563) = 0.553\), \(\ln 4 = 1.386\) and \(T_c = 0.832\). For 1 MeV a crossing, near IBA’s 0.83 to 1 MeV, the cavity needs \(V_0 = 1\ \text{MV}/(2\times0.832) = 601\) kV. The field at the surface of the inner conductor, where it is largest, is \(V_0/(a\ln(b/a)) = 1.73\) MV/m, far below breakdown.

Re-entering in phase. The crossing takes \(2b/c = 6.67\) ns, and the RF period is \(1/f = 9.30\) ns. For the next crossing to see the same field as the first, it must begin a whole number \(n\) of periods after the first began. The mode looks the same at every azimuth, so the next crossing can be along any diameter. The time outside, from exit to re-entry, is \(n/f - 2b/c\): 2.63 ns for \(n = 1\), only 0.79 m of path, too short to turn the beam round; 11.93 ns for \(n = 2\), a path of 3.58 m outside the cavity, which a magnet can provide. Since the electrons travel at nearly \(c\) after the first crossing, every loop takes the same time and every magnet can be alike. The first is the exception: after one crossing an electron of about 1 MeV has \(\beta = 0.94\), so its loop must be shorter to keep time, which is why the handbook says the first magnet differs.

Power in the walls. The stored energy is the electric energy at the moment the field peaks, when the magnetic field is zero everywhere. The energy density of an electric field is \(\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2\) per unit volume (F4, section 6); the volume is cut into rings of radius \(r\), thickness \(dr\) and height \(dz\), of volume \(2\pi r\,dr\,dz\), and \(\int_0^l\sin^2(\pi z/l)\,dz = l/2\):

\[ U = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int E_r^2\,dV = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\,\frac{V_0^2}{\ln^2(b/a)}\int_a^b\frac{2\pi r\,dr}{r^2}\int_0^l \sin^2\frac{\pi z}{l}\,dz = \frac{\pi\varepsilon_0 V_0^2\, l}{2\ln(b/a)} . \]

The walls lose \(\tfrac12 R_s |H|^2\) per unit area, with \(H = B/\mu_0\) the peak tangential magnetic field and \(R_s\) the surface resistance of chapter 13. The peak \(|H|\) is \(V_0\cos(\pi z/l)/(\eta_0 r\ln(b/a))\), with \(\eta_0 = \mu_0c = 376.7\) Ω. Integrate over the walls. On the inner conductor, \(r = a\), the area element is \(2\pi a\,dz\) and \(\int_0^l\cos^2(\pi z/l)\,dz = l/2\), so it takes \(\tfrac12R_s \times V_0^2/(\eta_0^2a^2\ln^2(b/a)) \times 2\pi a \times l/2 = \pi R_sV_0^2 l/(2a\,\eta_0^2\ln^2(b/a))\); the outer conductor gives the same with \(b\) for \(a\). On each end plate the cosine is \(\pm1\) and the area element is \(2\pi r\,dr\), so \(\int_a^b (1/r^2)\,2\pi r\,dr = 2\pi\ln(b/a)\), and the two plates together take \(\tfrac12R_s \times 2 \times 2\pi\ln(b/a)\,V_0^2/(\eta_0^2\ln^2(b/a))\). Adding the three:

\[ P = \frac{\pi R_s V_0^2}{2\eta_0^2\ln^2(b/a)}\left[l\left(\frac1a + \frac1b\right) + 4\ln\frac{b}{a}\right], \qquad Q_0 = \frac{\omega U}{P} = \frac{\pi\eta_0\ln(b/a)}{R_s\left[l(1/a + 1/b) + 4\ln(b/a)\right]}, \]

Wangler’s equation 1.31. The thin inner conductor carries the most current per unit width, and with these dimensions it takes 45 per cent of the loss, the end plates 44 per cent, the outer wall 11 per cent.

At work. For copper at 107.5 MHz, \(R_s = \sqrt{\pi f\mu_0/\sigma_c} = 2.71\) mΩ, with \(\sigma_c = 5.8\times10^7\) S/m. The illustrative cavity has \(Q_0 = 48{,}500\), close to the 50,000 the handbook gives for IBA’s cavity at 107.5 MHz. At \(V_0 = 601\) kV it stores \(U = 5.06\) J and loses \(P = \omega U/Q_0 = 70.5\) kW, in line with the handbook’s 70 to 105 kW for a continuous-wave Rhodotron at medium energy.

Exercise R1. The Rhodotron patent’s example gains 1.67 MeV a pass. Using the illustrative cavity, what wall power would that take?

Answer

The gain is proportional to \(V_0\) and the power to \(V_0^2\), so \(P = 70.5\ \text{kW} \times 1.67^2 = 197\) kW. The patent gives 278 kW at 100 MHz for its own geometry: the same order, and a reminder that more energy a pass costs power as the square.

Exercise R2. IBA also builds Rhodotrons at 215 MHz. If every dimension of the illustrative cavity is halved to resonate at 215 MHz, what are \(l\) and \(Q_0\)?

Answer

\(l = c/(2\times215\ \text{MHz}) = 0.697\) m. In the formula for \(Q_0\), \(\ln(b/a)\) and \(l(1/a + 1/b)\) are ratios of lengths, unchanged when every length is halved. Only \(R_s\) changes: it grows as \(\sqrt f\), by \(\sqrt2\). So \(Q_0 = 48{,}500/\sqrt2 = 34{,}300\). Roughly, \(Q_0\) is the cavity’s size over the skin depth: halving every length halves the size, while the skin depth, which goes as \(1/\sqrt f\), shrinks only by \(\sqrt2\). This is the handbook’s point that dissipation rises with frequency.

Exercise R3. Show that a beam crossing the cavity above or below the median plane would be deflected along \(z\), and say which way.

Answer

Off the median plane \(\cos(\pi z/l) \neq 0\), so \(B_\theta\) is not zero, and the magnetic force \(-e\,\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\) on an electron moving radially has a component along \(z\). It changes sign with \(z - l/2\) and with time, so the beam is pushed along \(z\) in a way that depends on its height and phase. In the median plane this force vanishes, which is one reason the beam is kept there.

9. Beams in industry: dose, throughput, implantation

Dose and throughput. Absorbed dose \(D\) is energy per mass, in gray (1 Gy = 1 J/kg; here \(D\) is dose, not dispersion). A beam of power \(P\) (W) of which a fraction \(\eta_u\) is absorbed usefully in the product (the subscript because \(\eta\) is the phase-slip factor) treats a mass per second

\[ \dot m = \frac{\eta_u P}{D}. \]

The reason is a balance of energy: each second the beam brings \(P\) joules, \(\eta_uP\) of them land in the product, and each kilogram must receive \(D\) joules. Sterilising doses are tens of kilogray (food irradiation uses up to 30 kGy to sterilise spices). With \(D = 25\) kGy and an assumed \(\eta_u = 0.4\), a 100 kW electron beam treats \(0.4 \times 10^5/(2.5\times10^4) = 1.6\) kg a second, 5.8 tonnes an hour. Electrons of 10 MeV stop in roughly 5 cm of water (they lose about 2 MeV per centimetre), so thick pallets are treated with X-rays made from the electron beam on a heavy target, at a large loss in efficiency but with the penetration of photons.

Machines for power. These need high beam power and efficiency at modest energy. At a few MeV the parallel-fed cascade, Cleland’s Dynamitron of 1956, gives a high-power direct-current beam (chapter 5). Above, the Rhodotron, patented by Annick N’Guyen and Jacques Pottier of the French CEA (priority 1987), recirculates the beam through one coaxial cavity. The electric field of its fundamental mode is radial in the median plane, so a beam injected in that plane crosses the cavity along a diameter, gaining energy on the way in and on the way out (the field reverses while the electron passes the inner conductor), leaves through a hole, is turned through about 180° by a magnet outside, and crosses again along another diameter. The patent’s example is 10 MeV in six passes at 100 MHz with 278 kW lost in the walls: 1.67 MeV a pass. The electrons are relativistic after the first pass, so every loop takes the same time, and the magnets are placed so that the beam re-enters in phase with the RF field, a whole number of periods later; only the first magnet differs, because the beam is not yet relativistic. IBA’s machines gain 0.83 to 1 MeV a crossing, 3.3 MeV in the 40 MeV model, with up to 12 crossings (Chao and Tigner, Handbook, section 1.6.20). IBA builds Rhodotrons of 5, 7, 10 and 40 MeV and 20 to 560 kW.

Ion implantation. A semiconductor is doped, given a small, controlled amount of an impurity (the dopant) that sets how it conducts, by firing ions of boron, phosphorus or arsenic into it, then annealing the crystal, heating it so that its atoms can move, to repair the damage and put the dopant atoms on lattice sites, the regular places of the crystal’s atoms. William Shockley of Bell Telephone Laboratories applied for the patent on 28 October 1954; it was granted on 2 April 1957. The implanted dose, in ions per unit area, is set by counting charge: a dose \(\Phi\) (m\(^{-2}\)) of singly charged ions over an area \(A_w\) takes a charge \(\Phi A_w e\). A dose of \(10^{15}\) cm\(^{-2}\) on a 300 mm wafer, of area \(\pi \times (15\ \text{cm})^2 = 707\) cm\(^2\), is \(7.1\times10^{17}\) ions, and with \(e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\) C each, 0.113 C: 113 s at 1 mA, 11 s at 10 mA. (Here “dose” means ions per unit area, not energy per mass.) Wikipedia gives typical energies of 10 to 500 keV, medium-current machines from 10 µA to about 2 mA, and high-current ones up to about 30 mA. A crystal has open directions, corridors between rows of atoms, along which ions channel, steered gently by the rows, and go much deeper, so implantation is done a few degrees off a crystal axis.

Cargo inspection. Linacs of 5 to 10 MeV, the medical machine’s cousin, make bremsstrahlung for imaging trucks and containers. Wikipedia gives a penetration of 30 to 40 cm of steel for such X-ray systems, against 15 to 18 cm for cobalt-60 or caesium-137 sources.

Electron-beam welding. Karl-Heinz Steigerwald developed it from 1949, and the first practical machine ran in 1958. It is the cathode ray tube of chapter 2 scaled up: 60 to 150 kV typically, focused to a power density (beam power per unit area of the spot) of \(10^4\) to \(10^6\) W/mm\(^2\), in vacuum, making welds up to 300 mm deep.

Pre-requisite (continued): Spin and magnetic moments

Pre-requisite. Chapter 7 taught spin, the magnetic moment that goes with it, and polarisation; chapter 17 the Larmor precession of a moment about a field; and chapter 18 the \(g\)-factor and its anomaly. Section 10’s surface muons need three more things: helicity, the spin measured along the direction of motion; why the neutrino’s fixed helicity makes every muon from a pion at rest polarised; and how a muon reports where its spin points through the direction of its decay positron, so that the magnetic field inside a sample can be read from a precession frequency. Taught from Penrose, The Road to Reality, §22.7 (helicity) and §25.3 (the neutrino’s handedness); Chao and Tigner’s Handbook, section 1.6.16 (parity violation as the source of polarised muons, and the decay positron as the signal of their spin); and Tong, Quantum Mechanics, section 9.3 (spin in a magnetic field).

Helicity. The helicity of a particle is the component of its spin along its own direction of motion. For a particle of spin \(\tfrac12\) it is \(+\tfrac12\) (in units of \(\hbar\)) when the spin points along the motion, which is called right-handed, and \(-\tfrac12\) when it points against, left-handed. For a massive particle, helicity depends on the observer: overtake a muon and its momentum reverses in your frame while its spin does not, so its helicity flips. A massless particle moves at \(c\) and cannot be overtaken, so its helicity is the same for everyone; Penrose (§22.7) describes such a particle as spinning only about its direction of motion.

The neutrino is left-handed. The weak interaction, which drives beta decay and the decays of pions and muons, is not the same in a mirror (Penrose, §25.3 and §25.4). The neutrinos it makes are left-handed, helicity \(-\tfrac12\), and the antineutrinos right-handed, \(+\tfrac12\). Neutrinos have a tiny mass, but at these energies the rule holds to far better accuracy than any muon experiment needs.

Why every muon from a pion at rest is polarised. A positive pion has spin 0. At rest it decays into a muon and a muon neutrino, which fly apart back to back with 29.79 MeV/\(c\) each (the two-body decay above). Take \(z\) along the muon’s direction. Before the decay the angular momentum is zero. After it, the orbital angular momentum \(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p}\) is at right angles to the line of flight, so it has no \(z\) component, and the spins’ \(z\) components must add to zero. The neutrino moves along \(-z\) and is left-handed, so its spin points along \(+z\): its \(z\) component is \(+\tfrac12\). The muon’s must be \(-\tfrac12\). Its spin points opposite to its own motion: helicity \(-\tfrac12\), for every muon, a polarisation of 100 per cent.

At work. This is why surface muons are wanted. The pions that stop near the surface of the production target decay at rest, every muon they give has momentum 29.79 MeV/\(c\) and spin pointing back along its path, and a beam line that accepts that momentum delivers a fully polarised beam. Muons from pions decaying in flight are only partly polarised in the laboratory, because the boost mixes the directions. Chao and Tigner’s handbook (section 1.6.16) makes the same point for the muon \(g - 2\) experiments: parity violation, the weak interaction’s difference from its mirror image described above, supplies the polarised muons.

Reading the spin. A positive muon lives 2.197 µs on average at rest and decays into a positron, a neutrino and an antineutrino: \(\mu^+ \to e^+\,\nu_e\,\bar\nu_\mu\). Three bodies share 105.7 MeV, so the positron’s energy is spread from zero up to 52.8 MeV. The weak interaction again favours a direction: the positron tends to leave along the muon’s spin. Averaged over all positron energies, the number emitted at angle \(\theta\) to the spin goes as

\[ \frac{dN}{d\cos\theta} \propto 1 + A_\mu\cos\theta, \qquad A_\mu = \tfrac13 \]

(quoted, from the theory of the decay; the most energetic positrons have \(A_\mu = 1\)). A counter looking along the spin sees \((1 + \tfrac13)/(1 - \tfrac13) = 2\) times as many positrons as one looking the other way. So the muon reports the direction of its spin at the moment it decays, and the handbook notes that this is how the \(g - 2\) rings follow the spin too.

Precession in a sample. A muon stopped in a sample, with its spin at right angles to the local magnetic field \(B\), precesses about the field at the Larmor frequency of chapter 17:

\[ f_L = \frac{g_\mu e}{4\pi m_\mu}\,B = 135.54\ \text{MHz/T} \times B, \]

with \(g_\mu = 2.0023\) the muon’s \(g\)-factor and \(m_\mu = 1.884\times10^{-28}\) kg its mass. A counter on one side then records positrons at the rate

\[ N(t) = N_0\,e^{-t/\tau_\mu}\left[1 + A_\mu\cos(2\pi f_L t + \phi)\right], \]

an exponential decay with a wiggle on it, \(\tau_\mu = 2.197\) µs the mean life and \(\phi\) set by the starting direction of the spin. The wiggle is there because the counter sees the spin turn towards it and away again: \(\theta\), the angle between the spin and the counter’s direction, grows as \(2\pi f_Lt\), and the rate goes as \(1 + A_\mu\cos\theta\). The frequency of the wiggle gives the field at the muon’s site inside the sample: this is muon spin rotation, µSR (figure 28.7). The muon’s frequency per tesla is 3.18 times the proton’s 42.58 MHz/T, the ratio of the proton’s mass to the muon’s (8.88) times the ratio of their \(g\)-factors (\(2.0023/5.586\)).

Figure 28.7. What a µSR counter records, \(N(t)/N_0 = e^{-t/\tau_\mu}[1 + \tfrac13\cos(2\pi f_Lt)]\), for muons in 0.01 T (\(f_L = 1.355\) MHz, one turn every 0.738 µs). The dashed line is the plain decay of the muons; the dotted lines, \(1 \pm \tfrac13\) times it, are the envelope between which the rate swings as the spin turns towards the counter and away. Fitting the wiggle’s frequency gives the field.

At work. In 0.01 T a muon turns at 1.355 MHz, about three turns in a mean life (\(1.355\ \text{MHz} \times 2.197\) µs \(= 2.98\)). After 10 µs only \(e^{-10/2.197} = 1.05\) per cent of the muons remain, so a measurement uses a window of about 10 µs, and fields from tenths of a millitesla, a fraction of a turn, up to several tesla can be read.

Exercise S1. A negative pion at rest decays into a negative muon and a muon antineutrino. Which way does the muon’s spin point?

Answer

The antineutrino is right-handed: moving along \(-z\), its spin points along \(-z\), a \(z\) component of \(-\tfrac12\). The muon’s spin must then have \(+\tfrac12\) along \(z\), its own direction of motion: helicity \(+\tfrac12\), spin along the path.

Exercise S2. A µSR counter shows a precession at 8.5 MHz. What is the field at the muon’s site?

Answer

\(B = 8.5/135.54 = 0.0627\) T, 62.7 mT.

Exercise S3. At a pulsed source, all the muons of one pulse arrive within a time \(t_p\), so their spins start precessing at different moments. Take the rule that the spread of precession angle, \(2\pi f_L t_p\), must stay below about one radian. What is the largest field a source with \(t_p = 100\) ns can measure, and what does a continuous source such as PSI’s gain?

Answer

\(f_L < 1/(2\pi\times100\ \text{ns}) = 1.6\) MHz, so \(B < 1.6/135.54 = 0.012\) T. A continuous source sends muons into the sample one at a time, each timed from its own arrival by a counter it passes on the way in, so there is no such limit and fast precession in high fields can be followed. Its cost is rate: a second muon must rarely arrive before the first has decayed.

10. Neutrons and muons by the megawatt

Spallation. A proton of about 1 GeV striking a heavy nucleus knocks out nucleons (protons and neutrons) in a cascade, each struck nucleon striking others in turn, and leaves the nucleus hot, that is, with much internal energy shared among its nucleons; it cools by boiling off neutrons, as a hot drop loses molecules. “Spallation” means chipping pieces off. A 1 GeV proton on lead typically gives about 20, and the number grows roughly in proportion to the proton energy between 0.4 and 10 GeV (Chao and Tigner, Handbook, section 1.6.17; Padamsee, Knobloch and Hays, section 20.7, also gives 20): about 50 MeV of beam a neutron. Wikipedia gives a lower cost, about 30 MeV per neutron, six times lower than fission; it does not say on what that rests, and the handbook’s figure is used here. The neutrons are born at MeV energies and slowed in a moderator, a hydrogen-rich material such as water or liquid hydrogen beside the target.

The numbers. The beam power is \(P = W I/e\): \(I/e\) protons a second, each bringing \(W\) joules. With \(W\) in electronvolts, \(W/e\) is the voltage the protons were accelerated through, and \(P = (W/e) I\), volts times amperes, as for any power supply; 800 MeV at 200 µA is \(8\times10^8\ \text{V} \times 2\times10^{-4}\ \text{A} = 0.16\) MW.

1.4 MW at 1 GeV is \(1.4\times10^6/(10^9 \times 1.602\times10^{-19}) = 8.7\times10^{15}\) protons a second, and at 60 pulses a second \(1.5\times10^{14}\) a pulse; at 20 neutrons a proton that is \(1.7\times10^{17}\) neutrons a second. The beam is lost into the target, but nowhere else: the beam-loss limit of about 1 W/m (chapter 22; Chao and Tigner, Handbook, section 1.6.17) means losing less than a millionth of the beam per metre (1 W out of 1.4 MW is \(7\times10^{-7}\)). The rule keeps the machine’s parts from becoming too radioactive for people to service by hand.

Why pulses. A neutron’s de Broglie wavelength \(\lambda = h/(m_n v)\) (F7, section 8) is set by its speed. A neutron of 25.3 meV, the thermal energy \(k_BT\) at 20 °C (F6, section 12), moves at \(v = \sqrt{2W/m_n} = 2{,}200\) m/s and has \(\lambda = 0.180\) nm, about the spacing of atoms, which is why neutrons can map where atoms sit, as X-rays do (F7, section 5); it takes 4.5 ms to fly 10 m. If the source flashes for a short time, the time of arrival at a detector gives each neutron’s speed and wavelength, and a whole spectrum is used at once. A neutron that crosses a distance \(L\) in time \(t\) has \(v = L/t\) and so \(\lambda = ht/(m_nL)\): the arrival time measures the wavelength directly. The pulses must be far enough apart that slow neutrons of one pulse arrive before fast ones of the next. With \(t\) equal to the frame, the time between pulses, this gives the longest usable wavelength: at 20 m from the moderator, frames of 71.4 ms (14 Hz, ESS) reach \(\lambda = (6.626\times10^{-34} \times 0.0714)/(1.675\times10^{-27} \times 20) = 1.41\) nm before overlapping, frames of 16.7 ms (60 Hz) only 0.33 nm (figure 28.8).

Figure 28.8. Time of flight at a pulsed source. Each pulse (red marks on the time axis, 71.4 ms apart at 14 Hz) sends out neutrons of every wavelength at once; each wavelength is a straight line whose slope is its speed, \(h/(m_n\lambda)\). At a detector 20 m away the slowest neutrons that can be used, 1.41 nm, arrive just as the next pulse starts and its fastest neutrons follow at once; any slower and the frames would overlap. Muons. Protons of several hundred MeV on a graphite target make pions. A positive pion that stops near the target surface decays at rest into a muon and a neutrino (99.99 per cent of decays). Two-body kinematics fixes the muon momentum:

\[ p_\mu c = \frac{m_\pi^2 c^4 - m_\mu^2 c^4}{2 m_\pi c^2} = 29.79\ \text{MeV}, \qquad W_\mu = 4.12\ \text{MeV}, \]

with \(m_\pi c^2 = 139.570\) MeV and \(m_\mu c^2 = 105.658\) MeV. These “surface muons” are fully polarised, because the neutrino is left-handed, and slow enough to stop in a thin sample, where the direction of their decay positrons tells the magnetic field they felt. PSI’s Swiss Muon Source, fed by the 590 MeV beam, has six or seven beam lines (counts differ by what is included) and the most intense continuous muon beams in the world; ISIS and J-PARC have muon targets too.

11. Counting atoms: accelerator mass spectrometry

The problem. Living carbon holds about 1.2 atoms of carbon-14 per \(10^{12}\) of carbon-12. Carbon-14 and nitrogen-14 differ in mass by 1.2 parts in \(10^5\) (14.0032420 u against 14.0030740 u), so an ordinary mass spectrometer (one that bends ions in a magnet and sorts them by mass) would need a resolving power, the mass divided by the smallest mass difference it can separate, of \(14.0032/0.000168 = 83{,}000\) to separate them, at an abundance ratio of \(10^{12}\) or more, and molecules of mass 14 (\(^{13}\)CH, \(^{12}\)CH\(_2\)) are far more abundant still.

The tandem’s answer (chapter 6). Nitrogen forms no stable negative ion, so a source of negative ions makes no nitrogen-14 at all. The stripper at the terminal takes several electrons from each ion; molecules stripped to 3+ fly apart. Magnets and electric analysers after the tandem select carbon-14 in a given charge state; at 2.5 MV, \(^{14}\)C\(^{3+}\) leaves with 10 MeV (2.5 MeV gained as a negative ion, charge \(-e\), drawn up to the positive terminal, then \(3 \times 2.5 = 7.5\) MeV as a 3+ ion going down) and \(B\rho = p/(3e) = 0.568\) T m; \(^{13}\)C\(^{3+}\) has the same energy and, being lighter, less momentum, \(B\rho = 0.568\sqrt{13/14} = 0.547\) T m, so the magnet sends it along another path. A gas ionisation detector counts the carbon-14 ions one by one and measures their energy loss, which also identifies any isobar left. Carbon-12 and carbon-13 are too abundant to count, and are measured as currents in Faraday cups.

Counting statistics. A 50 µA beam of \(^{12}\)C\(^-\) is \(3.1\times10^{14}\) ions a second. From a modern sample it brings 370 atoms of carbon-14 a second, before losses. A count of \(N\) has a relative standard deviation \(1/\sqrt N\) (a Poisson count has variance equal to its mean, F6, section 6), so 1 per cent needs \(1/0.01^2 = 10{,}000\) counts, about 27 s; 0.3 per cent needs 111,000, about 5 minutes. Wikipedia’s figure of 1 per cent “in minutes” allows for transmission losses and the time spent on standards.

The age. By convention the radiocarbon age uses Libby’s half-life, 5,568 years, a mean life of \(5568/\ln 2 = 8{,}033\) years (the mean life \(\tau = 1/\lambda_d\)), and the fraction \(F\) of modern carbon measured after correcting for isotope fractionation (plants and chemistry take up the lighter isotopes slightly more readily, so a sample’s carbon-14 is corrected using its measured carbon-13, which is affected half as much). The carbon-14 left after a time \(t\) is \(F = e^{-t/\tau}\); taking the logarithm, \(\ln F = -t/\tau\), so

\[ t_{age} = -8033\ \text{years} \times \ln F, \]

counted in years before 1950. Ages are then converted to calendar dates by a calibration curve, made from tree rings and other samples of known age, because the atmosphere’s carbon-14 has varied. (Libby’s half-life is about 3 per cent short of the true 5,730 years; the convention keeps old and new ages comparable, and the calibration absorbs the difference.)

A real measurement. On 21 April 1988 a strip was cut from the Shroud of Turin, and samples of a few tens of milligrams (the protocol asked for 40 mg each) went to Arizona, Oxford and the ETH Zurich, with three control samples of known age. The laboratories’ radiocarbon ages were 646 ± 31, 750 ± 30 and 676 ± 24 years; the unweighted mean (the plain average of the three) was 691 ± 31 years, a fraction \(F = e^{-691/8033} = 0.918\) of modern carbon. Calibrated, the result published in Nature in 1989 was 1260 to 1390 at 95 per cent confidence (a range that, by the statistics of the measurement, holds the true date 95 times in 100; for a Gaussian it is about two standard deviations either side, F6, section 5). Later statisticians (Riani and others, 2013; Casabianca and others, 2019) have argued that the shroud results are less consistent with one another than the paper said.

Ion beam analysis. The same tandems, run with protons or helium of a few MeV, measure what a sample is made of without consuming it: Rutherford backscattering (RBS) by the energy of ions scattered back from nuclei, which tells the mass of the nucleus struck, PIXE (particle-induced X-ray emission) by the characteristic X-rays of the atoms the beam ionises, PIGE (particle-induced gamma emission) and nuclear reaction analysis by gamma rays and reaction products (chapter 6). At AGLAE, under the Louvre, the beam leaves the vacuum through a thin window and travels less than 2 mm of air to the object, so an object is analysed where it lies, without taking a sample.

12. Driving a subcritical reactor

A reactor whose effective multiplication factor \(k_{eff}\) is below 1 cannot sustain a chain reaction. Fed with \(S_0\) neutrons a second from an external source, each generation multiplying by \(k_{eff}\), it produces \(S_0(1 + k_{eff} + k_{eff}^2 + \dots) = S_0/(1 - k_{eff})\) neutrons a second in all, source neutrons included. At \(k_{eff} = 0.95\) the multiplication is 20. A spallation source can be that external source, and if the beam stops the power stops. MYRRHA plans a 600 MeV, 4 mA proton linac, a 2.4 MW beam, driving a lead-bismuth-cooled subcritical core of 50 to 100 MW thermal, with \(k_{eff}\) of about 0.95. Its hardest requirement is reliability: no more than 10 beam interruptions longer than 3 seconds in a three-month operating period, since each sudden loss of beam is a sudden change of power, and so of temperature, in the core. The first 100 MeV part, MINERVA, broke ground on 25 June 2024.

13. Machines on a bench

The fusor. Philo Farnsworth, the television pioneer, filed in 1962 for a device in which positive ions fall inward towards a spherical grid at high negative voltage, pass through its openings and collide at the centre (US patent 3,258,402, granted 28 June 1966); Robert Hirsch published his design in the Journal of Applied Physics in 1967, under the name inertial-electrostatic confinement. In deuterium the two branches of the D-D reaction release

\[ \mathrm{d} + \mathrm{d} \to {}^{3}\mathrm{He} + \mathrm{n} + 3.27\ \text{MeV}, \qquad \mathrm{d} + \mathrm{d} \to \mathrm{t} + \mathrm{p} + 4.03\ \text{MeV}, \]

from the CODATA nuclear masses (d is a deuteron, the nucleus of deuterium, t a triton, of tritium), the neutron carrying 2.45 MeV of the first, by the two-body split of the pre-requisite before section 5. At least about 25 kV is needed, because the deuterons must come close enough, against their Coulomb repulsion, to tunnel through the barrier (F7, section 12). The record for a fusor-like device is \(3\times10^{11}\) neutrons a second; with an equal number of the other branch, that is \(3\times10^{11} \times (3.27 + 4.03)\) MeV a second \(\times\ 1.602\times10^{-13}\) J/MeV \(= 0.35\) W of fusion power, far below the electrical power put in, because most ions strike the grid. A growing number of amateurs have built fusors and produced fusion with them; the parts are a vacuum system, deuterium, a supply of tens of kilovolts (chapter 4, chapter 5) and a neutron detector to prove it.

The small cyclotron. The cyclotron’s equations (chapter 10) make a teaching machine easy to size: protons at a radius of 0.15 m in 1 T reach \(W = (eBR)^2/2m_p = 1.08\) MeV (the momentum at radius \(R\) is \(p = eBR\), and \(W = p^2/2m_p\) at this low energy), at a revolution frequency \(f = eB/(2\pi m_p) = 15.2\) MHz. Machines of this size are built for teaching; Rutgers University’s physics department, for one, keeps a cyclotron.

How it was researched and built

The medical linac

The Hammersmith machine of 1953 was stationary; the isocentric mounting, in which the beam turns about a point in the patient, came the same year at Newcastle. Podgorsak counts five generations from the 1960s onward: low-energy straight-through machines (4 to 8 MV) with fixed flattening filter, isocentric mounting and one ionisation chamber; then bent beams, electron modes (the target withdrawn, so that the electrons themselves treat shallow tumours) and dual chambers; dual photon energies with an achromatic bending magnet (one that brings electrons of slightly different energies to the same spot on the target, chapter 20’s “without colour”) and independent jaws; computer control, the electronic portal imager (a flat detector behind the patient that images the treatment beam itself, to check the aim) and the multileaf collimator (MLC); and finally intensity modulation with the MLC, in which the leaves move during treatment so that each field gives more dose in some places than others.

Today’s machines accelerate electrons to 4 to 25 MeV, nearly all at 2856 MHz, with a magnetron or a klystron (chapter 13). Above 6 MeV the guide (the accelerating waveguide, the linac structure itself) is too long to point at the patient, so it lies along the gantry axis and a 90°, 270° (achromatic) or 112.5° magnet bends the beam onto a retractable target. In the head the photons pass a flattening filter (a metal cone, thickest on the axis, that evens out the forward-peaked beam of section 2 into a flat field), two sealed transmission ionisation chambers that count monitor units (1 MU is set to give 1 cGy, a centigray, at \(z_{max}\) in water for a 10 × 10 cm\(^2\) field at 100 cm), the jaws (heavy movable blocks that set a rectangular field), and an MLC of up to 120 tungsten leaves (60 pairs) covering 40 × 40 cm\(^2\), each with its own motor (Podgorsak, sections 5.5.10 to 5.5.15). Shaping the field with leaves was first done by Takahashi in 1965. A linac gives up to 10 Gy a minute against the betatron’s 1. A course of treatment is typically 60 to 80 Gy for a solid tumour, in daily fractions of 1.8 to 2 Gy (spreading the dose over weeks lets healthy tissue repair between sessions better than the tumour does).

Proton and ion therapy

The physics laboratories at Berkeley, Uppsala and Harvard showed the method, with beams spread by scattering and shaped for each patient. At PSI, Gantry 1 used spot scanning for the first time, in 1996, and treated patients until the end of 2018: a pencil beam is steered by magnets from spot to spot across a layer, and the energy is changed to move from layer to layer, so that no patient-specific hardware is needed. IBA, founded in 1986 by Yves Jongen as a spin-off of the Cyclotron Research Center of UCLouvain, installed its first proton therapy system at Massachusetts General Hospital in 2001; by the mid-2010s it had sold about 40 therapy cyclotrons and more than 300 for isotopes (Kleeven and Zaremba) (chapter 12). Superconducting synchrocyclotrons (cyclotrons whose frequency falls during acceleration, chapter 12, with superconducting coils for a strong, compact field) have since shrunk the accelerator to ride on the gantry. In 2022, by PTCOG’s statistics, 107 proton therapy facilities were operating and 31 were under construction, over 280,000 patients had been treated with protons, and 14 ion centres, mostly carbon and mostly in Japan, had treated 42,000 (Chao and Tigner, Handbook, section 1.6.14). Wikipedia gives 41 proton centres in the United States at the start of 2023.

Carbon came from Japan and Germany. NIRS in Chiba began treating with carbon ions at HIMAC in 1994. GSI in Darmstadt developed and demonstrated carbon-ion therapy independently, treating about 440 patients between 1997 and 2008, then handed the technology to the Heidelberg Ion-Beam Therapy Centre, which began treating patients in early November 2009 and cost 119 million euros to build. Its 670 tonne gantry brings carbon to the patient from any angle.

Isotope cyclotrons

Medical cyclotrons accelerate H\(^-\) ions and extract by stripping them on a foil at the radius of the wanted energy, which turns them into protons that curve the other way out of the field (chapter 21). Rotating a foil into the beam picks the exit port and so the target. IBA’s current 18 MeV machine measures 1.9 × 1.9 × 1.8 m, has two ion sources for redundancy and up to eight exit ports. Implanters, the second-largest class of accelerator, descend from Shockley’s patent; the Dynamitron and the Rhodotron serve the radiation-processing industry.

Spallation sources

ISIS was built in the hall of Nimrod, the Rutherford Laboratory’s 7 GeV synchrotron, reusing its equipment; Nimrod gave about 0.1 µA at 7 GeV (1.5 to \(2\times10^{12}\) protons a pulse every 2.6 s), ISIS 200 µA at 0.8 GeV. The first neutrons came on 16 December 1984 and Margaret Thatcher opened it in October 1985. Its ring is filled by charge-exchange injection of H\(^-\) (chapter 21). The SNS at Oak Ridge put its first beam on target in April 2006 and was formally completed that June for 1.4 billion US dollars, with a superconducting linac and a liquid mercury target. J-PARC delivers 1 MW at 3 GeV to its Materials and Life Science Facility. ESS at Lund, estimated in 2013 at about 1.84 billion euros (since rebaselined to about 3 billion), plans its user programme from late 2027, with a rotating tungsten target cooled by helium gas; the funded first phase runs at 870 MeV and 2 MW, and the 2 GeV, 5 MW design awaits further funding.

AMS and the laboratories

After Muller’s proposal of 1977, Nelson, Korteling and Stott at McMaster and Bennett, Beukens, Clover, Gove and colleagues at Rochester measured carbon-14 at natural levels with tandems, published in the same issue of Science in November 1977. Sample sizes fell from the 10 g or more of decay counting to milligrams; Wikipedia gives 0.5 mg of carbon as enough. Other long-lived isotopes followed: beryllium-10, aluminium-26, chlorine-36, iodine-129.

AGLAE (Accélérateur Grand Louvre d’analyse élémentaire) is a 2 MV Pelletron tandem made by NEC, launched in 1983, installed at the Louvre in 1987 and inaugurated in 1989, in the basement of the Centre de recherche et de restauration des musées de France. A rebuilt New AGLAE was inaugurated on 23 November 2017. It uses PIXE, PIGE, RBS, ERDA and ion-beam-induced luminescence, and its external beam is its signature.

How such machines are designed today

An applied accelerator is designed from its use backward: a therapy machine from depth (energy), field size, dose rate and the precision of dose and position, with the beam followed through every material by Monte Carlo transport codes (programs that follow millions of simulated particles one by one, choosing each collision at random with the probabilities the cross-sections give, and add up where the energy goes); an isotope machine from the cross-section and the heat its target can take; an industrial machine from throughput, \(\eta_u P/D\), and wall-plug efficiency (beam power over the electrical power drawn from the mains); a spallation source from the pulse structure its instruments need. Reliability and running cost rank with energy, and the machine is bought from industry rather than built by its users. Chapter 29 follows the practice.

At the bench

The morning on a hospital linac

A radiotherapy department starts with checks before the first patient. The radiographer or physicist warms the machine up and sets a constancy device, a plastic phantom (a block that stands in for the patient’s tissue) with ionisation chambers, on the couch at the standard distance. A fixed number of monitor units is delivered in each photon and electron mode, and the reading is compared with the baseline. The action levels of AAPM Task Group 40, as Podgorsak tabulates them (table 12.3), are:

  • daily: X-ray and electron output constancy, 3 per cent; lasers (the lines of light on the walls that cross at the isocentre, used to set up the patient), 2 mm; distance indicator, 2 mm; door interlock and audio-visual monitor, working;
  • monthly: output constancy, 2 per cent; backup monitor chamber, 2 per cent; X-ray flatness constancy, 2 per cent; electron flatness, 3 per cent; symmetry, 3 per cent; light and radiation field coincidence, 2 mm;
  • annually: output calibration, 2 per cent; isocentres of collimator, gantry and table rotation within a 2 mm diameter; monitor chamber linearity, 1 per cent.

A reading inside tolerance needs nothing. Between tolerance and action level, treatment may continue to the next day’s check; past the action level the machine is stopped until fixed and verified. Flatness is \(F_{fl} = 100\,(D_{max} - D_{min})/(D_{max} + D_{min})\) (subscripted to keep it apart from the fraction \(F\) of modern carbon) over the central 80 per cent of the profile at 10 cm depth, and a standard specification is below 3 per cent for the largest field (Podgorsak, section 6.9.1).

What goes wrong, and how it shows. An output that drifts the same way day after day points to the monitor chamber or the energy. A change in symmetry means the electron beam is striking the target off-centre or at an angle, and the steering coils need adjustment. A change in flatness means the energy has moved, since the flattening filter suits one spectrum only. A laser off by millimetres sets the patient up off by millimetres. If the primary monitor chamber fails, the secondary chamber or the timer ends the irradiation.

A PET cyclotron’s morning run

The cyclotron runs before dawn, so that the product is ready when the scanners open. The operator checks the vacuum and the cooling water, starts the ion source and the RF, and loads the target with a few millilitres of oxygen-18 water. Beam is brought up on the target in steps while the operator watches four things: the current on the target; the current on the collimator in front of it, which should be a small fraction of the total; the target pressure; and the vacuum.

The target is a small sealed cavity behind thin metal windows. An 18 MeV beam of 100 µA puts \(18\ \text{MV} \times 100\ \mu\text{A} = 1.8\) kW into a few millilitres of water, which boils; the cavity is pressurised and cooled so that the boiling stays steady. A steady pressure means steady boiling; a pressure that keeps rising means the cooling is losing; a sudden fall means a window has broken, and the run is over until the foil is replaced. A rising collimator current means the beam has drifted off-centre or grown. At the end of the run the water is pushed through a line to a shielded hot cell (a lead-walled box in which radioactive material is handled by remote control), where an automated module makes FDG; if the synthesis takes half an hour, that alone costs \(1 - 2^{-30/109.7} = 17\) per cent of the activity. The yield, measured in a dose calibrator (an ionisation chamber shaped as a well, into which the vial is lowered), is compared with the expected \(Y_{sat} I (1 - e^{-\lambda_d t})\); a low yield with a normal current points to the target water or the foil, a low current to the ion source or the stripping foil. Stripping foils wear out and are replaced; parts of the ion source are consumables too.

An AMS measurement

The sample, a bone or a thread, is cleaned chemically, burned to carbon dioxide, and reduced to graphite, which is pressed into a small holder: the target for a caesium sputter source of negative ions (chapter 7). A wheel holds many targets: unknowns, standards of oxalic acid (the NIST batch HOxII, made in 1977), and blanks. Two blanks are used: dead carbon straight into the source, which shows the machine background, and dead carbon carried through all the chemistry, which shows contamination from preparation.

The machine then works through the wheel, measuring each target in turn and returning to each several times. For each target it records the carbon-12 and carbon-13 currents in Faraday cups and the carbon-14 count in the detector, and computes \(^{14}\)C/\(^{12}\)C and \(\delta^{13}\)C (the sample’s \(^{13}\)C/\(^{12}\)C ratio, as its difference in parts per thousand from a reference standard; it measures the fractionation of section 11). The ratio of each unknown to the standards, corrected by the blanks and by \(\delta^{13}\)C to the reference value for wood, gives \(F\) and the age. The operator watches the terminal voltage, the source current, the transmission (the high-energy carbon current over the low-energy one) and the detector’s energy spectrum. A transmission that falls points to a thinning or broken stripper foil; a source current that fades points to a target used up or caesium running low; counts in the detector at the wrong energy point to an isobar or to scattered ions reaching it; a blank that reads high points to contamination in the chemistry or memory of a previous sample in the source.

Where it stops

For each kind of applied machine the limit is not energy but size and cost per patient or per product, set by physics. A therapy beam must reach 30 cm, which fixes the energy, and the energy fixes the rigidity: 2.32 T m for protons, 6.62 T m for carbon, and a carbon gantry of 670 tonnes. Iron saturates near 2 T, so only superconducting magnets (chapter 17) make the gantry smaller; for the accelerator itself, the ideas in chapter 27, plasma and laser acceleration among them, aim at shrinking it. A spallation source is limited by its target, where megawatts are deposited in a few litres, and by the rule of about 1 W/m of beam loss (chapter 22) that lets people work on the machine by hand. An isotope cyclotron is limited by the heat its target can shed, and an AMS laboratory by its counting statistics: a 50,000-year sample keeps \(2^{-50000/5730} = 0.24\) per cent of its carbon-14.

Exercises

1. What is the shortest wavelength of the X-rays from a tube at 120 kV? Where does the rest of the 24 kW of a 120 kV, 200 mA tube go?

Answer

\(\lambda_{min} = hc/eV = (6.626\times10^{-34} \times 2.998\times10^{8})/(1.602\times10^{-19} \times 1.2\times10^{5}) = 1.03\times10^{-11}\) m, 0.0103 nm. With a radiative yield of order 1 per cent, about 240 W leave as X-rays; nearly all of the 24 kW heats the focal spot on the anode.

2. A 6 MV beam has \(z_{max} = 1.5\) cm. Treat it as 2 MeV photons (\(\mu_a/\rho = 0.0494\) cm\(^2\)/g in water) from a source at 100 cm and estimate the dose at 10 cm relative to the maximum. Why is the measured 67 per cent higher?

Answer

Attenuation over 8.5 cm: \(e^{-0.0494 \times 8.5} = 0.657\). Inverse square: \((101.5/110)^2 = 0.851\). Product 0.56. The estimate leaves out photons scattered in the patient, which add dose at depth and grow with field size, and it treats a spectrum as one energy.

3. Use the Bragg-Kleeman rule \(\mathcal{R} = 0.00226\,W^{1.765}\) (cm, MeV) to find the proton energy that reaches 25 cm of water, and compare with the integrated Bethe formula.

Answer

\(W = (25/0.00226)^{1/1.765} = 195.5\) MeV. Integrating the Bethe formula gives 195.7 MeV. The rule is a fit and good to about 2 per cent in range between 50 and 250 MeV.

4. Near 230 MeV the stopping power of water is 4.11 MeV cm\(^2\)/g. How large an energy error moves the Bragg peak by 1 mm? What range straggling does Bohr’s formula give, and what does it mean for the sharpness of the distal edge?

Answer

\(\Delta W = S\,\Delta\mathcal{R} = 4.11 \times 0.1 = 0.41\) MeV. Integrating Bohr’s variance over the slowing down gives \(\sigma_{\mathcal{R}} = 0.34\) cm, 1.0 per cent of 32.9 cm. The distal fall-off of a single peak is spread over several millimetres, about the same as a 1.4 MeV error in energy; the beam’s own energy spread adds to it.

5. Find the magnetic rigidity of 230 MeV protons and of carbon-12 at 430 MeV per nucleon (\(M c^2 = 11{,}175\) MeV, charge 6e), and the bending radius in a 1.8 T dipole for each.

Answer

Protons: \(pc = \sqrt{(230 + 938.27)^2 - 938.27^2} = 696.1\) MeV, \(B\rho = 696.1\times10^6/(2.998\times10^8) = 2.32\) T m, \(\rho = 1.29\) m. Carbon: \(W = 5160\) MeV, \(pc = \sqrt{16335^2 - 11175^2} = 11{,}914\) MeV, \(B\rho = 11914\times10^6/(6 \times 2.998\times10^8) = 6.62\) T m, \(\rho = 3.68\) m. The ratio, 2.85, is why carbon gantries are so large.

6. A cyclotron run ends at 07:00 with 35 Ci of fluorine-18. How much is left at a hospital that receives it at 10:00? How many 300 MBq doses could that be at most?

Answer

\(e^{-\ln 2 \times 180/109.7} = 0.321\): 11.2 Ci, 415 GBq, at most 1,384 doses of 300 MBq if nothing were lost in synthesis or dispensing and all were injected at 10:00. Each further hour costs another 32 per cent.

7. Show that a molybdenum-99 generator’s technetium-99m activity is greatest at \(t_{max} = \ln(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)/(\lambda_2 - \lambda_1)\) after elution, and evaluate it.

Answer

\(\mathcal{A}_2 \propto e^{-\lambda_1 t} - e^{-\lambda_2 t}\). Setting the derivative to zero: \(\lambda_1 e^{-\lambda_1 t} = \lambda_2 e^{-\lambda_2 t}\), so \(e^{(\lambda_2 - \lambda_1)t} = \lambda_2/\lambda_1\). With \(\lambda_1 = \ln 2/65.93\) h and \(\lambda_2 = \ln 2/6.007\) h, \(t_{max} = 22.8\) h.

8. An AMS laboratory has 50 µA of \(^{12}\)C\(^-\) from a sample about 10,000 years old (half-life 5,730 years for the decay; take the modern ratio as \(1.2\times10^{-12}\) and perfect transmission). How long does a measurement to 0.5 per cent take?

Answer

The fraction left is \(2^{-10000/5730} = 0.298\). The beam is \(3.12\times10^{14}\) ions a second, so \(3.12\times10^{14} \times 1.2\times10^{-12} \times 0.298 = 112\) carbon-14 atoms a second. 0.5 per cent needs \(1/0.005^2 = 40{,}000\) counts: 358 s, about 6 minutes, before losses and time on standards.

9. The SNS was designed for 1.4 MW of 1 GeV protons at 60 Hz. How many protons are in each pulse? At about 20 neutrons a proton, how many neutrons a second does it make? What frame length does 60 Hz give, and up to what wavelength do neutrons arrive within one frame at 20 m?

Answer

\(1.4\times10^6/(10^9 \times 1.602\times10^{-19}) = 8.74\times10^{15}\) protons a second, \(1.46\times10^{14}\) a pulse. \(8.74\times10^{15} \times 20 = 1.7\times10^{17}\) neutrons a second. A frame is 16.7 ms; a neutron covering 20 m in 16.7 ms has \(v = 1200\) m/s and \(\lambda = h/(m_n v) = 0.33\) nm.
